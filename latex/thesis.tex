\section*{TL;DR}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{TL;DR}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We queried Google ClusterData2019 for cells \(a\)--\(h\), using
        \codepath{machine_events} for cell capacity,
        \codepath{instance_usage} for measured CPU use, and
        \codepath{collection_events} for priority and completed no-SLO
        durations.
  \item The extraction produced one aggregate CPU curve, one
        service/residual-versus-batch curve, machine metadata, and a compact
        deadline-statistics file per cell. The primary study uses cells
        \(a\)--\(f\); cells \(c\)--\(h\) provide an overlapping sensitivity to
        the choice of workload traces.
  \item The V6 factory removes one terminal row, averages twelve five-minute
        samples into each hour, repeats the resulting 744-hour profile across
        the 5,808-hour market calendar, and supplies immediate arrivals and
        earliest-deadline-first batch arrivals to the controller.
  \item We joined hourly ClusterData2019 CPU utilization to Google
        PowerData2019 measured power utilization and fitted one affine
        CPU-to-power model per cell. The runtime combines those coefficients
        with executed work and a 100 MW proxy rating to calculate site power
        used by the ramp objective.
  \item We acquired 5,808 common UTC hours of physical grid conditions and
        day-ahead prices for six US market cases. Operator price feeds,
        operator or EIA physical data, training-only scales, and causal
        reconstructed forecasts are combined into the panel that supplies
        native net load, forecast trajectories, and the wholesale-cost context
        used by V6.
  \item The problem treats the fleet as six fungible proxy sites with hourly
        capacity, immediate service, deadline-constrained batch work, and
        unrestricted inter-site movement. Grid conditions and prices are
        exogenous. A feasible causal scheduler may choose service destinations,
        batch execution volume, and batch destinations, but may not drop work
        or violate capacity or deadlines.
  \item At each hourly decision, V6 compares the grid's native ramp with the
        ramp after adding scheduled data-center power over 1 h and 3 h windows.
        That incremental difference becomes the PPO learning signal; day-ahead
        cost remains a separate guardrail, while causal panel observations
        inform where service and batch work should be executed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Google ClusterData2019 Workload Trace}
\label{sec:clusterdata2019}

\subsection{Role in the study}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-role}

The workload input is derived from Google's ClusterData2019 trace, not from
ClusterData2011 and not from a synthetic arrival generator. ClusterData2019
contains production measurements from eight Borg cells during May 2019 and is
hosted in BigQuery because the complete dataset is approximately 2.4 TiB
compressed \cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}. The repository does not vendor the
BigQuery tables. It retains derived time series, compact job-duration summaries,
machine metadata, and cryptographic hashes.

The final primary experiment uses cells \(a\)--\(f\). The purpose of the
secondary \(c\)--\(h\) mapping is to test whether the accepted controller's
result depends on the exact six Borg workload profiles selected for the
primary map. After the primary sealed test passed, the policy, markets,
calendar, forecasts, decoder, and constraints remained fixed while the
workload profiles were replaced by cells \(c\)--\(h\) and the fleet was scaled
to 1 GW total. No policy selection or tuning used this analysis.

The secondary map overlaps the primary population in cells \(c\)--\(f\).
It can therefore show sensitivity to an alternative workload mix, but it
cannot establish independent out-of-sample generalization. The declared
mapping is in \codepath{env/protocols/independent_us_v3.yaml}; the executable
primary mapping is in \codepath{energy_model_v3/ramp_factory.py}; and the
post-selection sensitivity is implemented in
\codepath{ramp_rl/ensemble_robustness.py}.

\subsection{Measured utilization extraction}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-utilization}

For each machine \(m\) in cell \(c\), the extraction takes the maximum reported
CPU \emph{capacity} over its machine-event records. A machine can have several
add, update, or snapshot-derived records; this maximum collapses those records
to one capacity value. It does not select a maximum CPU-usage observation. The
cell denominator is
\begin{equation}
  C_c = \sum_{m \in c}
        \max_{e \in \mathcal{E}_m}
        \left(\texttt{capacity.cpus}_{m,e}\right).
  \label{eq:clusterdata-cell-capacity}
\end{equation}

The phrase ``top-level instances'' means instances that are not running inside
an allocation set. In Google's trace schema,
\codepath{alloc_collection_id} equal to zero means
\codepath{NO_ALLOC_COLLECTION}; the query also accepts a null value
\cite{googleTraceFormatV3}. This filter follows Google's cell-level analysis
pattern and excludes nested task records associated with an alloc set. It does
not choose the highest utilization during a five-minute interval.

The query requires each retained usage record to span at least 300 seconds.
Records are assigned to five-minute buckets by
\(\lfloor\texttt{start\_time}/(10^6 \cdot 300)\rfloor\), and
\codepath{average_usage.cpus} is summed across every qualifying record in the
bucket. The normalized aggregate CPU demand is
\begin{equation}
  u_{c,k}
  =
  \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
  \left(
    \frac{
      \sum_{r \in (c,k)} \texttt{average\_usage.cpus}_r
    }{C_c}
  \right).
  \label{eq:clusterdata-aggregate}
\end{equation}
Clipping prevents reported overcommit from producing a normalized value above
one. The committed aggregate files are
\codepath{data/cells/cell_X.csv}, with columns
\codepath{timestep} and \codepath{cpu_demand_norm}.

\subsection{Service and deferrable-batch decomposition}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-tier-split}

Priority is recorded in \codepath{collection_events}, not in
\codepath{instance_usage}. The extraction therefore forms one priority record
per submitted collection (\codepath{type} equal to zero) and left-joins it to measured
usage by \codepath{collection_id}. Using the priority bands documented in
Google's ClusterData2019 analysis, matched priority at or below 115 is treated
as no-SLO, deferrable work: free tier at or below 99 and best-effort batch from
100 through 115 \cite{googleClusterAnalysisColab}.

Let \(p_r\) be the matched collection priority for usage record \(r\). Before
the simulator-side runtime admission envelope defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-admission} is applied, measured batch demand is
\begin{equation}
  b_{c,k}
  =
  \min\left[
    u_{c,k},
    \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
    \left(
      \frac{
        \sum_{r \in(c,k)}
        \mathbf{1}\{p_r \leq 115\}
        \texttt{average\_usage.cpus}_r
      }{C_c}
    \right)
  \right],
  \label{eq:clusterdata-batch}
\end{equation}
and the service/residual demand is
\begin{equation}
  s_{c,k} = u_{c,k} - b_{c,k}.
  \label{eq:clusterdata-service}
\end{equation}
Consequently, every committed row satisfies
\begin{equation}
  s_{c,k} + b_{c,k} = u_{c,k}
  \label{eq:clusterdata-conservation}
\end{equation}
to floating-point precision.

The left join is important for interpretation. Usage without matched priority
metadata is assigned zero classified batch use and therefore remains in the
service/residual curve. The extraction does not contain a canonical audit of
the unmatched-priority rate. The thesis must therefore call this component
``service/residual,'' not claim that every such observation has a measured
production priority.

The direct runtime inputs are
\codepath{data/cells/cell_X_tiers.csv}. Each file contains
\codepath{timestep}, \codepath{cpu_demand_norm}, \codepath{batch_share},
\codepath{service_demand_norm}, and \codepath{batch_demand_norm}. Cells
\(a\)--\(d\) are generated by \codepath{extract_tier_curves.ipynb}; cells
\(e\)--\(h\) are generated by \codepath{extract_cells_eh.ipynb}. These are
measured usage decompositions.

\subsection{From five-minute rows to the final hourly workload}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-hourly-transform}

Each committed tier file contains 8,929 indexed rows. The final factory detects
the extra terminal row, removes it, and retains 8,928 complete five-minute
intervals. It then averages consecutive groups of 12:
\begin{equation}
  x^{\mathrm{hour}}_{c,j}
  =
  \frac{1}{12}
  \sum_{q=0}^{11}
  x^{\mathrm{5min}}_{c,12j+q},
  \qquad x \in \{s,b\}.
  \label{eq:clusterdata-hourly-average}
\end{equation}
This produces a 744-hour profile. The factory repeats that profile from
2025-09-01 00:00 UTC until it fills the 5,808-hour market calendar ending
2026-05-01 00:00 UTC:
\begin{equation}
  x^{\mathrm{calendar}}_{c,t}
  =
  x^{\mathrm{hour}}_{c,\,t \bmod 744}.
  \label{eq:clusterdata-tiling}
\end{equation}
Eight copies are touched; the eighth is partial. This transformation preserves
the measured within-month shape, but it does not preserve an empirical
relationship between May 2019 workload and 2025--2026 electricity conditions.
The alignment is a counterfactual modeling convention, not a contemporaneous
operational replay.

\subsection{Runtime batch admission and conservation}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-admission}

A \emph{runtime admission envelope} is a simulator-side bound on how much new
work may enter an hourly decision. It is not a measured property of
ClusterData2019. Its purpose is to ensure that the constraint decoder can
produce a feasible schedule under the finite episode, capacity, and deadline
rules.

The final V6 protocol limits immediate service to 75\% of normalized fleet
capacity, leaving 25\% as guaranteed batch capacity. New batch is capped at
10\%, leaving a further 15\% available to drain batch already carried in the
queue. The environment uses this \(25\%-10\%=15\%\) remainder when certifying
future capacity by deadline. For the six-site primary fleet, the 10\% limit is
\(0.6\) work units per hour. Let \(B_t=\sum_c b_{c,t}\). The factory applies
\begin{align}
  \lambda_t
    &= \min\left(1,\frac{0.6}{B_t}\right), \\
  b^{\mathrm{admitted}}_{c,t}
    &= \lambda_t b_{c,t}, \\
  s^{\mathrm{runtime}}_{c,t}
    &= s_{c,t} +
       \left(b_{c,t}-b^{\mathrm{admitted}}_{c,t}\right).
  \label{eq:clusterdata-admission}
\end{align}
If \(B_t=0\), the implementation uses \(\lambda_t=1\). Excess measured batch
is therefore conserved and reclassified as immediate service; it is never
dropped. The service envelope is 75\% of normalized fleet capacity. These
rules are implemented in \codepath{energy_model_v3/ramp_factory.py} and recorded
under \codepath{workload_policy} in
\codepath{output/energy_model_v3/ramp_v6/factory_manifest.json}.

The need for positive carried-batch capacity is a feasibility reason for
having an admission bound, but the specific 10\% value was a frozen protocol
design choice rather than a quantity estimated from ClusterData2019 or an
external source. In the committed primary profile it binds in 407 of 744 hours
and reclassifies 8.31\% of measured batch mass as immediate service. A future
protocol should remove this fixed cap and redesign the feasibility treatment
to admit the complete measured batch series, or preregister a sensitivity over
admission limits. Removing it from the present study would create a new
protocol and would not retroactively change the frozen V4R result.

\subsection{Experimental deadline construction}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-deadlines}

ClusterData2019 supplies observed collection durations, but it does not supply
the scheduling deadlines used in this experiment. The extraction route differs
between the first and second groups of cells, but the selection rule and the
runtime information are the same.

For cells \(a\)--\(d\), the comprehensive notebook creates full job extracts
from \codepath{collection_events} and \codepath{instance_events}.
\codepath{scripts/refit_freebeb_local.py} selects priority at or below 115,
retains positive-duration collections whose terminal type is FINISH
(\codepath{type} equal to six), and materializes rich
\codepath{data/jobs/batch_distributions_X.json} files. Those files also contain
inter-arrival, resource-request, and task-count statistics that V6 does not
read.

For cells \(e\)--\(h\), \codepath{extract_cells_eh.ipynb} applies the same
priority, FINISH, and positive-duration conditions directly in BigQuery. It
first writes mean, median, and completed-count summaries to
\codepath{data/jobs/durations_eh.json}; the corresponding compact
\codepath{batch_distributions_e.json} through
\codepath{batch_distributions_h.json} carry those values into the runtime
schema. Thus the files differ in extra metadata, but every cell provides the
same final field consumed by V6: the mean completed no-SLO duration.

The final hourly controller reads only \codepath{duration.mean} and defines
\begin{equation}
  H_c
  =
  \max\left[
    1,
    \left\lceil
      \frac{2\bar{d}_c}{3600}
    \right\rceil
  \right],
  \label{eq:clusterdata-deadline}
\end{equation}
where \(\bar{d}_c\) is mean completed no-SLO duration in seconds. Every hourly
batch arrival from cell \(c\) receives horizon \(H_c\), producing the values in
Table~\ref{tab:clusterdata-cell-summary}. These are experimental control
deadlines. They are not Borg SLOs, customer commitments, or observed production
queue policies.

\subsection{Reproduction boundary and limitations}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-limitations}

Re-extraction requires a Google Cloud project with BigQuery billing enabled.
The reproducible sequence is to run
\codepath{extract_clusterdata2019_full.ipynb} for the \(a\)--\(d\) aggregate,
machine, and full-job artifacts; run
\codepath{scripts/refit_freebeb_local.py} to materialize the
\(a\)--\(d\) duration JSON; run \codepath{extract_tier_curves.ipynb} for the
authoritative \(a\)--\(d\) measured tier curves; run
\codepath{extract_cells_eh.ipynb} for \(e\)--\(h\); and then rebuild the final
factory with \codepath{scripts/build_energy_v3_ramp_factory.py} after the
restricted electricity panel has been staged.

A fresh clone contains the committed curves, compact duration files, and
manifests, but not the full BigQuery tables or the large
\codepath{data/jobs/jobs_X.csv} extracts. Exact re-fitting of the \(a\)--\(d\)
duration JSON therefore requires rerunning the BigQuery extraction. The
repository also has no dedicated command that converts
\codepath{durations_eh.json} into the minimal \(e\)--\(h\)
\codepath{batch_distributions_X.json}; those committed JSON files are the
hash-bound runtime boundary.

Finally, the trace represents one company's workload during one month in 2019.
Repeating 744 hours suppresses long-term growth and seasonality. The model omits
application latency, data locality, residency constraints, wide-area bandwidth,
migration energy, replication, failures, and customer-specific SLOs. The
cell-to-market assignment is an experimental proxy, not evidence that the Borg
cells physically operated in those electricity markets.

\subsection{Hourly alignment and fitting}
\label{subsec:powerdata-fitting}

For each cell and hour, the power query averages
\codepath{measured_power_util} over all retained power-domain rows:
\begin{equation}
  y_{c,h}
  =
  \operatorname{mean}
  \left(
    \texttt{measured\_power\_util}_{c,h}
  \right),
  \label{eq:powerdata-target}
\end{equation}
after excluding rows where \codepath{bad_measurement_data} is true. The query
does not capacity-weight the contributing power domains; it is an arithmetic
mean of their normalized measurements.

Hourly CPU utilization is constructed from the same ClusterData2019 filter
described in Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-utilization}. Twelve five-minute
measurements are summed and divided by twelve times cell capacity:
\begin{equation}
  u_{c,h}
  =
  \frac{
    \sum_{r \in(c,h)} \texttt{average\_usage.cpus}_r
  }{12C_c}.
  \label{eq:powerdata-hourly-cpu}
\end{equation}
Power and CPU are joined by cell and integer hour index rather than by exact
timestamps. This follows the source-data guidance: power timestamps are aligned
to five-minute marks, whereas cluster event timestamps need not be
\cite{googlePowerAnalysisColab}.

For each cell, ordinary least squares fits
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{p}_{c}(u)
  =
  \alpha_c + \beta_c u,
  \label{eq:powerdata-affine-fit}
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha_c\) is the modeled power fraction at zero CPU and \(\beta_c\) is
the change in power fraction between zero and full normalized CPU utilization.
The stored \codepath{peak_power} value is
\(\alpha_c+\beta_c\), the fitted value at \(u=1\); it is not a separately
measured nameplate rating.

\subsection{Conversion to proxy megawatts}
\label{subsec:powerdata-megawatts}

PowerData2019 reports normalized power utilization, not the absolute MW rating
of each Google cell. The study therefore introduces a separate proxy rating
\(R_c\). The primary scenario sets \(R_c=100\) MW and normalized compute
capacity \(K_c=1\) for every site. At runtime,
\begin{align}
  u^{\mathrm{runtime}}_{c,t}
    &= \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
       \left(\frac{w_{c,t}}{K_c}\right), \\
  P_{c,t}
    &= R_c
       \left(
         \alpha_c + \beta_c u^{\mathrm{runtime}}_{c,t}
       \right),
  \label{eq:powerdata-runtime-power}
\end{align}
where \(w_{c,t}\) is the service plus batch work executed at the site during
hour \(t\). The 100 MW rating is an experimental scaling assumption; it is not
estimated from PowerData2019. The affine coefficients determine the fraction
of that proxy rating consumed.

\subsection{How the final environment uses the model}
\label{subsec:powerdata-runtime-use}

The model enters the final system in two related ways:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \codepath{energy_model_v3/cli.py} reads each aggregate
        \codepath{data/cells/cell_X.csv}, removes the terminal row, averages to
        744 hours, tiles the profile over the 5,808-hour market calendar, and
        applies Equation~\eqref{eq:powerdata-runtime-power}. It writes one
        locally staged
        \codepath{data/energy_model_v3/modeled_dc_power/MARKET.csv} file per
        market.
  \item \codepath{energy_model_v3/ramp_factory.py} loads those files as the
        three-hour warm-power history required to score the first ramp windows.
        It also places each cell's \(\alpha_c\) and \(\beta_c\) in the site
        configuration. During an episode,
        \codepath{env/ramp_v6/models.py} recomputes power from the work actually
        routed and scheduled by the controller.
\end{enumerate}

Thus the tiled measured profile initializes the counterfactual, but policy
power is not a fixed replay of that profile. Routing service among sites and
moving batch work among hours changes \(w_{c,t}\), which changes modeled MW and
therefore the adjusted grid ramps.

\subsection{Model limitations}
\label{subsec:powerdata-limitations}

The affine model intentionally omits cooling control, time-varying PUE, UPS and
distribution losses, storage, generators, and network equipment. Its
\(\alpha_c\) and \(\beta_c\) values describe normalized cell-level association,
not causal server physics. The power target is an unweighted average across
available power domains, and the fit uses one month from one operator. The
100 MW rating and equal compute capacity are proxy assumptions. Consequently,
the model supports controlled comparisons among schedules but does not claim
to reproduce the absolute consumption of a named Google facility.

\subsection{Canonical fields and time semantics}
\label{subsec:energy-canonical}

Every native product is converted to explicit
\codepath{interval_start_utc} and \codepath{interval_end_utc}. Source-specific
interval-beginning or interval-ending semantics, time zone, and daylight-saving
rules are applied before products are joined. For example, MISO is interpreted
using its fixed-EST report convention, while NYISO and ISO-NE repeated civil
hours are resolved using their report-specific rules.

The physical table requires:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \codepath{gross_demand_mw}, \codepath{wind_mw},
        \codepath{solar_mw}, and \codepath{net_load_mw};
  \item the method used to produce net load;
  \item the training-only market scale; and
  \item source-quality flags.
\end{itemize}

The price table retains total day-ahead LMP and, where supplied, real-time LMP
and energy, congestion, and loss components. The final controller uses total
day-ahead LMP. Real-time price is retained only as separate sensitivity
evidence and is prohibited as a policy input. Negative day-ahead prices are
preserved.

Subhourly products may be averaged to an hour only when every native interval
is present on the exact native grid. Duplicate timestamps, missing intervals,
or an incomplete hour raise an error. Forward filling is forbidden.

\subsection{Net-load construction}
\label{subsec:energy-net-load}

For the EIA-backed markets and ERCOT, hourly net load is derived once:
\begin{equation}
  N_{m,t}
  =
  D_{m,t} - W_{m,t} - Q_{m,t},
  \label{eq:energy-net-load}
\end{equation}
where \(D\) is gross demand, \(W\) is wind generation, and \(Q\) is solar
generation in MW.

NYISO is different. Zone J PAL is already the native load quantity used by the
study and reflects behind-the-meter solar treatment. Therefore
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{NYISO},t}
  =
  D^{\mathrm{PAL}}_{\mathrm{Zone\ J},t},
  \label{eq:energy-nyiso-pal}
\end{equation}
and NYCA wind and solar are retained as context rather than subtracted again.

EIA bulk series occasionally report negative renewable adjustments. Before
Equation~\eqref{eq:energy-net-load}, negative wind or solar generation is
clipped to zero and the raw minimum and affected-hour count are retained. In
the committed acquisition this affects 2,780 CAISO solar hours, with a raw
minimum of \(-66\) MW; no other retained wind or solar series required this
normalization. This is a disclosed non-negativity rule, not interpolation.

\subsection{Causal forecast construction}
\label{subsec:energy-forecasts}

No operator forecast is supplied to the controller. The repository reconstructs
causal ridge forecasts for gross demand and net load from the observed panel.
Features are observed lags at 1, 3, 24, and 168 hours; trailing means over 24
and 168 hours; and sine/cosine encodings of target hour and day of week.

During training, a new expanding-window model vintage is fitted daily, and
every training target used by a vintage strictly precedes that vintage. At the
2026-02-01 boundary, the final training-only model is frozen for validation and
sealed test.

The staged forecast files contain one-hour and three-hour records. The final
factory uses the three-hour model to assemble a causal trajectory with
endpoints at \(t+1\), \(t+2\), and \(t+3\). The corresponding forecasts were
issued at \(t-2\), \(t-1\), and \(t\), respectively, so the latest issue time
never follows the controller timestamp. Each bundle records model identity,
vintage, issue time, horizon, and quality. Realized future load or real-time
price is never used as a feature.

The resulting panel supplies the physical baseline, causal forecast state, and
price context used by the optimization problem in
Section~\ref{sec:optimization-objective}. These quantities are data inputs;
they do not become a learning objective until the controller executes an
hourly action.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:energy-limitations}

The panel covers one eight-month period and cannot support prior-year,
future-year, or long-run seasonal claims. Four physical tuples are
balancing-authority-wide EIA fallbacks; NYISO combines zonal load with
systemwide renewable context; and ERCOT combines a load-zone price with
system-level physical data. These geography mismatches are disclosed but do
not represent local feeder conditions.

The model is price-taking: data-center actions do not change LMP, dispatch,
unit commitment, reserves, congestion, or renewable output. Day-ahead LMP is
not a retail tariff. The forecast is a reconstructed causal model rather than
an operator-issued forecast. The panel contains no carbon-intensity input, and
carbon is not optimized. PJM/Northern Virginia is absent. Extending the study
requires a new complete all-six calendar, new forecast vintages and training
statistics, and a new sealed protocol.

\section{Problem Statement and System Boundary}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

\subsection{Purpose of the system model}
\label{subsec:problem-purpose}

Sections~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019}--\ref{sec:energy-data} define the measured
and derived inputs. This section defines the problem those inputs create
without yet choosing an optimization algorithm. It identifies:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item what is fixed and observed;
  \item what an operator is allowed to change;
  \item which outcomes are physically or operationally infeasible; and
  \item which real-world effects are outside the modeled boundary.
\end{enumerate}

The mathematical objective, reward, and PPO implementation are introduced only
after this feasible system boundary, in
Section~\ref{sec:optimization-objective}.

\subsection{Proxy-fleet interpretation}
\label{subsec:problem-proxy-fleet}

The six sites are controlled archetypes, not identified Google facilities and
not claims about the undisclosed geography of the Borg cells. Each primary
site has:
\begin{itemize}
  \item normalized compute capacity \(K_m=1\);
  \item proxy rated power \(R_m=100\) MW;
  \item one ClusterData2019 workload profile;
  \item one PowerData2019 affine power model; and
  \item one attached electricity-market case.
\end{itemize}

The primary fleet is therefore 600 MW. Assigning work to site \(m\) determines
the power added to market \(m\) through
Equation~\eqref{eq:powerdata-runtime-power}. The workload shape and power
response are measured or fitted; the 100 MW magnitude and the cell-to-market
pairing are experimental assumptions.

All compute work is represented as divisible aggregate work. Individual jobs,
applications, users, machine types, and task placement inside a cell are not
modeled. Memory appears only in a diagnostic PowerData fit and is not a runtime
resource constraint.

\subsection{Controllable decisions}
\label{subsec:problem-decisions}

At each hour, a feasible scheduler may control three quantities:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Service destination.} It chooses how the fleet-wide total of
        immediate service is divided among the six sites.
  \item \textbf{Batch execution volume.} It chooses how much queued batch work
        to execute now rather than retain for a later hour.
  \item \textbf{Batch destination.} It chooses where the selected batch work is
        executed.
\end{enumerate}

Let \(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}\) and
\(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t}\) denote service and batch work executed at destination
site \(m\). Let \(z_t=\sum_m x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t}\) be total batch execution.
The origin of service is not retained after arrivals are pooled. Batch origin
is retained in the deadline ledger, and an exact transport matrix
\(\Pi_{i,j,t}\) accounts for movement from origin \(i\) to execution site \(j\).

These are operational decisions, not yet PPO actions. The 13-dimensional
preference interface described later is one attempted parameterization of this
three-part decision.

\subsection{Immediate-service and batch semantics}
\label{subsec:problem-workload-semantics}

Immediate service cannot be delayed:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}
  =
  \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} s_{m,t}.
  \label{eq:problem-service-conservation}
\end{equation}

Batch work enters a global earliest-deadline-first (EDF) queue while retaining
its origin and absolute deadline. If \(Q_t\) is queued mass before new
execution, aggregate queue accounting follows
\begin{equation}
  Q_{t+1}
  =
  Q_t
  +
  \sum_m q_{m,t}
  -
  z_t.
  \label{eq:problem-batch-ledger}
\end{equation}

The queue must conserve mass exactly, work due by a deadline must be executed
no later than that deadline, and all admitted batch work must be complete at
the episode terminal. The implementation is the
\codepath{EDFQueue} ledger in \codepath{env/ramp_v6/models.py}.

The arrival envelope in Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-admission} is part of
the current problem instance: service is limited to 75\% of fleet capacity,
new batch to 10\%, and excess measured batch is conserved by reclassifying it
as immediate service. As noted earlier, the specific 10\% bound is not
externally calibrated and should be removed or varied in a future protocol.

\subsection{Capacity and transport feasibility}
\label{subsec:problem-feasibility}

Executed work cannot exceed destination capacity:
\begin{equation}
  0
  \leq
  x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}
  +
  x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t}
  \leq
  K_m
  \qquad
  \forall m,t.
  \label{eq:problem-site-capacity}
\end{equation}

For batch work drained from origin \(i\), transport accounting requires
\begin{align}
  \sum_j \Pi_{i,j,t}
    &= o_{i,t}, \\
  \sum_i \Pi_{i,j,t}
    &= x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t},
  \label{eq:problem-transport}
\end{align}
where \(o_{i,t}\) is the EDF-selected batch amount from origin \(i\). This
matrix enforces conservation only. It is not a network-flow model.

Routing is otherwise unrestricted. The model imposes no latency, wide-area
bandwidth, data-residency, transfer-energy, replication, or movement-cost
constraint. Consequently, spatial flexibility is an optimistic upper bound on
what a real operator could use.

\subsection{Episode and terminal boundary}
\label{subsec:problem-episode}

Each factory episode represents one UTC day with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item three real preceding hours for ramp history;
  \item 24 active arrival and decision hours; and
  \item a three-hour terminal tail with no new arrivals.
\end{itemize}

The tail allows late-day batch work to finish and allows 1 h and 3 h windows
ending after the final arrival hour to be scored. Circular wrap to the start of
the day is forbidden. The batch queue starts empty at reset and must be empty
at the true terminal. These rules are generated in
\codepath{energy_model_v3/ramp_factory.py} and enforced by
\codepath{env/ramp_v6/environment.py}.

\subsection{Causal information boundary}
\label{subsec:problem-causality}

A decision at hour \(t\) may depend only on information available by \(t\):
\begin{itemize}
  \item current and three trailing hours of observed gross and net load;
  \item native 1 h and 3 h ramps that have already closed;
  \item forecast endpoints whose issue time is no later than \(t\);
  \item current service and batch arrivals, queued work, and deadlines;
  \item previous modeled site power and fixed site parameters;
  \item current day-ahead LMP; and
  \item hour, day-of-week, episode-progress, and quality metadata.
\end{itemize}

Realized future load, realized future real-time price, and forecasts issued
after \(t\) are prohibited. Formally, a scheduling rule \(\mu_t\) must be a
function only of the causal information set \(\mathcal{I}_t\):
\begin{equation}
  \left(
    x^{\mathrm{svc}}_t,
    z_t,
    x^{\mathrm{bat}}_t
  \right)
  =
  \mu_t(\mathcal{I}_t).
  \label{eq:problem-causal-policy}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Grid and market boundary}
\label{subsec:problem-grid-boundary}

The six physical net-load trajectories and day-ahead prices are exogenous. The
modeled fleet is price-taking and does not alter dispatch, LMP, renewable
generation, unit commitment, reserves, congestion, or the source forecasts.
Fleet power is added to native net load for counterfactual evaluation, but the
source grid trajectory itself is unchanged.

The 5\% \(P/S\) gate described in
Section~\ref{subsec:energy-scales} limits the primary price-taking
approximation. Even so, market-level net load does not represent the local
substation, feeder, nodal constraint, or utility tariff faced by a real
facility.

\subsection{Three comparison systems}
\label{subsec:problem-baselines}

The thesis distinguishes three systems:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Native grid.} \(N_{m,t}\) without any modeled data-center power.
        This is a physical counterfactual, not an operating policy.
  \item \textbf{Operational status quo.} Native grid plus fleet power from an
        evaluation-only baseline that routes service and batch in origin
        proportions and requests immediate batch drainage through the same
        feasibility decoder. It maintains an independent shadow queue.
  \item \textbf{Candidate policy.} Native grid plus fleet power produced by the
        learned routing and timing preferences through that same decoder.
\end{enumerate}

Status quo and candidate use identical market, workload, forecast, capacity,
and deadline windows. The baseline entry points are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item evaluation controller:
        \codepath{status_quo_policy} in
        \codepath{env/ramp_v6/evaluation.py}; and
  \item independent environment shadow policy:
        \codepath{_status_quo_action} in
        \codepath{env/ramp_v6/environment.py}.
\end{itemize}

The native comparison isolates whether the modeled fleet increases or decreases
the grid's pre-existing ramp. The status-quo comparison asks the more
operational question: whether the candidate schedule improves on a simple
grid-unaware way of running the same fleet.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{subsec:problem-formal-statement}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Problem statement.}
Given six hourly service and batch arrival streams, batch deadlines, six
capacity and power models, causal physical-grid and forecast information, and
day-ahead prices, construct a causal hourly schedule for service destination,
batch execution volume, and batch destination such that:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item all immediate service is completed in its arrival hour;
  \item all batch work is conserved and completed by its deadline;
  \item no site exceeds compute capacity and the terminal queue is empty;
  \item the fleet's added power reduces, or at minimum does not amplify, the
        native grid's rapid net-load changes across the six market cases; and
  \item modeled day-ahead cost does not increase excessively relative to the
        same fleet under the status-quo comparator.
\end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

This statement does not prescribe reinforcement learning, a neural policy, the
1 h/3 h weights, or a scalar reward. Those choices constitute the attempted
solution defined next.

\section{Optimization Objective and Controller Inputs}
\label{sec:optimization-objective}

\noindent
Within the feasible system defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}, V6
chooses a specific ramp objective, cost guardrail, policy representation, and
constraint decoder. This section therefore describes the attempted solution,
not an additional data source or a restatement of the problem.

\subsection{What is decided each hour}
\label{subsec:objective-hourly-decision}

Each episode advances in one-hour decisions. At hour \(t\), the policy emits
13 preferences for the six-site fleet:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item six preferences for the destination of immediate service work;
  \item one preference for how much queued batch work to execute; and
  \item six preferences for the destination of that batch work.
\end{enumerate}

The policy does not directly set MW. A constraint-only decoder converts the
preferences into feasible service and batch allocations, enforcing immediate
service completion, site capacity, earliest-deadline-first batch requirements,
and exact work conservation. The PowerData2019 model then converts executed
work at each site into market-level data-center power \(P_{m,t}\).

Only after \(P_{m,t}\) is known does the environment score ramp windows whose
right endpoint is hour \(t\). Every reward therefore uses the action just
executed and already observed past values; no future realized load enters the
score.

\subsection{Meaning of the 1 h and 3 h horizons}
\label{subsec:objective-horizons}

The horizon \(h\) is the distance between the two endpoints of a ramp:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(h=1\) compares hour \(t\) with hour \(t-1\), capturing a fast
        hour-to-hour change; and
  \item \(h=3\) compares hour \(t\) with hour \(t-3\), capturing a broader
        multi-hour rise or fall.
\end{itemize}

Dividing by \(h\) expresses both as an average change per hour, so the two
horizons are comparable. The objective gives 40\% weight to the 1 h horizon
and 60\% to the 3 h horizon. This favors sustained multi-hour smoothing while
still penalizing sharp hourly changes.

\subsection{Native and data-center-adjusted ramps}
\label{subsec:objective-native-adjusted}

Let \(N_{m,t}\) be the physical net load in market \(m\) before adding the
modeled fleet, \(P_{m,t}\) the fleet power assigned to that market, and \(S_m\)
the training-only Q95 gross-demand scale from
Equation~\eqref{eq:energy-market-scale}. The two normalized ramps are
\begin{align}
  b_{m,t,h}
    &= \frac{N_{m,t}-N_{m,t-h}}{S_m h}, \\
  a_{m,t,h}
    &= \frac{(N_{m,t}+P_{m,t})-(N_{m,t-h}+P_{m,t-h})}{S_m h}.
  \label{eq:energy-ramps}
\end{align}

The native ramp \(b_{m,t,h}\) answers: ``How quickly was the grid already
changing without this modeled fleet?'' The adjusted ramp \(a_{m,t,h}\)
answers: ``How quickly would net load change after adding the fleet schedule?''
Normalization by \(S_m\) prevents a large market such as MISO from dominating
a smaller market such as NYISO solely because its MW scale is larger.

The adjusted ramps are used twice. First, they are reported as physical
per-market ramp magnitudes. Second, they are compared with the native ramps to
calculate the controller's incremental effect.

\subsection{Why ramps are squared}
\label{subsec:objective-squared-ramp}

The squared native ramp \(b_{m,t,h}^2\) is a dimensionless stress proxy for the
grid change that would have occurred without the modeled fleet. Squaring serves
two purposes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item upward and downward ramps receive the same sign-independent burden; and
  \item a large ramp is penalized more strongly than several small ramps.
\end{enumerate}

The study does not minimize \(b^2\) itself because the controller did not cause
the underlying grid ramp. Instead, \(b^2\) is the counterfactual baseline
subtracted from the adjusted squared ramp.

\subsection{Per-window incremental impact and PPO reward}
\label{subsec:objective-incremental-impact}

For one market, one endpoint, and one horizon, the incremental impact is
\begin{equation}
  I_{m,t,h}
  =
  a_{m,t,h}^2-b_{m,t,h}^2.
  \label{eq:energy-incremental-impact}
\end{equation}

Its interpretation is direct:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(I_{m,t,h}<0\): fleet power reduced the squared native ramp;
  \item \(I_{m,t,h}=0\): fleet power was ramp-neutral; and
  \item \(I_{m,t,h}>0\): fleet power worsened the squared native ramp.
\end{itemize}

The primary impact at hour \(t\) is the equal-market mean of the weighted
horizons:
\begin{equation}
  \overline{I}_t
  =
  \frac{1}{6}
  \sum_{m=1}^{6}
  \left(
    0.40 I_{m,t,1}
    +
    0.60 I_{m,t,3}
  \right).
  \label{eq:objective-primary-impact}
\end{equation}

This is the quantity accumulated for the primary reported native-relative
impact. A smaller training-Q90 tail term applies the same adjusted-minus-native
comparison only to unusually large absolute ramps. If
\(\overline{T}_t\) denotes the equal-market, horizon-weighted incremental tail
burden, the ramp objective and reward are
\begin{align}
  J_t^{\mathrm{ramp}}
    &= \overline{I}_t + 0.15\overline{T}_t, \\
  r_t^{\mathrm{ramp}}
    &= -J_t^{\mathrm{ramp}}.
  \label{eq:objective-ramp-reward}
\end{align}

PPO receives this scalar after each executed hourly action. It therefore learns
which routing and timing preferences tend to make future rewards larger---that
is, which actions tend to make incremental ramp impact more negative. The
0.15 tail term shapes learning, while the headline reported impact remains
\(\overline{I}_t\) without that shaping term.

\subsection{Purpose of day-ahead energy cost}
\label{subsec:objective-energy-cost}

Ramp smoothing could be achieved by moving work into extremely expensive
hours. Day-ahead cost is retained to detect and constrain that behavior:
\begin{align}
  C_{m,t}
    &= L^{\mathrm{DA}}_{m,t}P_{m,t}(1\ \mathrm{hour}), \\
  C_t
    &= \sum_{m=1}^{6} C_{m,t},
  \label{eq:energy-cost}
\end{align}
where \(L^{\mathrm{DA}}\) is in USD/MWh and \(P\) is in MW.

The environment compares policy cost with a status-quo schedule on the same
hours. A configurable Lagrangian penalty can reduce reward when the policy
exceeds its cost budget, and the accepted V4R evaluation required the aggregate
day-ahead cost ratio to remain at or below 1.02. In the accepted training run,
the Lagrangian multipliers remained zero, so the realized learning signal was
the ramp reward; cost still served as a validation gate and a separately
reported outcome.

This cost is only a wholesale proxy. It is not used as physical grid stress and
does not include retail rates, demand charges, taxes, ratchets, or contractual
terms.

\subsection{How observations affect controller behavior}
\label{subsec:objective-observations}

Observations do not mechanically force an action. They are inputs to the PPO
network from which it learns its 13 preferences. Their intended decision roles
are:

\begin{longtable}{@{}L{0.31\textwidth}L{0.61\textwidth}@{}}
  \caption{Why each energy-related observation is available to the policy.}
  \label{tab:objective-observations}\\
  \toprule
  Observation & Scheduling information it provides \\
  \midrule
  \endfirsthead
  \toprule
  Observation & Scheduling information it provides \\
  \midrule
  \endhead
  Current and trailing gross/net levels
    & Locates each market within its recent demand trajectory and identifies
      whether current conditions are relatively high or low. \\
  Native 1 h and 3 h ramps
    & Indicates the direction and speed at which each grid is already changing. \\
  Causal \(t+1,t+2,t+3\) forecast endpoints
    & Allows the policy to execute deferrable work before a forecast upward ramp,
      defer it toward a forecast valley, or route work to a market with a more
      favorable trajectory. \\
  Forecast age, vintage, and quality
    & Lets the policy distinguish a current, valid trajectory from older or
      lower-quality forecast context. \\
  Previous modeled site power
    & Provides the starting point for the data-center power change that will
      enter Equations~\eqref{eq:energy-ramps}. \\
  Day-ahead LMP
    & Provides economic context so ramp smoothing need not blindly concentrate
      work in costly hours or markets. \\
  Current arrivals, queued batch, deadlines, capacity, and power parameters
    & Describes what work can be moved, how urgently it must execute, and how a
      unit of work translates into site power. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The forecast affects the action choice, not the reward calculation. Reward is
computed later from realized net load and executed fleet power when a ramp
window closes. This separation allows anticipatory control without leaking
future realized values.
