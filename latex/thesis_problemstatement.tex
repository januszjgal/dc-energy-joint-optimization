\section{Introduction and Scope}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large data centers draw enough electrical power that the timing and location
of their computing work matter to the grids that serve them. Some work must
run immediately, while lower-priority work may be delayed. An operator with
multiple sites may also choose where that work runs.

The question asked here is:
\begin{quote}
\emph{How much can a causal scheduler jointly smooth normalized one-hour
regional net-load ramps and reduce regional monthly net-load peaks, using temporal deferral and geographic
routing while satisfying immediate-service, batch-window, and site-capacity
constraints?}
\end{quote}

Google workload traces and EIA grid data are used to model a synthetic fleet
of four data centers. Each hour, the scheduler chooses where service runs,
how much batch work to execute, and where to execute it. Proximal policy
optimization (PPO) will be used to train a policy that minimizes an equally
weighted combination of scaled monthly ramp impact and regional peak impact
while meeting workload deadlines and site-capacity limits.
Section~\ref{sec:literature} places this question in the literature, and the
remaining sections define the data, the synthetic fleet, and the problem.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}

The references are grouped by the lever they study and by the perspective
from which that lever is judged: data centers as loads on the electric grid,
geographic routing among sites, temporal deferral of batch work, learned
schedulers, and the workload and power models on which such studies rest.
The section closes by positioning this thesis against that work and stating
the perspective adopted here.

\subsection{Data centers as grid loads}
\label{subsec:lit-grid-loads}

In \emph{Key Questions on Energy and AI}, the International Energy Agency
\cite{iea2026energyai} reports that data-center electricity consumption grew
by more than 15\% between 2024 and 2025, to almost 500 TWh, and projects it to
roughly double, to about 950 TWh, by 2030. The report describes large-load
connection queues, notes that AI workloads can trigger steep and sudden load
ramps as clusters of GPUs transition synchronously between tasks, and observes
that peak demand often remains meaningfully below nameplate capacity. It
recommends that system operators explore non-firm connections and incentives
for demand response in return for faster grid access, arguing that demand
response and dynamic contracts could reduce the need for additional reserves,
network upgrades, and peaking capacity. These are grid-side concerns: what
matters to the system operator is the shape of the load, its ramps and its
peaks, rather than the data center's bill.

Vasques, Moura, and de Almeida, in \emph{A review on energy efficiency and
demand response with focus on small and medium data centers}
\cite{vasques2019review}, review the same relationship from the
demand-response side. They distinguish two roles for workload flexibility:
load management that lowers the data center's own operating cost, and
demand-response services that support the efficient and reliable operation of
the grid. Flexibility in their account may be a temporary load reduction or a
temporary load increase during a renewable surplus, and it is usually split
into ICT-flexible workload, meaning delay-tolerant jobs that can run at another
time, and non-ICT measures such as cooling set points and uninterruptible
power supplies.

Sakalkar \emph{et al.}, in \emph{Data Center Power Oversubscription with a
Medium Voltage Power Plane and Priority-Aware Capping}
\cite{sakalkar2020oversubscription}, show how Google manages power inside the
facility. A capping service that throttles only low-priority workloads on
every node lets the fleet oversubscribe its power infrastructure by 25\% or
more, with the achievable oversubscription limited by the share of power drawn
by high-priority work that must not be disrupted. Priority is thus already the
lever by which an operator sheds kilowatts, and the same priority structure
defines the deferrable work in Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-tier-split}.
That capping, however, protects electrical limits inside the facility; it does
not address the shape of the regional load.

\subsection{Geographic routing among sites}
\label{subsec:lit-spatial}

Qureshi \emph{et al.}, in \emph{Cutting the Electric Bill for Internet-Scale
Systems} \cite{qureshi2009cutting}, combine hourly wholesale electricity
prices at 29 United States locations with Akamai traffic data and simulate
price-conscious request routing. Existing systems could reduce energy cost by
at least 2\% without increasing bandwidth cost, and a fully energy-elastic
system with relaxed bandwidth constraints by over 30\%. The savings hinge on
energy elasticity, the fraction of a cluster's power that follows its load
rather than being drawn at idle, and they fall when routing reacts late to
price changes. Rao \emph{et al.}, in \emph{Minimizing Electricity Cost:
Optimization of Distributed Internet Data Centers in a Multi-Electricity-Market
Environment} \cite{rao2010minimizing}, formulate the same routing problem as a
mixed-integer program with service-delay guarantees, approximate it by a
linear program, and report hourly cost reductions of 30\% and 18\% on two sets
of real price data.

Liu \emph{et al.}, in \emph{Geographical Load Balancing with Renewables}
\cite{liu2011glb}, replace price with renewable availability. In a
trace-based study, ``follow the renewables'' routing significantly reduces the
renewable capacity needed to reach a given share of clean energy, small-scale
storage helps most in combination with routing, and wind is more valuable than
solar because its output is weakly correlated across sites and available at
night. Zhao, Zhou, and Li, in \emph{CFWS: DRL-Based Framework for Energy Cost
and Carbon Footprint Optimization in Cloud Data Centers} \cite{zhao2025cfws},
bring learned control to the same lever: a deep reinforcement learning agent
migrates virtual machines among geo-distributed data centers under
time-varying prices and location-varying carbon rates, reducing brown energy
by 6\% to 13\% while cutting the number of migrations.

In all four studies the grid enters only through a price or a carbon signal,
and the score is the data center's own cost, renewable use, or carbon. None
measures what the routed load does to the ramps or the peak of the receiving
region. The routing model in Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}, with
conservation of work, per-site capacity, and no transfer cost, descends from
these formulations; the objective does not.

\subsection{Temporal deferral of batch work}
\label{subsec:lit-temporal}

Liu \emph{et al.}, in \emph{Renewable and Cooling Aware Workload Management
for Sustainable Data Centers} \cite{liu2012renewable}, split the workload into
interactive services that run continuously and delay-tolerant batch jobs whose
deadlines range from several hours to multiple days. Predictions of renewable
supply and IT demand feed a plan that shifts batch work toward hours of
renewable availability and efficient cooling, reducing recurring power cost
and non-renewable energy by as much as 60\% against existing techniques.
Grange, Da Costa, and Stolf, in \emph{Green IT scheduling for data center
powered with renewable energy} \cite{grange2018green}, schedule batch jobs
with due dates in a single data center with on-site solar generation, keeping
the scheduler agnostic of the electrical infrastructure by having a separate
system supply the objective function. Brown energy falls by up to 49\%, and
the authors find that the freedom allowed by the service-level agreement, the
due-date slack, largely determines the achievable saving. Xu, Toosi, and
Buyya, in \emph{A Self-Adaptive Approach for Managing Applications and
Harnessing Renewable Energy for Sustainable Cloud Computing}
\cite{xu2021selfadaptive}, pair a brownout mechanism for interactive workloads
with a deferral algorithm for batch workloads and reduce brown energy by 21\%
in a prototype driven by real traces. Haghshenas \emph{et al.}, in
\emph{Infrastructure Aware Heterogeneous-Workloads Scheduling for Data Center
Energy Cost Minimization} \cite{haghshenas2022infrastructure}, schedule
service and batch jobs against renewable supply, cooling efficiency, and a
tariff that combines time-of-use prices with peak demand charges; batch jobs
must finish within 24 hours of submission, and cost falls by up to 46\%.

Radovanovi\'{c} \emph{et al.}, in \emph{Carbon-Aware Computing for
Datacenters} \cite{radovanovic2021carbonaware}, describe the system in
production across Google's fleet. Day-ahead forecasts of grid carbon intensity
and of cluster demand feed a risk-aware optimization that produces hourly
Virtual Capacity Curves: limits on the resources available to temporally
flexible workloads that preserve total daily capacity, so that all such work
completes within a day. The optimizer minimizes a weighted sum of expected
carbon footprint and daily power peak over all clusters, on the reasoning that
data centers are planned around peak power, so smaller peaks reduce the need
for new capacity. Cluster-level flexible usage proves predictable within a
day-ahead horizon despite job-level uncertainty.

The batch window \(H=24\) used in Section~\ref{subsec:problem-batch} follows
Radovanovi\'{c} \emph{et al.} and Haghshenas \emph{et al.}, and the
service/batch split follows Liu \emph{et al.} and Haghshenas \emph{et al.}
The finding of Grange \emph{et al.} that due-date slack governs the saving is
why \(H\) is reported as an assumption whose value matters. In this line of
work the objective is cost, brown energy, or carbon; the peak appears only as
a demand charge or as a capacity-cost term, and hour-to-hour ramps do not
appear at all.

\subsection{Learned schedulers}
\label{subsec:lit-rl}

Kahil \emph{et al.}, in \emph{Reinforcement learning for data center energy
efficiency optimization: A systematic literature review and research roadmap}
\cite{kahil2025rl}, review 65 studies from 2019 to 2024 that apply
reinforcement learning to cooling and to ICT tasks such as task scheduling,
resource allocation, and virtual-machine consolidation. Almost all are
evaluated in simulation, and the review names the lack of real-world
validation and of standardized metrics as the main gaps. Ran \emph{et al.}, in
\emph{DeepEE: Joint Optimization of Job Scheduling and Cooling Control for
Data Center Energy Efficiency Using Deep Reinforcement Learning}
\cite{ran2019deepee}, train a parameterized-action deep Q-network that jointly
schedules jobs and adjusts airflow on a two-time-scale simulation platform,
saving up to 15\% of energy against siloed control. Naug \emph{et al.}, in
\emph{SustainDC: Benchmarking for Sustainable Data Center Control}
\cite{naug2024sustaindc}, provide Gymnasium environments in which a
load-shifting agent, a cooling agent, and a battery agent act on a simulated
data center driven by public Alibaba and Google utilization traces. The
load-shifting agent observes the time of day, current and forecast grid carbon
intensity, and the amount of flexible work still to be placed; it is penalized
if flexible work is not rescheduled within its horizon; and its reward is the
carbon footprint. The baseline assumes no shifting, which the authors describe
as current practice. Outside data centers, Zhang and Lam, in \emph{Practical
Implementation and Evaluation of Deep Reinforcement Learning Control for a
Radiant Heating System} \cite{zhang2018practical}, train an agent on a
calibrated building simulator and deploy it in an office building for three
months, reducing heating demand by 17\% to 18\% while documenting the data
requirements and training cost that such a deployment entails.

This thesis follows the pattern set by SustainDC and DeepEE: a simulated
environment driven by public traces, a reinforcement-learning agent, and a
no-flexibility baseline. The forecasts in
Section~\ref{subsec:energy-forecasts} play the role that carbon-intensity
forecasts play in SustainDC and in Google's system, as the scheduler's only
view of the hours ahead. The reward differs: where SustainDC's load-shifting
agent minimizes carbon, the agent here is charged for the change its fleet
makes to the region's net-load ramps and monthly peak. As in the studies Kahil
\emph{et al.} review, the results are simulation results; no deployment is
claimed.

\subsection{Workload traces and power models}
\label{subsec:lit-data}

The workload here comes from Google's 2019 cluster trace, documented by Wilkes
in \emph{Google cluster-usage traces v3} \cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019} and
analyzed by Tirmazi \emph{et al.} in \emph{Borg: The Next Generation}
\cite{tirmazi2020borg}. The analysis defines priority tiers, of which the
free and best-effort batch tiers carry no service-level objective, finds that
best-effort batch has grown to about 20\% of cell capacity, and reports
resource consumption so heavy-tailed that the top 1\% of jobs consume over
99\% of resources. It summarizes each cell as each tier's CPU usage as a
fraction of cell capacity over time, the same aggregate view adopted in
Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019}. Da Costa, Grange, and De Courchelle, in
\emph{Modeling and Generating large-scale Google-like Workload}
\cite{dacosta2016modeling}, fit a generative model to the earlier 2011 trace
so that scheduling experiments can be reproduced on synthetic workloads; here
the measured aggregate usage is replayed instead. Lin \emph{et al.}, in
\emph{A systematic review of green-aware management techniques for
sustainable data center} \cite{lin2024systematic}, catalog the public
workload traces, renewable data, and price series used across this literature
and note the growing use of the temporal and spatial flexibility of batch
work.

For power, Dayarathna, Wen, and Fan, in \emph{Data Center Energy Consumption
Modeling: A Survey} \cite{dayarathna2016survey}, organize more than 200
models and observe that most rest on a few CPU or server metrics. They cite
the finding of Fan \emph{et al.} at Google that a linear function of CPU
utilization tracks dynamic power accurately at the power-distribution-unit
level, which is the form fitted in Section~\ref{sec:powerdata2019}. Cooling
is a large share of facility power: Zhang \emph{et al.}, in \emph{A survey on
data center cooling systems: Technology, power consumption modeling and
control strategy optimization} \cite{zhang2021cooling}, put it near 30\%, and
Zhang \emph{et al.}, in \emph{Towards Joint Optimization Over ICT and Cooling
Systems in Data Centre: A Survey} \cite{zhang2016joint}, review the coupling
models that joint ICT-and-cooling control requires. Cooling control is outside
the scope of this thesis. Its power is not omitted, however: the PowerData2019
measurements \cite{googlePowerData2019} fitted in
Section~\ref{sec:powerdata2019} include data-center-floor cooling, so the
affine power model carries cooling's response to load without modeling it
separately. Software-level efficiency measures, such as the virtualization,
consolidation, and placement techniques surveyed by Katal, Dahiya, and
Choudhury in \emph{Energy efficiency in cloud computing data centers: a
survey on software technologies} \cite{katal2023energy} and by Panwar,
Rauthan, and Barthwal in \emph{A systematic review on effective energy
utilization management strategies in cloud data centers}
\cite{panwar2022systematic}, reduce the energy needed per unit of work. They
are complementary to, and separate from, the question asked here, which holds
the work fixed and changes only when and where it runs.

\subsection{Position of this thesis}
\label{subsec:lit-position}

The reviewed work supplies both levers used here, geographic routing from
Section~\ref{subsec:lit-spatial} and temporal deferral from
Section~\ref{subsec:lit-temporal}, and the learned-control pattern of
Section~\ref{subsec:lit-rl}. What it does not supply is a measurement of the
effect of a specific scheduling policy on the ramps and peak of a specific
region's net load. The cost- and carbon-driven studies see the grid through a
price or an intensity signal; the grid-side reports describe flexibility as
valuable without attaching it to a scheduler.

This thesis takes the operator perspective on grid-ramp and peak-kW
reduction. The decision-maker is the operator of a multi-site fleet, who
controls only what such an operator controls: where service work runs, and
when and where batch work runs. Regional demand, wind, and solar are exogenous
history. The score is what those decisions do to the grid, namely the change
in each region's squared one-hour net-load ramp and in each region's monthly
net-load peak, measured in MW relative to the grid without the fleet and
normalized by a fixed regional scale (Equation~\eqref{eq:objective}).
Electricity cost, carbon, and energy use are neither optimized nor reported.
This is the operator's view of the ramp and kW quantities that the system
operator cares about; no demand-response program, tariff, market signal, or
payment is modeled, and reductions are reported as reductions in the modeled
score rather than as reserve, cost, or reliability savings. The forecasts, the
priority-based split, the 24-hour window, and the no-flexibility baseline are
each taken from the studies above, so that the departure lies in the objective
alone.

\section{Google ClusterData2019 Workload Trace}
\label{sec:clusterdata2019}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-role}

A \emph{Borg cell} is a group of machines managed as one computing cluster.
Google provides records for eight cells, labeled \(a\)--\(h\), from May 2019
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019, tirmazi2020borg}. The trace includes machine
capacity, per-instance resource usage, collection events, priorities, and
resource requests. It is sanitized: it contains no user, application,
location, or other identifying information.

Three tables supply the workload data used here: \codepath{machine_events}
for the CPU reference capacity, \codepath{instance_usage} for measured CPU
execution, and \codepath{collection_events} for priority. The full trace is
approximately 2.4 TiB, so it is queried through BigQuery rather than
downloaded.

Rather than tracking individual jobs and their execution, the aggregate CPU
utilization in each five-minute window is used as demand. This is how the
trace is summarized by Tirmazi \emph{et al.} in \emph{Borg: The Next
Generation} \cite{tirmazi2020borg}, who plot each priority tier's CPU usage as
a fraction of cell capacity over hour-long intervals (their Fig.~2). Aggregate
flexible and inflexible CPU usage per cluster is likewise the quantity
scheduled by Radovanovi\'{c} \emph{et al.} in \emph{Carbon-Aware Computing
for Datacenters} \cite{radovanovic2021carbonaware}, from Google's internal
fleet data rather than this trace, and utilization curves from Alibaba and
Google traces drive the workload environment of \emph{SustainDC}
\cite{naug2024sustaindc}.

\subsection{Fixed reference capacity}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-capacity}

Google expresses CPU capacity in normalized units, with the largest machine in
a cell equal to 1.0. A cell's reference capacity is then defined as the sum
over its machines:
\begin{equation}
  C^{\mathrm{ref}}_c
  =
  \sum_{n\in\mathcal{J}_c}\kappa_n ,
  \label{eq:reference-capacity}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{J}_c\) is all machines ever observed in cell \(c\)
and \(\kappa_n\) is machine \(n\)'s largest reported
\codepath{capacity.cpus}. Machines can have multiple add, update, and
snapshot records in \codepath{machine_events}; the largest reported capacity
is kept. This gives a fixed monthly reference rather than the active capacity
in each five-minute interval, so the utilization defined next is measured
relative to that fixed reference.

\subsection{Five-minute executed CPU}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-utilization}

For each five-minute window, recorded CPU usage is summed and divided by the
cell's fixed reference capacity. Let \(k\) identify the window and
\(\mathcal{E}_{c,k}\) denote its set of retained usage rows:
\begin{equation}
  u_{c,k}
  =
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathcal{E}_{c,k}} a_r}
         {C^{\mathrm{ref}}_c} ,
  \label{eq:clusterdata-utilization}
\end{equation}
where \(a_r\) is row \(r\)'s \codepath{average_usage.cpus} value. Both the
numerator and denominator use Google's normalized CPU units, so
\(u_{c,k}\) is dimensionless.

Only top-level instances and full five-minute measurement windows are
retained. Using only top-level instances avoids counting both an allocation
set and the work running inside it. Google reports each instance's usage over
windows of at most 300 seconds \cite{googleTraceFormatV3}. Following the
instance-usage query example in Google's analysis notebook
\cite{googleClusterAnalysisColab}, rows whose \codepath{end_time-start_time}
is at least 300 seconds are kept, so each retained \(a_r\) is an average over
a whole window. An audit of cells \(a\)--\(d\) found that the 300-second
filter removes 6.5\% of CPU work, proportionally more batch (11--15\% of batch
work) than service (2--8\%). Hour by hour, the retained curves correlate with
all-row curves at 0.96--0.99 in level and 0.88--0.97 in one-hour ramps, with
slightly larger relative ramps (2.6--4.9\%). The retained curves therefore
describe retained execution rather than all execution.

\subsection{Modeled service and batch classes}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-tier-split}

Google records job priorities rather than explicit service and batch
labels. The priority bands from Google's analysis in
Table~\ref{tab:priority-bands} are used, and work in the no-SLO bands is
assumed to be deferrable. Priority comes from \codepath{collection_events}:
\codepath{type=0} events are kept, the highest priority recorded for each
\codepath{collection_id} is taken, and that priority is joined to the usage
rows in BigQuery.

Priority 115 or below is classified as batch and the remaining usage as
service. Let \(p_r\) be the priority attached to row \(r\), and
\(\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{bat}}_{c,k}\) the set of rows in
\(\mathcal{E}_{c,k}\) with \(p_r\leq115\). Usage rows without a matching
priority record remain in the service residual. The two classes are
\begin{align}
  u^{\mathrm{bat}}_{c,k}
  &=
  \frac{1}{C^{\mathrm{ref}}_c}
  \sum_{r\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{bat}}_{c,k}} a_r,
  \label{eq:batch-utilization}\\
  u^{\mathrm{svc}}_{c,k}
  &=
  u_{c,k}-u^{\mathrm{bat}}_{c,k}.
  \label{eq:service-utilization}
\end{align}
These curves are referred to as \emph{deferrable batch} and
\emph{nondeferrable service}. Four cells supply the workload profiles, which
are converted to hourly arrivals in Section~\ref{sec:data-transformations}.
Appendix~\ref{app:data-schemas} lists the stored columns and example values.

\section{Google PowerData2019 Power Model}
\label{sec:powerdata2019}

\subsection{Data fit}
\label{subsec:powerdata-purpose}

PowerData2019 \cite{googlePowerData2019} provides five-minute normalized
power-domain measurements for the same Borg cells. These measurements
include data-center-floor cooling, as documented under
\codepath{measured_power_util} on p.~2 of the \emph{Google Data Center Power
Trace} documentation \cite{googlePowerTraceDocumentation}.
CPU utilization is used to predict measured power-domain utilization; both
series are averaged by cell and hour before an ordinary least-squares line is
fitted:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_c(u)
  =
  \alpha_c+\beta_c u.
  \label{eq:powerdata-affine-fit}
\end{equation}
The intercept \(\alpha_c\) is the modeled power draw at zero CPU utilization,
and \(\beta_c\) describes how power increases with utilization. The fitted
idle draw ranges from
about 38\% to 55\% of reference power across the cells.

\section{Grid, Market, and Forecast Data}
\label{sec:energy-data}

\subsection{Four market cases}
\label{subsec:energy-sources}

Each modeled site is assigned to an electricity market. Hourly demand and
generation by fuel are taken from the EIA grid monitor \cite{eiaGridMonitor}.
CAISO NP15 draws on the CISO balancing authority, MISO Minnesota on MISO,
SPP North on SWPP, and ISO-NE NEMA on ISNE.

For each region, demand, wind generation, and solar generation are
used.\footnote{The EIA fields \codepath{D},
\codepath{NG.WND}, and \codepath{NG.SUN} are stored as
\codepath{gross_demand_mw}, \codepath{wind_mw}, and \codepath{solar_mw}.}
EIA labels each interval by its ending hour. One hour is subtracted to store
hour-beginning UTC timestamps, giving one row per market per hour.

\subsection{Net load}
\label{subsec:energy-net-load}

Net load is demand minus wind and solar generation. Let \(\tau\) be a UTC
hour, \(D_m\) demand, and \(G^{\mathrm{wnd}}_m\), \(G^{\mathrm{sol}}_m\)
observed wind and solar output, all in MW:
\begin{equation}
  N_m(\tau)
  =
  D_m(\tau)
  -G^{\mathrm{wnd}}_m(\tau)
  -G^{\mathrm{sol}}_m(\tau).
  \label{eq:energy-net-load}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Forecasts}
\label{subsec:energy-forecasts}

Each panel row carries gross-demand and net-load forecasts with lead times of
one, three, six, and twelve hours from its issue time, which is the row's own
hour. The decision at hour \(t\) reads the row for \(t-1\), so the forecasts
available to it target \(t\), \(t+2\), \(t+5\), and \(t+11\): the current
decision hour and two, five, and eleven hours later. Queue urgency is reported
separately as work due within one, three, six, twelve, and twenty-four
execution slots.

No external forecast product is used. The forecasts are produced from the EIA
data by ridge regression, fitted and written by
\codepath{scripts/build_causal_forecasts.py}, so that their inputs, their
fitting, their output, and their accuracy are known exactly. Each is described
in turn.

\subsubsection{Model}
For each market \(m\), each quantity \(Y\in\{D_m,N_m\}\), and each lead
\(h\in\{1,3,6,12\}\), a separate direct multi-step model predicts the value
\(h\) hours after the issue hour \(\tau\) from 28 inputs \(x_k(\tau)\)
observed at or before \(\tau\):
\begin{align}
  \widehat Y(\tau+h\mid\tau)
  &=
  \bar y+\sum_{k=1}^{28} w_k\,\frac{x_k(\tau)-\bar x_k}{\sigma_k},
  \label{eq:forecast-model}\\
  \mathbf w
  &=
  \left(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X+\alpha\mathbf I\right)^{-1}
  \mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\left(\mathbf y-\bar y\mathbf 1\right).
  \label{eq:forecast-ridge}
\end{align}
Here \(\bar x_k\) and \(\sigma_k\) are the mean and standard deviation of
input \(k\) over the training rows, \(\mathbf X\) is the matrix of
standardized inputs over those rows, \(\mathbf y\) is the vector of targets
\(Y(\tau+h)\), \(\bar y\) is its mean, and \(\alpha>0\) is the ridge penalty.
The intercept is the training mean of the target and is not penalized, and the
coefficients are computed in closed form. Four markets, two quantities, and
four leads give 32 models. A separate model per lead is what makes the four
forecast columns differ; one one-step model applied recursively, or one series
copied into every column, would not.

\subsubsection{Inputs}
Table~\ref{tab:forecast-inputs} lists the inputs. Every input is a function of
observations at or before the issue hour. The level at \(\tau\) alone
reproduces a persistence forecast, so any improvement over persistence must
come from the other inputs. The one- and three-hour changes are included
because the objective concerns ramps: the rate at which a series is already
moving carries information beyond its level. Wind and solar output at the
issue hour drive the gap between gross demand and net load, and the calendar
terms carry the daily and weekly cycles. Causality is checked mechanically:
the inputs are rebuilt from a copy of the series truncated six hours early,
and every value on the overlapping hours must be unchanged to within
\(10^{-9}\). A column that used a later row would move, and the build stops.
The panel loader separately rejects any forecast whose recorded issue time is
later than its row's hour.

\subsubsection{Fitting}
A model is fitted on rows whose issue hour and target hour both fall in the
eleven training months. No April row with a May target is used, and no May
hour serves as an issue hour or a target hour in any fit or in the penalty
selection. Rows without a complete input set are excluded: the weekly lag and
the 168-hour mean need a week of history, so the first 168 hours of the panel,
through 2025-01-07 22:00 UTC, carry no model forecast. The rows that remain
number 7,847 per model at the one-hour lead and 7,825 at the twelve-hour lead.
The penalty \(\alpha\) is chosen from \(\{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000\}\) by
rolling-origin cross-validation: the training rows are placed in time order,
their second half is divided into four consecutive blocks of 981 rows, and for
each block a model is fitted on every earlier row and scored on that block by
mean absolute error. The penalty with the lowest mean error over the four
blocks is kept, and the model is refitted on all training rows. A model is
therefore never scored on an hour that precedes the hours it was fitted on.
The selected penalties range from 0.01, for most ISO-NE and SPP models, to
1000, for the CAISO net-load models at the three- and six-hour leads. The
standardization statistics \(\bar x_k\) and \(\sigma_k\) are computed once on
all training rows before the cross-validation.

\subsubsection{Output}
The output of each model is a point forecast, in MW, of the target quantity
at \(\tau+h\). For every panel row, the eight forecasts are written to the
columns \codepath{forecast_gross_h1_mw} through
\codepath{forecast_gross_h12_mw} and \codepath{forecast_net_h1_mw} through
\codepath{forecast_net_h12_mw}, listed in Appendix~\ref{app:data-schemas};
the issue time is recorded as the row's own hour in
\codepath{forecast_issue_time_utc}; and the model version is recorded in
\codepath{forecast_vintage_id} and in the calendar file. Rows without a
complete input set receive the persistence value, the issue-hour observation
of the target quantity, in every forecast column. The scheduler reads these
columns as part of its observation and never calls the model. Scoring always
uses the realized net load, as Section~\ref{subsec:problem-causality} states.

\subsubsection{Accuracy}
Accuracy is measured by the mean absolute error (MAE) between each forecast

\subsection{Pairing repeated workload with grid time}
\label{subsec:transform-pairing}

The 744-hour Google workload restarts at the beginning of each calendar
month; shorter months use only the hours they need. The same workload is
used in the eleven training months and the May validation month.

Each month is an independent, continuous simulation. Queues and site-power
history carry across midnight, and every decision hour is scored. The
decision hours for month \(e\) are \(\mathcal T_e=\{0,\ldots,T_e-1\}\), where
\(T_e\) is its length in hours. One preceding hour,
\(\mathcal W=\{-1\}\), initializes the grid and site-power history for the
first decision and ramp calculation.
Its power is computed by executing the preceding workload in place;
that warm-up work is not added to the queues. The warm-up hour is excluded
from monthly peak maxima.

Queues start empty and must be empty at month-end. Arrivals in the final
23 hours receive shorter completion windows under
Equation~\eqref{eq:batch-deadline}.

Let \(s_{m,t}\) and \(q_{m,t}\) denote hourly service and batch arrivals at
site \(m\). Service must run immediately; batch may wait.

The five-minute utilization curves from
Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019} are converted to work by averaging the
twelve windows in each hour, then multiplying by the site's hourly capacity
\(K_m\Delta t\). Each hour's recorded CPU execution is treated as work
arriving at the start of the corresponding simulation hour, and the hour's
aggregate work is assumed known at that moment; for example, average
utilization of 60\% becomes 0.6 work units. Let \(t\) be the hour within the
month:
\begin{align}
  s_{m,t}
  &=
  \frac{K_m\Delta t}{12}
  \sum_{b=0}^{11} u^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,12t+b},
  \label{eq:synthetic-service-arrivals}\\
  q_{m,t}
  &=
  \frac{K_m\Delta t}{12}
  \sum_{b=0}^{11} u^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,12t+b}.
  \label{eq:synthetic-batch-arrivals}
\end{align}
With \(K_m=1\) work unit per hour
and \(\Delta t=1\) h, the resulting work amounts equal the hourly average
utilization values numerically.

Grid data from the corresponding UTC hours are used. If \(\tau_e\) is the
month's UTC start time,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    D_{m,t} &= D_m(\tau_e+t\Delta t), \\
    N_{m,t} &= N_m(\tau_e+t\Delta t).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:episode-grid-map}
\end{equation}
This mapping also applies to the preceding warm-up hour.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

\subsection{Sets, inputs, and units}
\label{subsec:problem-given-inputs}

Let \(\mathcal M=\{1,\ldots,4\}\) be the modeled sites and their paired market
cases. Indices \(i\) and \(j\) identify batch origin and execution
destination, respectively. Let
\(\mathcal T_e=\{0,\ldots,T_e-1\}\) be the decision-slot indices of simulated
month \(e\) with \(T_e\) hours, and \(\mathcal W=\{-1\}\) the preceding warm-up
slot. One interval has duration \(\Delta t=1\) h. The month index is omitted
from hourly quantities.

For each site and hour, the model uses synthetic service arrivals
\(s_{m,t}\), synthetic batch arrivals \(q_{m,t}\), standardized compute
capacity \(K_m\), reference power \(\overline P_m\), power coefficients
\(\alpha_m,\beta_m\), the batch window \(H\), regional net load \(N_{m,t}\),
and training-only scale \(S_m\).

Work can be divided among sites. Individual jobs, memory limits, machine
types, application dependencies, and scheduling within a site are not
modeled.

\subsection{Scheduler controls}
\label{subsec:problem-decisions}

Each hour, the scheduler chooses:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\), service executed at destination \(j\);
  \item \(z_t\), total queued batch work executed now; and
  \item \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\), that batch total divided by destination.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Immediate service}
\label{subsec:problem-service}

All service arrivals must execute in the same hour; the scheduler chooses
where:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\in\mathcal M}x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}
  =
  \sum_{m\in\mathcal M}s_{m,t},
  \qquad
  x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\geq0.
  \label{eq:service-conservation}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Batch queue and deadlines}
\label{subsec:problem-batch}

The scheduler chooses how much batch work to execute and distributes it
among sites with available capacity. Its execution site determines its
power consumption. Let \(z_t\) be the total release, \(y_{i,t}\) the work
released from origin \(i\)'s backlog, and \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\) the work
executed at destination \(j\). Released work executes within the same hour:
\begin{equation}
  z_t
  =
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal M}y_{i,t}
  =
  \sum_{j\in\mathcal M}x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t},
  \qquad
  x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\geq0.
  \label{eq:batch-total}
\end{equation}
Let \(B_{i,t}\) be origin \(i\)'s backlog before the current arrivals.
Arrivals increase the backlog, and execution reduces it. Released work
cannot exceed available work:
\begin{align}
  B_{i,t+1}
  &=
  B_{i,t}+q_{i,t}-y_{i,t},
  \label{eq:queue-balance}\\
  0
  &\leq y_{i,t}\leq B_{i,t}+q_{i,t}.
  \label{eq:queue-availability}
\end{align}
With \(H=24\), batch may execute immediately or in the following 23 hourly
slots, completing within 24 hours and by month-end. A 24-hour completion
window for flexible work is the convention of Google's carbon-aware system
\cite{radovanovic2021carbonaware} and of the batch model of Haghshenas
\emph{et al.} \cite{haghshenas2022infrastructure}. \(H\) is an assumption
rather than a measurement, because the trace records when work ran, not when
it was due.

An arrival in slot \(\nu\) must therefore run by slot
\begin{equation}
  d_{\nu}
  =
  \min\{\nu+H-1,\;T_e-1\}.
  \label{eq:batch-deadline}
\end{equation}
Releases follow one global earliest-deadline-first queue, breaking ties by
origin index and then arrival order. They are increased when necessary to
leave enough capacity to complete the remaining work on time. For each
origin, cumulative execution must cover all work due:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\rho=0}^{t}y_{i,\rho}
  \geq
  \sum_{\substack{\nu\in\mathcal T_e\\d_{\nu}\leq t}}q_{i,\nu},
  \qquad i\in\mathcal M,\ t\in\mathcal T_e.
  \label{eq:deadline-feasibility}
\end{equation}
Finally, the month must end with every backlog empty:
\begin{equation}
  B_{i,T_e}=0,
  \qquad i\in\mathcal M.
  \label{eq:terminal-queue}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Capacity and site power}
\label{subsec:problem-feasibility}

Service and batch execution together cannot exceed the site's hourly
capacity:
\begin{align}
  w_{m,t}
  &=
  x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}
  +x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t},
  \label{eq:executed-work}\\
  0
  &\leq w_{m,t}\leq K_m\Delta t.
  \label{eq:site-capacity}
\end{align}
Executed work is converted to power using the model from
Section~\ref{sec:powerdata2019} and the 500 MW reference scale:
\begin{equation}
  P_{m,t}
  =
  \overline P_m
  \left(
    \alpha_m+\beta_m\frac{w_{m,t}}{K_m\Delta t}
  \right)
  \quad\mathrm{MW}.
  \label{eq:runtime-power}
\end{equation}

Work may execute at any site with available capacity. Transfers have no
modeled latency, bandwidth limit, data-residency restriction, energy cost,
or movement charge. This gives the model an optimistic upper bound on
geographic flexibility.

\subsection{Adjusted net load, ramps, and peaks}
\label{subsec:problem-objective}

The regional net load after adding modeled site power is
\begin{equation}
  A_{m,t}
  =
  N_{m,t}+P_{m,t}.
  \label{eq:adjusted-net-load}
\end{equation}
Changes in net load between consecutive hours are measured, before and after
adding fleet power. This one-hour interval matches the data and scheduling
decisions. Dividing by \(S_m\Delta t\) expresses each change as a fraction of
the market's demand scale per hour:
\begin{align}
  \Delta^{\mathrm{original}}_{m,t}
  &=
  \frac{N_{m,t}-N_{m,t-1}}{S_m\Delta t},
  \label{eq:native-ramp}\\
  \Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}
  &=
  \frac{A_{m,t}-A_{m,t-1}}{S_m\Delta t}.
  \label{eq:adjusted-ramp}
\end{align}
Squaring penalizes larger ramps and treats upward and downward ramps
equally. The fleet's ramp impact is measured by
subtracting the original grid's squared ramp:
\begin{equation}
  I_{m,t}
  =
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}\right)^2
  -
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{original}}_{m,t}\right)^2.
  \label{eq:incremental-impact}
\end{equation}
A negative value means the scheduled fleet reduced the squared ramp relative
to the original grid; a positive value means it increased it.

The reduction of each region's monthly net-load peak is the second aim. Under
scheduling policy \(\mu\), its normalized adjusted and original peaks in
month \(e\) are
\begin{align}
  Q^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,e}(\mu)
  &=
  \max_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\frac{A_{m,t}(\mu)}{S_m},
  \label{eq:monthly-peak}\\
  Q^{\mathrm{original}}_{m,e}
  &=
  \max_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\frac{N_{m,t}}{S_m}.
  \label{eq:monthly-original-peak}
\end{align}
Each takes the highest net load during the month and divides it by the
region's demand scale, \(S_m\). As with ramps, the fleet's peak
impact is measured by subtracting the original grid's peak:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{m,e}(\mu)
  =
  Q^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,e}(\mu)-Q^{\mathrm{original}}_{m,e}.
  \label{eq:peak-impact}
\end{equation}
No schedule can change the original peak, so subtracting it changes every
schedule's score by the same amount. It keeps the grid's own peak records,
which the scheduler cannot influence, out of the hourly training signal.
Site power is positive, so \(\Phi_{m,e}(\mu)>0\).

Ramp impact and monthly peaks have different numerical scales. To combine
them in one objective, each is divided by a fixed positive reference value.
The no-flexibility policy,
\(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\), runs all work immediately at its original site.
Using this policy over \(\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}\), the references are
calculated as
\begin{align}
  C_R
  &=
  \frac{1}{|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|}
  \sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}}
  \sum_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M}
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})\right)^2,
  \label{eq:ramp-reference}\\
  C_Q
  &=
  \frac{1}{|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|}
  \sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M}
  Q^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,e}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}).
  \label{eq:peak-reference}
\end{align}
Both references are totals for the whole grid with the no-flexibility fleet:
\(C_R\) is its average monthly total of squared ramps, and \(C_Q\) is its
average monthly sum of normalized regional peaks. Dividing by them expresses
a change in \(\sum_{t,m}I_{m,t}\) or \(\sum_m\Phi_{m,e}\) as a fraction of total
squared ramp or of total peak, so the equal weights below value equal
fractional reductions of the two totals. The fleet can change total ramps
proportionally more than total peaks, so ramp gains usually contribute more
to the joint score. Both references remain fixed during training and May
validation.

\subsection{Joint objective}
\label{subsec:problem-normalization}

Each simulated month \(e\) is one optimization episode. Decisions are hourly,
and the objective covers the full month. The schedule with the
smallest equally weighted combination of scaled monthly ramp impact and
regional monthly peak impact is sought.
Let \(\mathfrak M\) be the causal scheduling policies satisfying
Equations~\eqref{eq:service-conservation}--\eqref{eq:site-capacity}:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    &\min_{\mu\in\mathfrak M} J_e(\mu),\\
    &J_e(\mu)
    =
    \frac{0.5}{C_R}
    \sum_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M} I_{m,t}(\mu)\\
    &\hspace{2em}
    +\frac{0.5}{C_Q}
    \sum_{m\in\mathcal M} \Phi_{m,e}(\mu).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The 0.5 weights apply after dividing each term by its reference scale.
A lower joint score need not improve both components.

\subsection{No-flexibility comparison}
\label{subsec:problem-evaluation}

The scheduler is compared with \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\) using
the same arrivals, power models, grid data, forecasts, and month. Improvement
is reported as
\begin{equation}
  \text{Improvement}
  =
  J_e(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})-J_e(\mu),
  \label{eq:improvement}
\end{equation}
which is positive when the scheduler \(\mu\) improves on the baseline.

The ramp-impact and peak components are reported separately, along with
regional adjusted peaks in MW and their reductions from
the no-flexibility fleet. Since site power is nonnegative, peak reduction
means improvement over that fleet without flexibility, not a peak below the
original grid without the fleet.

\subsection{Causal information}
\label{subsec:problem-causality}

The scheduler chooses an action before the hour-\(t\) net load is revealed.
Once the hour ends, the action is scored using that realized net load.
Its available information, \(\mathcal I_t\), consists of:
\begin{itemize}
  \item gross demand and net load for hour \(t-1\);
  \item the gross-demand and net-load forecasts issued at hour \(t-1\),
        which target \(t\), \(t+2\), \(t+5\), and \(t+11\) (the current
        decision hour and two, five, and eleven hours later);
  \item the hour-\(t\) service and batch arrivals, queue totals, and urgency;
  \item modeled site power from hour \(t-1\);
  \item each region's running adjusted and original net-load peaks over
        completed decision hours, normalized by \(S_m\) (zero before the
        first decision); and
  \item UTC hour, UTC day of week, and the fraction of the month remaining.
\end{itemize}

Each decision under policy \(\mu\) must use only \(\mathcal I_t\). The methods
chapter will describe the observation features and solution algorithm.

\subsection{Formal problem statement}
\label{subsec:problem-formal-statement}

\begin{quote}
Choose a causal policy for hourly service destinations, batch release
volumes, and batch destinations to minimize the joint monthly ramp-impact and
regional net-load peak score in Equation~\eqref{eq:objective}. Use only the
information available at each decision and meet the service, deadline, and
capacity constraints above. Report improvement over the no-flexibility
baseline using Equation~\eqref{eq:improvement}.
\end{quote}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The study has the following limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The workload comes from one company and one month in 2019, and
        measured execution is reused as synthetic arrival demand.
  \item The service/batch split, completion windows, standardized compute
        capacity, 500 MW scale, and cell-to-market mapping all include
        modeling assumptions.
  \item The workload extraction uses a fixed monthly capacity denominator,
        removes partial five-minute rows, and places unmatched priorities in
        the service residual.
  \item The affine power fit is deterministic, omits fit uncertainty,
        and is extrapolated when scheduling pushes a site
        outside its commonly observed CPU range.
  \item The forecasts are ridge regressions fitted here on public EIA data.
        An operator would hold system-operator or commercial forecasts of
        different accuracy, and the scheduler's benefit may depend on that
        accuracy.
  \item The warm-up period provides only one hour, which limits the
        scheduler's history of grid observations.
\end{itemize}

        computes Equations~\eqref{eq:native-ramp}--\eqref{eq:incremental-impact};
        \codepath{_step_info} in \codepath{environment.py} applies it to each
        market, using the previous hour's site power.
  \item \codepath{update_peak} in \codepath{env/ramp_v6/objective.py}
        maintains running maxima. \codepath{_step_info} tracks each region's
        adjusted and original peaks and charges each hour the adjusted increase
        minus the original increase. Running maxima telescope, so a month of
        these increments sums to \(\Phi_{m,e}\).
  \item \codepath{reference_trajectory_scores} scores one month under
        \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\). \codepath{calibrate_objective} in the factory
        script averages those scores over the training months and stores the
        results, with each month's scores, under
        \codepath{objective_calibration} in \codepath{factory.json}.
        \codepath{objective_from_factory} loads them, and
        \codepath{tests/energy_model_v3/test_four_market_v2.py} checks that May
        is never read.
  \item \codepath{JointObjective} implements
        Equation~\eqref{eq:objective}: \codepath{score} returns \(J_e\), and
        \codepath{reward_components} gives each hour's share, which sums to
        \(-J_e\) over a month. The weights are \codepath{component_weights} in
        the protocol file, and \codepath{OBJECTIVE_VERSION} labels the
        definition so that results under different definitions are never
        mixed. \codepath{tests/ramp_v6/test_joint_objective.py} checks the
        monthly identity.
\end{itemize}
