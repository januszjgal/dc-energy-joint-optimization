\section{Background: Data Centers on the Electric Grid}
\label{sec:intro-background}

Large data centers consume enough electrical power that the timing and location of
their computing workloads affect the power grids that serve them.

\section{Workload Flexibility: Timing and Location}
\label{sec:intro-flexibility}

Some workloads must execute immediately, whereas lower-priority workloads can be
deferred. An operator with multiple sites can also select where these workloads
execute. In this study, workloads that must execute immediately are referred to as
service, and workloads that can be deferred are referred to as batch. These two
levers, deferring batch workloads and routing workloads across sites, are the only
ones the scheduler controls. In each hour, the scheduler determines where service
executes, how much batch work to execute, and where to execute it.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}
\label{sec:intro-questions}

This study addresses the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{How much can a causal scheduler jointly smooth each market's normalized
one-hour net-load ramps and reduce its monthly net-load peak, using temporal
deferral and geographic routing while satisfying immediate-service,
batch-window, and site-capacity constraints?}
\end{quote}

A scheduler is \emph{causal} when each hourly decision relies only on information
available at that hour, never on future demand, generation, or workload. The
scheduler operates on aggregate workloads across sites and hours rather than on
individual jobs.

Google workload traces and EIA grid data are used to model a synthetic fleet
of four data centers. Proximal policy
optimization (PPO) is used to train a policy that minimizes an equally
weighted sum of scaled monthly ramp impact and peak impact while satisfying
workload deadlines and site-capacity limits. The objectives of this study are
therefore to construct the fleet from public data, define a joint score for ramps
and peaks, train the scheduler, and compare it with a no-flexibility baseline that
executes all work where and when it was recorded.
% [stages 2026-10-21] was: "The comparison is made by four-fold

The scheduler is trained and validated on the twelve months of 2025 using
four-fold cross-validation, and it is evaluated on May 2026, the test month, using
a second fleet built from different Google cells.

\section{Scope and Assumptions}
\label{sec:intro-scope}

This study adopts the perspective of the operator of a multi-site data center fleet,
who controls only where service executes and when and where batch work executes.
Each market's demand, wind, and solar generation are retrieved from historical
records and cannot be influenced by the scheduler. Electricity prices, carbon emissions, on-site
generation, and demand-response programs or payments are not modeled.
Cooling is not controlled, although the measured power used here already
includes data-center-floor cooling.
The scheduler is compared with the no-flexibility baseline, which executes all work
at its original site in its recorded hour.

Three modeling assumptions underlie most of the model. First, the site built from
cell \(c\), the largest training cell, has a rated power of 500~MW, representing a
modern hyperscale data center, and every other site, in both fleets, is scaled by its
measured CPU capacity relative to cell \(c\). Second, each Google cell is mapped to one
electricity market. Third, batch work must be completed within 24 hours. Each
assumption is stated where it is introduced.

The Google cluster trace datasets are large and detailed, containing far more
granularity than this study requires. For instance, the workload trace records
every task across roughly 96,000 machines, but the scheduler operates on
aggregate workloads rather than individual tasks. The workload trace is
therefore condensed into five-minute windows to measure each cell's overall
utilization, distinguishing between work that must run immediately (service)
and work that can wait (batch). Similarly, the power trace is processed into a
linear function per cell that maps CPU utilization directly to power
consumption. Finally, while the grid data retains its original hourly
resolution, it is extended with the following derived parameters: net load, a
fixed demand scale for each market, and multi-hour lookahead forecasts.

The two Google traces cover May 2019, and the grid data spans calendar year
2025 and May 2026.

\section{Data Center Workload Data: Google ClusterData2019}
\label{sec:clusterdata2019}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-role}

A \emph{Borg cell} is a set of heterogeneous machines managed together as a
single autonomous administrative unit. The Google May 2019 trace contains data
for eight cells, labeled \(a\)--\(h\)
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019, tirmazi2020borg}. Borg supports two kinds of
resource requests: a job and an allocation set. A \emph{job} is a
user-submitted computation composed of one or more \emph{tasks} (i.e.,
individual programs running on single machines). An \emph{allocation set} is a
reservation of machine resources within which jobs can run. Google's
documentation categorizes jobs and allocation sets as \emph{collections}, and
tasks and reserved resource blocks as \emph{instances}
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}. For each machine and task, the trace records
machine capacity, actual CPU utilization, requested resources, and job
priority, all stripped of user, application, or other identifying information.

This study uses three data tables of the trace: Machine Events (to determine
each cell's total CPU capacity), Instance Usage (to track actual CPU utilization),
and Collection Events (to extract job priorities). Because the trace is approximately 2.4 TiB compressed, local downloads are impractical, and
the trace is queried online through BigQuery, Google's cloud database service.

Rather than tracking individual jobs and their execution, this study uses
aggregate CPU utilization within five-minute windows to represent computing
demand. This summarization approach follows Tirmazi \emph{et al.}
\cite{tirmazi2020borg}, where CPU usage per priority tier was tracked over
hour-long intervals, and the utilization analysis provided in Google's GitHub
repository \cite{googleClusterAnalysisColab}.

\subsection{Fixed Reference Capacity}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-capacity}

Google measures CPU in Google Compute Units (GCUs). In the released trace, GCUs
are normalized so that the largest machine across all eight cells has a CPU
capacity of 1.0 \cite[p.~4]{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}, which the machine tables of
all eight cells confirm. Thus, a machine with capacity
0.5 possesses half the CPU capacity of the largest machine. A cell's
\emph{reference capacity} \(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}\) is defined as the
sum of these normalized GCUs over all machines in that cell:
% [symbols 2026-10-09] was:
%   \sum_{n\in\mathcal{J}_c}\kappa_n ,
% where \(\mathcal{J}_c\), the \emph{machine set} of cell \(c\), is all
% machines ever observed in the cell, and \(\kappa_n\), the \emph{machine CPU
% capacity}, is machine \(n\)'s CPU capacity.
\begin{equation}
  C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}
  =
  \sum_{\text{machines in } cell}
  \bigl(\text{machine CPU capacity}\bigr),
  \label{eq:reference-capacity}
\end{equation}
where the summation extends over every machine ever observed in the cell.
Because a machine can appear in the Machine Events table multiple times throughout the
month, such as when it is added, removed, or updated, its maximum recorded
capacity is used \cite{tirmazi2020borg, googleClusterAnalysisColab}.

\subsection{Five-Minute Executed CPU}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-utilization}

For each five-minute window \(k\), the CPU usage by all tasks in a cell is
summed and divided by the cell's fixed reference capacity. The resulting
metric, the \emph{cell CPU utilization} \(u_{\mathrm{cell},k}\), represents the
fraction of total cell capacity in use during that time window:
% [symbols 2026-10-09] was:
% utilization} \(u_{c,k}\), is the share of the cell's capacity in use. Let \(k\)
% identify the window and \(\mathcal{E}_{c,k}\), the window's \emph{measurement
% set}, denote its retained usage measurements:
%   \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathcal{E}_{c,k}} a_r}
% where \(a_r\), the \emph{measured CPU use}, is the average CPU used during
% measurement \(r\), on the same scale as machine capacity: a task that keeps a
\begin{equation}
  u_{\mathrm{cell},k}
  =
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{r\ \text{in window}\ k} a_r\,\ell_r/300}
         {C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}} ,
  \label{eq:clusterdata-utilization}
\end{equation}
where \(k\) indicates the five-minute windows, and the summation runs over every
usage measurement \(r\) (representing an individual task outside an allocation set
or a reserved block) recorded within window \(k\).
The term \(a_r\) denotes the average CPU usage over the measurement, expressed on the
same normalization scale as machine capacity, and \(\ell_r\) denotes the measurement's
length in seconds. For example, a task that fully utilizes a machine of capacity 0.5
throughout the five-minute window yields \(a_r=0.5\) and \(\ell_r=300\), contributing
0.5 to the sum. The same task measured for only 60 seconds contributes 0.1.
The numerator of Equation~\eqref{eq:clusterdata-utilization}, the total CPU usage of
the window, is therefore on the same scale in every cell, and the denominator turns
it into a fraction of the cell's capacity.

Two properties of the usage measurements determine the form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:clusterdata-utilization}. First, tasks in an
allocation set draw resources from a reserved machine block, and Google reports
the block's aggregate usage across all contained tasks
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}. To avoid double-counting CPU consumption,
individual measurements for tasks within a block are dropped, retaining only
the block's top-level measurement. Second, usage measurement windows last five minutes
or less: they are typically five minutes long, and Google records shorter windows when a
task starts, stops, or is updated
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019, googleTraceFormatV3}. No measurement exceeds 300 seconds
or crosses a window boundary. All windows are included, and because \(a_r\) is an average
over the measurement's own length, each is weighted by \(\ell_r/300\), the share of the
five-minute window that it covers.

\section{Data Center Power Data: Google PowerData2019}
\label{sec:powerdata2019}

\subsection{Data Fit}
\label{subsec:powerdata-purpose}

The PowerData2019 dataset \cite{googlePowerData2019} provides five-minute power
measurements for the same eight Borg cells discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019}. Electricity reaches machines through power
distribution units, each supplying several rows of server racks. Google defines
the set of machines behind a single power distribution unit as a
\emph{power domain}, and each cell has multiple domains. Every five minutes, the
trace records each domain's power draw as a fraction of its rated capacity. For
example, a value of 0.6 indicates that the domain consumed 60\% of its rated
power. Absolute power in MW is not published in the trace. Each fraction is
relative to that domain's own capacity \cite{googlePowerTraceDocumentation}.
Because each cell has multiple domains, the cell-level power fraction is the average over
its domains, weighted by their machines' CPU capacity, just as the cell's CPU utilization
weights them. Per the
\emph{Google Data Center Power Trace} documentation
\cite{googlePowerTraceDocumentation}, the power measurements incorporate both IT
load and data center cooling. To predict the power fraction from CPU
utilization, both series are averaged by cell and by hour before fitting an
ordinary least-squares linear regression. Only complete hours are used, because the
first and last hours of the trace hold fewer than twelve five-minute windows. The
resulting model estimates the
\emph{power fraction} \(\widehat p_{\mathrm{cell}}(u)\) of a cell as a function
of its hourly CPU utilization \(u\):
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{\mathrm{cell}}(u)
  =
  \alpha_{\mathrm{cell}}+\beta_{\mathrm{cell}} u.
  \label{eq:powerdata-affine-fit}
\end{equation}
The intercept \(\alpha_{\mathrm{cell}}\), the \emph{idle power fraction},
represents the modeled power draw at zero CPU utilization, while the slope
\(\beta_{\mathrm{cell}}\), the \emph{dynamic power fraction}, quantifies the
increase in power consumption from zero to full utilization. Note that the
intercept relies on extrapolation, as hourly CPU utilization ranges only between
0.36 and 0.75 in the training cells and between 0.33 and 0.78 in the test cells.
Therefore, the regression model must be projected beyond the empirical domain to
estimate power consumption at zero load.

\section{Power Grid Data: EIA Demand, Wind, and Solar}
\label{sec:energy-data}

\subsection{Four Markets}
\label{subsec:energy-sources}

Each cell is mapped to an electricity market. Hourly demand and fuel-specific
generation profiles are retrieved from the EIA Grid Monitor \cite{eiaGridMonitor}
for four balancing authorities, CAISO, MISO, SPP, and ISO-NE, each of which is
referred to as a \emph{market} in this study.

For each market, hourly time series of gross electricity demand, wind power
generation, and solar power generation are used. Because the EIA indexes data by
interval-ending hours, timestamps are shifted back by one hour to align with the
beginning-of-hour convention used in Google traces. Records for each month of
EIA data are compiled into a \emph{panel} table. For the training and validation months, the
data are retrieved for all of 2025, from 06:00 UTC on January 1, 2025 to 06:00 UTC
on January 1, 2026. No earlier data are retrieved, so the first week of January
has no look-back history and its forecasts use the latest observed value. For the
test month, the data are retrieved for
May 2026, alongside the preceding week and the initial 18 hours of June 2026 to
provide the necessary historical context and target sequences for forecasting.

\subsection{Net Load}
\label{subsec:energy-net-load}

Net load is defined as the gross electricity demand minus combined wind and
solar power generation. Let \(\tau\) denote a UTC hour, \(D_m\) the
\emph{gross demand} for market \(m\), and \(G^{\mathrm{wnd}}_m\) and
\(G^{\mathrm{sol}}_m\) its observed \emph{wind power generation} and
\emph{solar power generation}, all in MW. The \emph{net load} \(N_m\) is given by:
\begin{equation}
  N_m(\tau)
  =
  D_m(\tau)
  -G^{\mathrm{wnd}}_m(\tau)
  -G^{\mathrm{sol}}_m(\tau).
  \label{eq:energy-net-load}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy-net-load}, wind and solar power generation are deducted
from the gross demand because they are generally regarded as non-dispatchable.
In contrast, hydroelectricity is treated as dispatchable generation. Net load
thus represents the power demand that must be satisfied by dispatchable
generation sources.

Figure~\ref{fig:net-load-week} illustrates the gross demand and net load for
each market during a week in July. The gap between the two curves
reflects wind and solar power generation: prominent in CAISO and SPP, and
negligible in ISO-NE. Notably, CAISO's net load drops during midday solar peak
hours and ramps up sharply in the evening.

\subsection{Demand and Net-Load Forecasts}
\label{subsec:energy-forecasts}

Each hourly record in the panel includes gross demand and net-load forecasts
issued at that hour, with lead times of one, three, six, and twelve hours.
The decision for hour \(t\) is made at the start of that hour, before its
actual demand and generation are observed. Because each record is indexed by its
beginning hour (Section~\ref{subsec:energy-sources}), the most recent complete
record available at the moment of decision is for hour \(t-1\). Consequently,
making the decision requires querying the panel at hour \(t-1\), accessing
forecasts targeting \(t\), \(t+2\), \(t+5\), and \(t+11\), corresponding to the
current decision hour along with look-ahead horizons of two, five, and eleven
hours. These forecasts are generated from the EIA dataset using ordinary
least-squares regression.

Each forecast is produced by the linear regression model in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:forecast-model}. Let \(Y\) denote either gross demand \(D_m\) or
net load \(N_m\) for a given market, \(\tau\) the \emph{issue hour}, and
\(h\in\{1,3,6,12\}\) the forecast \emph{lead time} in hours. The \emph{forecast}
of \(Y\) for the \emph{target hour} \(\tau+h\)
is expressed as a constant \(b_0\) plus each \emph{input feature}
(\(x_1,\dots,x_9\)) multiplied by its corresponding regression coefficient
(\(b_1,\dots,b_9\)):
% [forecast-eq 2026-10-10] was the three-line form:
%   b_0
%   +b_1\,Y(\tau)
%   +b_2\,\bigl[Y(\tau)-Y(\tau-1)\bigr]
%   +b_3\,Y(\tau-24)
%   +b_4\,Y(\tau+h-24)
%   +b_5\,Y(\tau+h-168)
%   +b_6\sin\frac{2\pi\,\mathrm{hour}(\tau)}{24}
%   +b_7\cos\frac{2\pi\,\mathrm{hour}(\tau)}{24}
%   +b_8\,G^{\mathrm{wnd}}_m(\tau)
%   +b_9\,G^{\mathrm{sol}}_m(\tau).
\begin{equation}
  \widehat Y(\tau+h\mid\tau)
  =
  b_0+b_1x_1+b_2x_2+\dots+b_9x_9 .
  \label{eq:forecast-model}
\end{equation}

representing the time of day, and two features (\(x_8\) and \(x_9\))
corresponding to wind and solar power generation at the issue hour.

The \emph{regression coefficients} \(b_0,\dots,b_9\) are estimated using ordinary
least squares. A separate regression equation is used for each combination of
market (4), response variable (2), and lead time (4), yielding 32 distinct
models. The coefficients vary with lead times because relative to a 1-hour
forecast, a 12-hour forecast relies less on the most recent observation and more
heavily on the value from one week prior. The simplest benchmark,
\emph{persistence}, assumes that the forecasted value \(h\) hours ahead equals
the latest observed value. Mathematically, this corresponds to
Equation~\eqref{eq:forecast-model} with \(b_1=1\) and all other coefficients set
to zero.

Once the regression models are fitted on the training dataset, they are held
constant throughout evaluation. The models are not updated online as the
simulation advances. Instead, only the input features update at each hour as new
data become available. Therefore, a forecast issued during the May 2026 test
evaluation relies strictly on regression models trained on the 2025 dataset and
observations available up to its issue hour.

\subsection{Forecast Fitting}
\label{subsec:forecast-fitting}
% [held-out 2026-10-10] was:
% The training data is the hourly EIA gross demand, net load, wind output,
% and solar output of each balancing authority over the training months. A
% fitting row pairs an issue hour with its target \(h\) hours later. A row is
% used only if its target hour, and every hour its inputs read, lie in training
% months. In a fold, this leaves out the rows whose target falls in a held-out

Each issue hour in the hourly EIA data supplies one data point for the regression:
the nine input features at that hour and the value of \(Y\) realized at the target
hour. A data point is used only if all of its hours, from the oldest input
(\(x_5\)) to the target, fall in training months. This rule keeps a month held out
for evaluation unseen by the regression. Otherwise, its
values would enter through the inputs of the hours after it and the targets of the
hours before it. With all of 2025 as training months, 8,592 of the 8,760 data points
are used for each model, and the May 2026 test month supplies none.

\begin{equation}

  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl|\widehat Y_i-Y_i\bigr|.
  \label{eq:forecast-metrics}
\end{equation}
The MAE represents the average absolute magnitude of the forecast error,
expressed in MW.

% given as a share of the market scale \(S_m\)."
MAE values were evaluated across two evaluation sets and compared against the
persistence baseline model. The cross-validation set comprises all hourly
instances in 2025, where predictions were generated out-of-fold using models
trained without the target month. The test set

The scheduler makes decisions on an hourly basis. Hence, all data center
workload and power reported below correspond to one-hour intervals.
% [no-K 2026-10-10] was: a work unit = what the site built from cell c completes
% in one hour at full utilization; ISO-NE completes at most K_m = 0.846; one work
% unit adds 500 beta_m MW at any site m.
One \emph{work unit} is the computation of the largest machine in the trace operating at
full capacity for one hour. Each site carries this over
to its market: its capacity \(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\) is the
\(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}\) of the cell placed in market \(m\), and
is the most work the site can complete in one hour. A work unit
is the same amount of computation at every site, so work from different
sites can be added and moved between them. A site's utilization is its work
divided by \(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\). Because the power coefficients
\(\alpha_m,\beta_m\) differ, the same work draws different power at each
site.

\subsection{Monthly Simulation and Hourly Workloads}
\label{subsec:transform-pairing}

Google workload data starts at midnight Pacific Daylight Time on May 1, 2019.
Workloads from cells \(a\)--\(d\) are applied to every month in 2025, regardless of
whether a month serves for training or cross validation. Because the dataset spans
only a single month, the 744-hour workload trace loops at the beginning of each
month, with shorter months using only the hours necessary. Workloads from cells
\(e\)--\(h\) are used directly for the test month, May 2026.

Each month is simulated independently as a continuous period. For a given month
\(e\), the set of \emph{decision hours} is defined as
\(\mathcal T_e=\{0,\ldots,T_e-1\}\), where \(T_e\) represents the total number of
hours in that month.

% [symbols 2026-10-09] was: \(\mathcal W=\{-1\}\); for February 2025, ...
Each monthly simulation begins one hour early at a designated \emph{warm-up hour}
\(t=-1\) (e.g., 06:00 UTC on July 1). The warm-up hour is neither a decision hour nor part of performance scoring. Its sole purpose is to establish two
necessary initial conditions for the first decision hour: the grid data and the
site power demand. The grid data is taken directly from historical records, while
the site power demand is calculated by assuming all tasks recorded in that hour are
processed in place without queuing and reallocation to other sites. Consequently,
every month begins with an empty queue of tasks. 

Let \(s_{m,t}\) and \(q_{m,t}\) denote the hourly \emph{service workload} and
\emph{batch workload} at site \(m\), respectively. Service workloads must run
immediately, whereas batch workloads may be deferred.

The normalized CPU utilization at five-minute intervals described in
Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019} is converted to hourly work units by averaging the
twelve intervals within each hour and multiplying by the site's computing capacity
\(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\)
(Eqs.~\eqref{eq:synthetic-service-arrivals} and~\eqref{eq:synthetic-batch-arrivals}).
Multiplying by capacity undoes the division in
Equation~\eqref{eq:clusterdata-utilization}, so the result is the numerator of that
equation averaged over the hour. These work units are measured against a common Google Compute Unit (GCU)
scale, enabling them to be aggregated and migrated across different cells (sites).
Both service work \(s_{m,t}\) and batch work \(q_{m,t}\) represent the aggregate work to be
processed at site \(m\) and are assumed to be known at the beginning of each simulation
hour:

% 0.508, and 0.0897 + 0.0846 = 0.174 work units.
\begin{align}
  s_{m,t}
  &=
  C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\,
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{b=0}^{11} u^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,12t+b}}{12},
  \label{eq:synthetic-service-arrivals}\\[6pt]
  q_{m,t}
  &=
  C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\,
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{b=0}^{11} u^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,12t+b}}{12}.

\subsection{Information Available to the Scheduler}
\label{subsec:problem-causality}

The scheduler makes its decisions before the hour-\(t\) net load is revealed. Once the hour
ends, these decisions are evaluated using the realized net load.

% \(\mathcal I_t\), consists of:
At decision hour \(t\), the scheduler has access only to the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item gross demand and net load for hour \(t-1\);
  \item the gross-demand and net-load forecasts issued at hour \(t-1\),
        which target \(t\), \(t+2\), \(t+5\), and \(t+11\) (the current
        decision hour and two, five, and eleven hours later);
  \item the hour-\(t\) service and batch workloads, and each site's queued
        batch work;
    \item the \emph{urgency} of the queued batch work: how much of it must execute
        within the next 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours;
  \item modeled site power from hour \(t-1\);
    \item each market's highest net load of the month so far, with and without the site's
        power, over completed decision hours (zero before the first decision); and
  \item UTC hour, UTC day of week, and the number of hours left in the
        month.
\end{itemize}

Each decision may use only this information.

\section{Control Decisions}
\label{subsec:problem-decisions}

In each decision hour \(t\in\mathcal T_e\), the scheduler optimizes three primary
operational variables:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Executed service work} \(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\): the amount of
        service workload processed at destination site \(j\);
  \item \emph{Total batch release} \(z_t\): the total queued batch workload released
        for execution; and
  \item \emph{Executed batch work} \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\): the amount of batch
        workload processed at destination site \(j\).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Constraints}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Every schedule must satisfy the constraints in this section.

\subsection{Immediate Service}
\label{subsec:problem-service}

All service workloads must be executed immediately within their recorded hour. Their
spatial allocation across sites must satisfy the following:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\in\mathcal M}x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}
  =
  \sum_{m\in\mathcal M}s_{m,t},
  \qquad
  x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\geq0.
  \label{eq:service-conservation}
\end{equation}
Because both sides of the constraint are expressed in standardized work units, the
sum holds valid across sites regardless of differences in their individual computing
capacities. For example, at 22:00 UTC on 29 July 2025, the CAISO, MISO, SPP, and
ISO-NE sites have service workloads of 4,035.4, 3,579.7, 3,303.5, and 2,634.8 work
units. Equation~\eqref{eq:service-conservation} then requires the total, 13,553.4 work
units, to run somewhere in the fleet that hour. With no flexibility, each site runs its
own workload; a schedule may instead split the total among the four sites in any way
that fits their capacity.

% both 10% utilized, receive 1,000 and 100 work units; the equation requires

\subsection{Batch Queue and Deadlines}
\label{subsec:problem-batch}

The scheduler determines the amount of queued batch workloads to execute and
distributes them across sites with available compute capacity. Let \(z_t\) denote the
total batch release from the fleet, \(y_{i,t}\) the batch work released from site
\(i\)'s backlog, and \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\) the batch work executed at destination
site \(j\). The released and executed batch workloads satisfy the following:
\begin{equation}
  z_t
  =
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal M}y_{i,t}
  =
  \sum_{j\in\mathcal M}x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t},
  \qquad
  x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\geq0.
  \label{eq:batch-total}
\end{equation}
Let \(B_{i,t}\) represent site \(i\)'s \emph{batch work backlog} at the beginning of
hour \(t\). The batch workload \(q_{i,t}\) from
Equation~\eqref{eq:synthetic-batch-arrivals} increases the backlog, while the batch
release \(y_{i,t}\) reduces it. Furthermore, released batch work cannot exceed the
available work. The following constraints apply:
\begin{align}
  B_{i,t+1}
  &=
  B_{i,t}+q_{i,t}-y_{i,t},
  \label{eq:queue-balance}\\
  0
  &\leq y_{i,t}\leq B_{i,t}+q_{i,t}.
  \label{eq:queue-availability}
\end{align}
The \emph{completion window} \(H\) is the number of hourly slots in which batch work
may execute, including its entry slot. For \(H=24\), new batch work may execute
immediately or during any of the following 23 hourly slots, provided these hours fall
within the current month. Setting \(H=24\) for flexible workloads aligns with the
convention of Google's carbon-aware system \cite{radovanovic2021carbonaware} as well
as the batch model proposed by Haghshenas \emph{et al.}
\cite{haghshenas2022infrastructure}. Note that \(H\) is a modeling assumption rather
than an empirical measurement, as the trace records when work actually executed, not
when it was due.

Batch work entering in the \emph{entry slot} \(\nu\) must therefore be executed by its
\emph{deadline slot}
\begin{equation}
  d_{\nu}
  =
  \min\{\nu+H-1,\;T_e-1\}.
  \label{eq:batch-deadline}
\end{equation}
Releases follow a global earliest-deadline-first queue. All queued batch work across
all sites is maintained in a unified list sorted by deadline slot. Each hour, the
batch release \(z_t\) is dispatched from the head of the queue, prioritizing work with
the earliest deadline regardless of its origin. Ties in deadline slots are broken
first by origin site index in ascending order, and then by entry order.

For each site, cumulative
execution of batch work through hour \(t\) must meet
or exceed all batch work due by that hour:
% [symbols 2026-10-09] was: summed over the hours \(\rho\) up to \(t\),
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\rho=0}^{t}y_{i,\rho}
  \geq
  \sum_{\substack{\nu\in\mathcal T_e\\d_{\nu}\leq t}}q_{i,\nu},
  \qquad i\in\mathcal M,\ t\in\mathcal T_e.
  \label{eq:deadline-feasibility}
\end{equation}
In words, by the end of hour \(t\), site \(i\) must have released at least as much
batch work from its backlog as the total volume of work due by \(t\). The sum on the
right covers every entry slot \(\nu\) whose deadline slot \(d_\nu\) is at or before
hour \(t\). For example,
with \(H=24\), batch work entering in hour 7 may execute during any of hours 7 through
30. Thus, by the end of hour 30, all batch work from hour 7 must be completed.
% [review 2026-10-10] dropped the terminal-queue equation B_{i,T_e} = 0
% (eq:terminal-queue); it follows from the equation above at t = T_e - 1.
Furthermore, because all deadlines are capped at the final hour of the month,
evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:deadline-feasibility} at \(t=T_e-1\) ensures that every site
backlog is completely empty by month-end. Without this cap, the batch work of the final
day could be deferred past the last evaluated hour, reducing the fleet's power demand
without ever being executed.

\subsection{Capacity and Site Power}
\label{subsec:problem-feasibility}

The total \emph{executed work} \(w_{m,t}\), combining service and batch execution, cannot
exceed the site's hourly compute capacity:
\begin{align}
  w_{m,t}
  &=
  x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}
  +x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t},
  \label{eq:executed-work}\\
  0
  &\leq w_{m,t}\leq C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m.
  \label{eq:site-capacity}
\end{align}
% [no-K 2026-10-10] was: w <= K_m; reference power 500 K_m MW; ratio w/K_m.
Executed work is converted to power using the model from
Section~\ref{sec:powerdata2019} together with the site's rated power
\(\overline P_m\) (Section~\ref{subsec:transform-mapping}). With
\(w_{m,t}/C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\) representing the site's CPU utilization, the
\emph{site power} \(P_{m,t}\) is given by:
\begin{equation}
  P_{m,t}
  =
  \overline P_m
  \left(
    \alpha_m+\beta_m\frac{w_{m,t}}{C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m}
  \right)
  \quad\mathrm{MW}.
  \label{eq:runtime-power}
\end{equation}

Work may execute at any site with available capacity. This study does not account for
potential inter-site transfer constraints such as latency, bandwidth limit,
data-residency restrictions, energy overhead, or data movement costs. Consequently,
the formulation provides an optimistic upper bound on geographic flexibility.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:problem-objectives}

\subsection{Net-Load Ramps and Peaks with the Fleet}
\label{subsec:problem-objective}

% [two-goals 2026-10-10] added the opening sentence and "For the first goal,"
% so that "The second goal" below has something to refer to.
The scheduler pursues two primary objectives: smoothing hourly net load ramps within
each market and reducing each market's monthly peak net load. Both objectives are
evaluated on the \emph{adjusted net load} \(A_{m,t}\), defined as the market's
baseline net load augmented by the site power:
\begin{equation}
  A_{m,t}
  =
  N_{m,t}+P_{m,t}.
  \label{eq:adjusted-net-load}
\end{equation}
% [nf-reference 2026-11-05] Both impacts are now measured from the no-flexibility
% baseline instead of the grid without the fleet, after Dr. Wang's comment on the
% ramp equations: "it would make more sense if the data center power is included
% in N". J_e(mu_NF) is therefore zero, and the separate improvement equation is gone.
A \emph{scheduling policy} \(\mu\) dictates the control decisions for each hour, so the
adjusted net load depends on \(\mu\). Both objectives are measured from the
\emph{no-flexibility baseline} \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\), the policy that executes all work
immediately at its original site. Because the fleet is present under both policies, the
comparison isolates the effect of flexibility from that of the fleet itself.

For the first objective, the change in adjusted net load between consecutive hours is
evaluated. This one-hour interval aligns with the temporal resolution of the EIA power
grid data and the scheduling decisions. Normalizing by \(S_m\) expresses each one-hour
change as a fraction of the market's nominal hourly demand scale, yielding the
\emph{fleet-adjusted grid power ramp} \(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}\):
\begin{equation}
  \Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu)
  =
  \frac{A_{m,t}(\mu)-A_{m,t-1}(\mu)}{S_m}.
  \label{eq:adjusted-ramp}
\end{equation}
The policy's impact on net load ramp, the \emph{ramp impact} \(I_{m,t}\), is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
  I_{m,t}(\mu)
  =
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu)\right)^2
  -
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})\right)^2.
  \label{eq:incremental-impact}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:incremental-impact}, the power ramps are squared to penalize large
fluctuations and treat upward and downward ramps equally. A negative value of
\(I_{m,t}\) indicates that the policy reduces the power ramp relative to the
no-flexibility baseline, whereas a positive value indicates an increase.

% [peaks 2026-10-10] was: separate equations for the adjusted peak
% Q^adjusted_{m,e}(mu) = max_t A_{m,t}(mu)/S_m (eq:monthly-peak) and the original
% peak Q^original_{m,e} = max_t N_{m,t}/S_m (eq:monthly-original-peak), then
% Phi = Q^adjusted - Q^original.
The second objective is to reduce each market's monthly peak net load. As with ramps,
the policy's impact is evaluated relative to the no-flexibility baseline. The policy's
impact on peak power, the \emph{peak impact} \(\Phi_{m,e}(\mu)\), in month \(e\) is
defined as the difference between the monthly peak adjusted net load under \(\mu\) (the
\emph{adjusted peak}) and under \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\) (the \emph{no-flexibility peak}),
divided by \(S_m\):
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{m,e}(\mu)
  =
  \frac{\displaystyle\max_{t\in\mathcal T_e} A_{m,t}(\mu)
        -\max_{t\in\mathcal T_e} A_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})}{S_m}.
  \label{eq:peak-impact}
\end{equation}
A negative value of \(\Phi_{m,e}\) indicates that the policy lowers the monthly peak
relative to the no-flexibility baseline.

\subsection{Joint Objective}
\label{subsec:problem-normalization}

Because the impacts on power ramp and monthly peak have different numerical scales,
combining them into a single objective function requires normalizing each metric by a
fixed positive reference value. Based on the no-flexibility baseline, the reference
values for the net load ramp impact \(C_R\) and monthly peak power impact \(C_Q\) are
defined as:
\begin{align}
  C_R
  &=
  \frac{1}{|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|}
  \sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}}
  \sum_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M}
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})\right)^2,
  \label{eq:ramp-reference}\\
  C_Q
  &=
  \frac{1}{|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|}
  \sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M}
  \max_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\frac{A_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})}{S_m}.
  % [peaks 2026-10-10] was: Q^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,e}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})
  \label{eq:peak-reference}
\end{align}
where the \emph{training set} \(\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}\) is the set of months in
the training dataset and \(|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|\) is their number.

\(C_R\) and \(C_Q\) represent the average monthly squared ramps and the
average monthly normalized peak power across all markets, respectively. After
cross-validation, the final reference values are computed from all twelve months of
2025, resulting in \(C_R=6.102\) and \(C_Q=3.654\). During cross-validation, each
iteration computes its own values from its nine training months, with \(C_R\) from
5.853 to 6.613 and \(C_Q\) from 3.576 to 3.739. The final references are subsequently
applied to scale the test month, ensuring zero data leakage from the out-of-sample
evaluation period. 

Each simulated month \(e\) is a distinct optimization episode. The joint objective is
to identify a scheduling policy that minimizes the equally weighted sum of the scaled
monthly net load ramp impact and the scaled monthly peak power impact. Defining
\(J_e(\mu)\) as the joint objective, or \emph{joint score}, and \(\mathfrak M\) as the
\emph{feasible policy set} satisfying
Equations~\eqref{eq:service-conservation}--\eqref{eq:site-capacity}, the optimization
problem is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    &\min_{\mu\in\mathfrak M} J_e(\mu),\\
    &J_e(\mu)
    =
    \frac{0.5}{C_R}
    \sum_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M} I_{m,t}(\mu)\\
    &\hspace{2em}
    +\frac{0.5}{C_Q}
    \sum_{m\in\mathcal M} \Phi_{m,e}(\mu).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
subject to the constraints in
Equations~\eqref{eq:service-conservation}--\eqref{eq:site-capacity}.
The 0.5 weights apply after dividing each term by its reference scale.
A lower joint score need not improve both components. Because both impacts are measured
from \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\), \(J_e(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})=0\): a negative joint score indicates
that the policy outperforms the no-flexibility baseline, and a positive score indicates
that it performs worse.

\subsection{Evaluation and Formal Problem Statement}
\label{subsec:problem-evaluation}

The scheduler and \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\) are evaluated using the same workloads, power
models, grid data, forecasts, and month. The ramp-impact and peak components of the joint
score are reported separately, along with each market's adjusted peak in MW and its
reduction from the no-flexibility peak. Because site power is nonnegative, a peak below
the no-flexibility peak can still lie above the \emph{baseline peak}, the monthly peak of
the baseline net load without the fleet.

The full problem can now be stated.
\label{subsec:problem-formal-statement}

\begin{quote}
Choose a causal policy for hourly service destinations, batch release
volumes, and batch destinations to minimize the joint monthly ramp-impact and
net-load peak score in Equation~\eqref{eq:objective}. Use only the
information available at each decision and meet the service, deadline, and
capacity constraints above.
\end{quote}

\section{Reinforcement Learning Overview}
\label{sec:method-rl}

Sutton and Barto, in \emph{Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction}, describe
\emph{reinforcement learning} as learning how to map situations to actions so as to
maximize a numerical reward. The learner is not told which action is correct and must
discover it through trial \cite[Sec.~1.1]{sutton2018reinforcement}. They identify two
defining features: trial-and-error learning and delayed reward, because an action
affects not only the immediate reward but also subsequent situations and rewards
\cite[Sec.~1.1]{sutton2018reinforcement}. In this study, reinforcement learning is used
to identify a scheduling policy for the problem of Chapter~\ref{chap:problem}. As a
real-time scheduler must, the policy acts each hour using only the information available
at that hour (Section~\ref{subsec:problem-causality}).

Reinforcement learning involves an \emph{agent}, the learner and decision maker, that
interacts with an \emph{environment}, everything outside the agent, over a sequence of
time steps. At each step, the agent selects an \emph{action}, and the environment
responds with a new situation and a numerical \emph{reward}
\cite[Sec.~3.1]{sutton2018reinforcement}. Table~\ref{tab:rl-terms} lists the meaning of
each term in this study. Anything the agent cannot change arbitrarily belongs to the
environment. Accordingly, the rules that enforce the constraints and the reward
calculation are both part of the environment \cite[Sec.~3.1]{sutton2018reinforcement}.

% sections, and the word "decoder", which is not yet defined here.

One hour of a simulated month illustrates how these terms fit together. The
scheduler, as the agent, reads the observation: the grid data of the previous hour,
the forecasts issued at that hour, the current workloads and queues, and other
entries. The scheduler then selects an action. The environment converts the action into
a schedule that satisfies every constraint, calculates each site's power, reveals the
hour's net load, and returns the hourly reward. The next hour begins from the
resulting queues and site power. After the final hour of the month, the episode ends
with every queue empty, and the hourly rewards sum to the month's \emph{return}, the
joint score with its sign reversed, \(-J_e\). Therefore, a schedule with a lower joint
score earns a higher return.

A \emph{policy} is defined as a mapping from what the agent observes to the probabilities of
selecting each action, and a reinforcement learning method specifies how the policy
changes with experience \cite[Sec.~3.5]{sutton2018reinforcement}. In this study, a small
neural network represents the policy, and PPO updates it. The policy
changes only during training on the training months. Once training ends, the policy is
held constant and evaluated on months not used for training.

\subsection{Actions and the Feasibility Decoder}
\label{sec:method-actions}

% [ch567 2026-11-05] the softmax and release fraction are now equations, with the
% decoder's request symbols; the 2.718 explanation and the repeat of Section 5.1 are
% gone, the example uses the real workloads of Section 4.4.1, and the paragraph on the
% bound of +-6 moved to Appendix C.
The policy does not select amounts of work directly. Instead, it outputs nine
\emph{preferences}, each a number between \(-6\) and 6, and a fixed set of rules, the
\emph{decoder}, converts any nine preferences into a schedule that satisfies every
constraint. The decoder is not learned and is identical for every policy. The nine
preferences serve three roles:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Four \emph{service preferences} \(a^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\), one per site, request
        where the hour's service executes.
  \item One \emph{release preference} \(a^{\mathrm{rel}}_t\) requests how much of the
        queued batch work executes in the hour.
  \item Four \emph{destination preferences} \(a^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\), one per site,
        request where the released batch work executes.
\end{itemize}

The service preferences determine the \emph{requested service work}
\(\hat x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\) at each site through a softmax
\cite[Sec.~13.1]{sutton2018reinforcement}, which divides the fleet's total service
workload among the sites:
\begin{equation}
  \hat x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}
  =
  \frac{\exp\bigl(a^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\bigr)}
       {\sum_{j'\in\mathcal M}\exp\bigl(a^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j',t}\bigr)}
  \sum_{m\in\mathcal M}s_{m,t}.
  \label{eq:service-request}
\end{equation}
Consequently, only the differences between the four preferences matter. Equal values
yield equal shares, a preference 1 higher than another requests approximately 2.7 times
its share, and a preference six or more below the others requests almost nothing.

The release preference determines the \emph{requested batch release} \(\hat z_t\) as a
fraction of the queued batch work:
\begin{equation}
  \hat z_t
  =
  \frac{a^{\mathrm{rel}}_t+6}{12}
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal M}\bigl(B_{i,t}+q_{i,t}\bigr).
  \label{eq:release-request}
\end{equation}
A preference of \(-6\) requests nothing, 0 requests half of the queue, and \(+6\) requests
the entire queue. The decoder obtains the total batch release \(z_t\) from this request,
raising it when queued work would otherwise miss its deadline and reducing it when the
capacity remaining after service is insufficient. The destination preferences then
determine the \emph{requested batch work} \(\hat x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\) at each site
through a second softmax:
\begin{equation}
  \hat x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}
  =
  \frac{\exp\bigl(a^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\bigr)}
       {\sum_{j'\in\mathcal M}\exp\bigl(a^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j',t}\bigr)}
  \,z_t.
  \label{eq:destination-request}
\end{equation}
If a request exceeds the capacity available at a site, the decoder caps that site and
reallocates the excess to sites with available capacity, which yields the executed work
\(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\) and \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\). The released work is always the
work with the earliest deadline, regardless of its origin
(Section~\ref{subsec:problem-batch}).

Because every set of preferences yields a schedule that satisfies the constraints, the
policy can explore any action during training without violating one, and the reward
requires no penalties. The decoder never considers net load, the forecasts, or the
objective. Consequently, the decoder only enforces feasibility, and the choice among
feasible schedules originates from the policy.

For example, suppose that at 22:00 UTC on 29 July 2025, the hour whose service workloads
are given in Section~\ref{subsec:problem-service}, the policy outputs
\[
  (0.5,\;0,\;0,\;-6 \;\mid\; 3 \;\mid\; 0,\;0,\;0,\;0)
\]
for CAISO, MISO, SPP, and ISO-NE, in that order. Equation~\eqref{eq:service-request} then
requests 6,120, 3,712, 3,712, and 9 of the 13,553 work units of service, and nearly all of
ISO-NE's service therefore moves to the other three sites.
Equation~\eqref{eq:release-request} requests \((3+6)/12=0.75\) of the queued batch work, and
the equal destination preferences divide the released work evenly across the sites, within
the capacity remaining after service.

The no-flexibility baseline can be expressed as one of these actions. Its service
preferences are the logarithms of each site's share of the hour's service workload, and
Equation~\eqref{eq:service-request} therefore returns exactly those shares. Its release
preference is \(+6\), which executes all queued batch work immediately, and its
destination preferences return each site's batch work to its origin.

\subsection{Reward}
\label{sec:method-reward}

In each hour, the environment returns a reward: the hour's share of the joint score of
Equation~\eqref{eq:objective}, with its sign reversed so that a lower score earns a
higher reward:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    r_t
    ={}&-\frac{0.5}{C_R}\sum_m I_{m,t}\\
    &-\frac{0.5}{C_Q}\sum_m\left[
      \left(M_{m,t}-M_{m,t-1}\right)
      -\left(M^{\mathrm{NF}}_{m,t}-M^{\mathrm{NF}}_{m,t-1}\right)
    \right],
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where the \emph{hourly reward} \(r_t\) denotes the reward of hour \(t\) and
\(I_{m,t}\) denotes the ramp impact of Equation~\eqref{eq:incremental-impact}. The
\emph{running adjusted peak} \(M_{m,t}\) and \emph{running no-flexibility peak}
\(M^{\mathrm{NF}}_{m,t}\) represent the highest adjusted net loads of the month so far under
\(\mu\) and under \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\), respectively, divided by \(S_m\). The first line
penalizes the ramp of the hour. The second line applies only when the hour sets a new
monthly peak: any increase in the running adjusted peak counts against the hour, less any
increase in the running no-flexibility peak during the same hour. Each running peak begins
in the first decision hour of the month, with its first increase measured from zero.

The rewards of a month sum exactly to \(-J_e\). The ramp terms sum to the month's ramp
impact. Because the increases in a running peak sum to its final value, the peak terms
sum to the month's peak impact. The no-flexibility baseline earns zero reward in every
hour. Therefore, a positive hourly reward indicates that the policy outperformed the
baseline in that hour.

\section{Training with PPO}
\label{sec:method-ppo}

\subsection{Algorithm}
\label{subsec:method-algorithm}

% [ch567 2026-11-05] dropped the four-step list (its library steps are now described
% with the library calls in Appendix C) and moved the paragraph on trained behavior and
% the noise standard deviations to Section 6.1, since those are results.
The policy is trained using proximal policy optimization (PPO), a standard reinforcement
learning method for continuous actions such as the nine preferences
\cite{schulman2017ppo}. This study uses the implementation in the Stable-Baselines3 library
\cite{raffin2021sb3}. PPO trains two neural networks simultaneously: the policy, which
selects actions, and the \emph{critic}, which estimates the future reward that each
situation typically yields. The critic is used only during training, to assess whether each
action performed better or worse than expected \cite[Sec.~13.5]{sutton2018reinforcement}.

In each hour, the policy network receives the observation and outputs a mean for each of
the nine preferences. During training, PPO adds Gaussian \emph{exploration noise} to each
mean, so that the policy tries different actions and can identify those that perform
better; any result outside \([-6,6]\) is clipped to the nearest bound. The standard deviation
of the noise is learned separately for each preference, starting at 1. During evaluation,
the noise is switched off. Because the outputs of an untrained network are close to zero,
an untrained policy distributes service evenly across the four sites and requests
approximately half of the queued batch work each hour, regardless of grid conditions.

Training proceeds in \emph{rollouts}. In each rollout, the policy acts for 2,048 time steps
with exploration noise. The critic's estimates are then used to assess each action as better
or worse than expected. The policy is updated so that actions that performed better become
more likely in similar situations and those that performed worse become less likely. PPO
limits how far each rollout can change the policy, and
Appendix~\ref{subsec:ppo-details} gives the update equations. A run of 1,024,000 time steps
comprises 500 rollouts.

\subsection{Normalization and Discounting}
\label{subsec:method-normalization}

% [ch567 2026-11-05] merged with Section 5.2.1, so normalization is explained once; the
% neural-network paragraph became one sentence, the example table moved to Appendix C,
% and 0.99^168 is 0.18 (was misprinted 0.19).
The 82 entries of the observation differ by several orders of magnitude: net load reaches
tens of thousands of MW, whereas the sine of the hour remains below one. Because a neural
network updates its parameters in small steps, large inputs would initially dominate its
outputs, and many updates would pass before small inputs could contribute. Accordingly, the
guide to the library states that agent inputs should always be normalized \cite{sb3tips}.
Each entry is therefore standardized with running statistics and clipped:
\begin{equation}
  o=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{v-\bar v}{\sigma_v},\,-10,\,10\right),
  \label{eq:observation-normalization}
\end{equation}
where \(v\) denotes an entry of the observation in its own unit, \(\bar v\) and \(\sigma_v\)
denote the running mean and standard deviation of that entry over the training hours so far,
and \(o\) denotes the \emph{normalized entry} that the policy receives.

PPO, like most reinforcement learning methods, also discounts future rewards. Training
maximizes the \emph{discounted return} \(G_t\) rather than the total reward of the month:
\begin{equation}
  G_t=\sum_{l=0}^{T_e-1-t}\gamma^{l}\,r_{t+l},
  \label{eq:discounted-return}
\end{equation}
where the \emph{discount factor} \(\gamma\) equals 0.99
\cite[Sec.~3.3]{sutton2018reinforcement}. Consequently, a reward one day ahead counts 0.79
as much as an immediate reward, one week ahead 0.18, and one at the end of a month almost
nothing. In contrast, the joint score weights every hour of the month equally.

Each reward is normalized as well, so that its magnitude remains in a range suited to
training:
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde r_t=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{r_t}{\sigma_r},\,-10,\,10\right),
  \label{eq:reward-normalization}
\end{equation}
where \(\widetilde r_t\) denotes the \emph{normalized reward} and \(\sigma_r\) denotes the running
standard deviation of a discounted sum of the rewards. Training on discounted, normalized
rewards is therefore an approximation of the monthly problem. During evaluation, the running
statistics are frozen at their end-of-training values, and every reported result uses the
original rewards and the undiscounted joint score.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:method-evaluation}

\subsection{Training, Validation, and Test Months}
\label{sec:method-cv}

% [wwm 2026-11-01] moved from Chapter 3 (was Section 3.6, "Cross-Validation and
% Test Data") and rewritten, at Dr. Wang's request. A fold is a group of three
% months; an iteration is one pass that holds out one fold.
A policy must be evaluated on months not used for training, because strong performance on
its training months could simply indicate that it has memorized them. The data are organized
by month because each month forms a continuous simulation
(Section~\ref{subsec:transform-pairing}), where queued batch work carries from
hour to hour and each electrical power ramp connects adjacent hours. Two groups
of months are used:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \emph{training and validation data} are the twelve months of 2025,
        processed using the training fleet (cells \(a\)--\(d\)). All design
        choices, such as the forecast inputs, and all values computed from the
        data, such as the market demand scales, come from these months.
  \item The \emph{test month} is May 2026, processed using the test fleet (cells
        \(e\)--\(h\), Section~\ref{subsec:transform-mapping}). This month is
        used because it aligns with the calendar month of the Google trace and
        lies one year past the 2025 data. It is evaluated only after every
        choice has been finalized.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Cross-validation} is applied to the 2025 months for two purposes. It
estimates how well a trained policy performs on months it was not trained on,
and it allows design choices, such as the forecast inputs, to be compared
without using the test month. It is not used to search over the PPO settings of
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}.

In \(k\)-fold cross-validation, the data are divided into \(k\) equal parts, or
folds. A model is trained on \(k-1\) folds and evaluated on the remaining fold.
This process is repeated until every fold has served as the validation set once,
and the \(k\) resulting scores are averaged. Each pass through this process is
one \emph{iteration}. Every month is used for validation exactly once; therefore,
the performance estimate does not depend on a single validation split.

Here \(k=4\), with each fold comprising three whole months. Folds are
constructed by selecting one month from each four-month block of the year,
thereby ensuring that the nine training months per iteration mirror the
four-season distribution of the complete dataset.
Table~\ref{tab:data-splits} lists the four iterations.

Each iteration uses only its nine training months for anything computed from the
data: the forecast coefficients (Section~\ref{subsec:energy-forecasts}), the
market demand scales \(S_m\) (Section~\ref{subsec:energy-market-scale}), and the
objective references \(C_R\) and \(C_Q\)
(Section~\ref{subsec:problem-normalization}). The scheduler is then trained on these
nine months and evaluated on the three validation months. The \emph{cross-validation
estimate} is the mean joint score over the twelve months.

Three runs are trained in each iteration, which yields twelve policies and three
evaluations of every 2025 month, each by a policy not trained on that month. The runs
differ only in their \emph{seed}, the starting number of the random-number generator,
which determines the initial network parameters, the exploration noise, the order of the
minibatches, and the order of the training months. Because data, constraints, and settings
remain constant within an iteration, differences between runs reflect training randomness.
\emph{Run 1} denotes the first run of every iteration: four policies, one per iteration,
that together evaluate all twelve months. Runs 2 and 3 are defined in the same way.

% look-back that the rule does not cover stays.
Because the validation months sit between training months, a training month can still read
values from a validation month. For example, iteration 1 validates on May and trains on
June, and June's warm-up hour and first-week forecast inputs use May's values. These values
serve only as inputs: no May hour is evaluated during training, and no May hour is used to
fit the forecast regression (Section~\ref{subsec:forecast-fitting}).

Once every choice has been finalized, ten new policies are trained on all twelve months of
2025, and these
\emph{final policies} are evaluated on the test month. No choice in the method is made using
the test month; therefore, it remains out of sample.

The test month differs from the 2025 months in three ways: its grid data are a year later,
its workload comes from cells \(e\)--\(h\) rather than \(a\)--\(d\), and its fleet is larger,
2,398 MW against 1,869 MW (Section~\ref{subsec:transform-mapping}). Because every 2025 month
replays the same 744-hour workload of cells \(a\)--\(d\), cross-validation evaluates new grid
months with a familiar workload, whereas the test month also introduces an unseen workload
and an unseen fleet.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:method-protocol}

% [ch567 2026-11-05] the violations sentence moved to Section 6.2.1; cut the orphan
% "The forecast lead times were set earlier in development." and the pointer to Chapter 6.
Each checkpoint is evaluated on each of its iteration's three validation months, one
continuous episode per month of 672 to 744 hours, with the exploration noise switched off and
the normalization statistics frozen. The final policies are evaluated in the same way on the
test month.

The joint score of Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} is reported for every evaluated month and
run, together with its weighted ramp and peak parts. Two measures are reported for each
market. The first is the change in the total squared one-hour ramp relative to the
\emph{baseline grid}, the grid without the fleet. The second is the change in the monthly
peak, in MW, relative to the no-flexibility baseline. Negative values of both measures
denote reductions.

Percentage reductions relative to the no-flexibility baseline are also reported for three
quantities: total squared normalized ramps, the sum of the markets' normalized peaks, and the
\emph{fleet-added peak}. The fleet-added peak is defined as the amount by which the fleet
raises each market's adjusted peak above its baseline peak, divided by \(S_m\) and summed over
the markets. Each percentage divides the reduction by the value under the no-flexibility
baseline.

The test score represents the final measure of the scheduler. Because it covers only one month, the
twelve validation months indicate how much the result varies from month to month.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion-findings}

Section~\ref{sec:intro-questions} asked how much a causal scheduler can smooth each market's
one-hour net-load ramps and reduce its monthly peak by deferring and routing work within the
service, deadline, and capacity limits. For the fleet modeled here, the gain appears in every
evaluated month but is modest in size. The cross-validation estimate of the joint score over the
twelve months of 2025 is \(-0.0188\). The mean joint score of the ten final policies on the May
2026 test month is \(-0.0168\), with every policy between \(-0.0191\) and \(-0.0142\).
This score is closer to zero than the cross-validation estimate, although it lies within the range
of the 2025 months, \(-0.0237\) to \(-0.0147\). One possible reason is that the test sites
are larger than the training sites: because the observation expresses site quantities in their own
units, the policies act on site inputs outside their training range
(Section~\ref{subsec:results-test-inputs}).

Most of the gain comes from ramps, which account for approximately 92\% of the joint score in both
cross-validation and the test month. Total squared normalized ramps fall by
3.6\% in cross-validation and 3.3\% in the test month. The markets' summed peaks fall
by approximately 0.3\%, which removes approximately 29\% of the fleet-added peak in
cross-validation and 22\% in the test month. The policies act primarily by routing service:
25\% to 33\% of each month's service work changes sites, whereas batch work
waits only 0.6 to 1.4 hours on average within its 24-hour window. Because no run
separates the two levers, this result describes what the policies do, not which lever produces the
gain.

The largest and smallest ramp reductions correspond to the fleet's size relative to each market's hourly
net-load ramps (Table~\ref{tab:native-ramp-scale}). ISO-NE, where a site's idle-to-peak power range is
approximately 60\% of the median hourly ramp, benefits the most relative to its market demand scale. The total squared ramp falls
8\% to 10\% below that of the baseline grid, and the peak ends 109 to
116 MW below the no-flexibility baseline. MISO, where a site's power range is 13\% to 23\%
of the median hourly ramp, changes little, and its peak rises in 20 of the 46 evaluations.
CAISO and SPP lie between. CAISO's test site has approximately 1.6 times the power range of its
training site, and CAISO's gains are larger in the test month: a 3.3\% ramp reduction against
2.7\%, and 115 MW off the peak against 91 MW. For a fleet operator, these
results indicate in which markets a fleet of this size can change the shape of net load, and by how
much.

The peak results are most readily interpreted in MW. In the test month, the fleet under the
no-flexibility baseline raises ISO-NE's monthly net-load peak by 428 MW, from 17,506 to 17,934 MW, and
CAISO's by 297 MW. The policies remove 109 MW of the ISO-NE increase and 115 MW of the
CAISO increase, representing 26\% and 39\% of what the fleet adds, respectively. In
SPP and MISO, the peak changes little: on average, it ends 6 MW lower in SPP and 28
MW lower in MISO than under the no-flexibility baseline. The fleet still raises every market's peak
above that of the baseline grid. Flexibility therefore reduces the data centers' contribution to the
peak rather than lowering the peak below the baseline grid's.

The gains are reported as changes in the modeled joint score and in MW rather than as cost or
reliability savings. Electricity prices, capacity charges, ramping products, and reserve requirements
are not modeled (Section~\ref{sec:intro-scope}), and the value of a lower peak or a smoother ramp
depends on each market's rules. Converting these changes into savings would require those rules.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The data impose four limitations. First, the workload comes from one company and one month in 2019,
and measured execution is reused as synthetic workload. Because every 2025 month replays the same
workload of cells \(a\)--\(d\), cross-validation tests new grid months but not new workload; only the
test month, with cells \(e\)--\(h\), tests unseen workload. Second, the trace is replayed from its
first day in every month, and its weekdays therefore do not align with those of the month: May 1, 2019
was a Wednesday, whereas February 2025, for example, starts on a Saturday. Third, batch work enters the
queue in the hour it executed in the trace rather than the hour it was submitted, and any delay already
imposed by Google's scheduler therefore remains in the workload. Fourth, one anomalous EIA value is
retained as published. CAISO's demand at 2025-07-31 20:00 UTC is 11,819 MW, compared with 29,881 MW and
29,886 MW in the preceding and subsequent hours. The resulting net load of \(-7{,}242\) MW introduces
two steep ramps of about 18,000 MW in July. An audit across all markets and both years found no other
single-hour anomaly of this kind.

The model rests on several assumptions. The service and batch classification, the completion window,
the 500 MW rated power of the site built from cell \(c\), the proportionality of power capacity to CPU
capacity, and the cell-to-market mapping are all modeling assumptions. The forecasts are simple
least-squares regressions trained on public EIA data. An operator would have access to system-operator
or commercial forecasts of different accuracy, and the scheduler's benefit may depend on that accuracy.
Routing remains free and instantaneous, which makes the available geographic flexibility optimistic. A
task running across several hours may also change sites between hours at no cost, whereas real running
tasks can be moved only at a cost, if at all.

The method and its evaluation carry three further limitations. The test covers only one month. In 2025,
the joint score of a single month ranged from \(-0.0237\) to \(-0.0147\)
(Table~\ref{tab:months}); therefore, the test month's \(-0.0168\) carries month-to-month variation of
about that size. Because the observation expresses each site's workloads, queued work, and power in
their own units, a policy trained on one fleet encounters inputs outside its training range when the
sites are larger, as in the test fleet (Section~\ref{subsec:results-test-inputs}). Finally, the PPO
settings are the library's defaults and were not tuned for this problem
(Section~\ref{sec:method-training}), and better settings may exist.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion-future}

Several extensions would examine the method. Ramp-only and peak-only policies would indicate how much
each objective drives the other's gains, and a variant in which service remains at its own site and only
batch work moves would indicate what deferral alone achieves. A simple forecast-aware rule, using the
same observations, workload constraints, and feasibility decoder, would indicate what learning adds
beyond flexible scheduling. Expressing each site's workloads, queued work, and power as fractions of its
capacity would make the observation independent of site size, which could allow policies trained on one
fleet to transfer to another. Tuned PPO settings, a normalized action range, or other learning algorithms
may also outperform the policies reported here.

Other extensions would relax the assumptions of the model. Routing costs, latency, and limits on workload
migration would make the geographic flexibility less optimistic. The trace's job records include
submission and scheduling events, which would allow the delay already imposed by Google's scheduler to be
measured. Additional test months, other years, and workloads from other cells or operators would test
whether the gains hold beyond one month of one company's workload. Finally, electricity prices or carbon
signals could be added to the objective alongside ramps and peaks, and cooling control or on-site
generation would provide the scheduler with additional levers.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion-conclusions}

A four-site data center fleet built from public Google and EIA data was used to test whether a learned
scheduler can smooth grid ramps and reduce monthly net-load peaks within its service, deadline, and
capacity limits. A PPO policy that routes service and defers batch work
outperformed the no-flexibility baseline in every month tested. The mean joint score was \(-0.0188\)
over the cross-validation months of 2025 and \(-0.0168\) in the test month, whose larger sites place
the policies' site inputs outside their training range. Most of the gain comes from ramps, and the gain
is largest where the fleet is large relative to the grid, as in ISO-NE. The fleet still adds to every
market's peak, but in the test month flexibility removes 26\% of that addition in ISO-NE and 39\% in
CAISO. Because routing is free and the workload comes from one month of one
company, these results indicate what such flexibility can achieve in an optimistic setting rather than
what a real fleet would save.

\section{Workload Extraction (Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019})}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \codepath{extract_cells.ipynb} runs the BigQuery queries in
        Google Colab or a local Jupyter kernel.
  \item The trace's tables are \codepath{machine_events} (the machine list),
        \codepath{instance_usage} (the task usage measurements), and
        \codepath{collection_events} (the job records). The fields used are
        \codepath{capacity.cpus}, a machine's CPU capacity on the scale of
        Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-capacity};
        \codepath{average_usage.cpus}, the average CPU used during one
        measurement, \(a_r\); \codepath{start_time} and
        \codepath{end_time}, the measurement window; and
        \codepath{alloc_collection_id}, the allocation set a task runs in,
        which is empty or 0 for tasks outside any set and for the reserved
        blocks themselves.
  \item The query's \codepath{cap} step, a common-table expression (a named
        sub-query), computes
        \(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:reference-capacity}) by
        taking \codepath{MAX(capacity.cpus)} for each \codepath{machine_id}
        and summing over machines. The capacity cell sums the same
        per-machine maxima, counting machines that report both a CPU and a
        memory capacity, and saves the result, with each cell's machine count, to
        \codepath{data/cell_capacity.json}, which sets the site sizes in
        Table~\ref{tab:active-market-map}.
  \item The usage query assigns each row to a 300-second bucket by
        \codepath{start_time}, sums \codepath{average_usage.cpus} times
        \codepath{end_time-start_time} as a share of 300 seconds, divides by
        \(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}\), and clips the result to \([0,1]\), a bound no
        bucket reaches. Its \codepath{WHERE} clause keeps rows whose
        \codepath{alloc_collection_id} is empty or 0, which drops tasks
        running inside an allocation set and keeps the blocks.
  \item The \codepath{prio} step takes, for each job
        (\codepath{collection_id}), the highest priority among its
        submission events, which have \codepath{type=0}. A left join keeps
        usage rows with no matching priority, and those count as service.
  \item \codepath{scripts/bigquery/all_usage_windows.sql} runs that extraction for
        all eight cells in one query, saved as
        \codepath{scripts/bigquery/results/usage_all_windows.csv}. It also counts the
        rows that are short, exceed 300 seconds, or cross a bucket boundary; the last
        two counts are zero.
  \item The notebook writes \codepath{data/cells/cell_a_tiers.csv} through
        \codepath{data/cells/cell_h_tiers.csv}. \codepath{TRAIN_CELLS} lists
        \(a\)--\(d\) and \codepath{TEST_CELLS} lists \(e\)--\(h\); setting
        \codepath{INCLUDE_TEST_CELLS = False} extracts only the training
        cells.
\end{itemize}

\section{Constraints (Sections~\ref{subsec:problem-decisions}--\ref{subsec:problem-feasibility})}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \codepath{project_action} in \codepath{env/ramp_v6/projection.py}
        turns each hourly action into \(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\), \(z_t\), and
        \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\) satisfying
        Equations~\eqref{eq:service-conservation}--\eqref{eq:site-capacity}.
        Section~\ref{sec:method-actions} describes the action encoding, and
        \codepath{action_schema} in \codepath{environment.py} names its nine
        preferences.
  \item The bound of \(\pm6\) on the preferences, set in the action space of
        \codepath{RampAwareEnv} and in \codepath{bounded_fraction}, is a fixed
        convention rather than a tuned value. It provides exact endpoints of 0 and 1
        for the release fraction and is wide enough for routing: a difference of 12
        between two preferences gives one site approximately
        \(e^{12}\approx160{,}000\) times the other's share, and wider bounds would
        therefore not materially change the routing. This range departs from the guide
        to the library, which recommends rescaling continuous actions to \([-1,1]\)
        \cite{sb3tips}; its cost is narrower early exploration.
  \item \codepath{project_capped_simplex} splits total service among
        destinations so that it is conserved and within capacity.
        \codepath{EDFQueue.drain} in \codepath{models.py} gives \(y_{i,t}\),
        and \codepath{exact_transport} pairs origins with destinations so both
        totals match.
  \item \codepath{EDFQueue} keeps the backlog ledger, and after every hour
        \codepath{step} checks that entered work equals completed plus queued
        work. \codepath{_load_current_arrivals} stores each hour's batch work with its
        first late slot, \(\min\{\nu+H,\;T_e\}\), one past \(d_\nu\).
  \item \codepath{project_action} raises the release to cover both the work due
        now and, for each later deadline, the work due by then minus
        \codepath{_guaranteed_future_batch_capacity_by_deadline}. That bound
        assumes future service could reach the month's largest hourly workload
        total, so releases are sometimes earlier than strictly needed. In the
        final slot it releases the whole queue.
  \item \codepath{step} stops the simulation if due work remains, a site
        exceeds capacity, or work is left at month-end.
        \codepath{tests/ramp_v6/test_continuous_month.py} and
        \codepath{tests/ramp_v6/test_ramp_math.py} test the window, drain order,
        and deadline guard.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{PPO POLICY UPDATES}
\label{subsec:ppo-details}

\section{Policy Updates in Words}

After each rollout of 2,048 time steps, the library assesses each action as better or worse
than expected and then updates the policy and the critic. The two steps are as follows, each
with the library setting that controls it.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Assess each action.} For each of the 2,048 time steps, the library compares
        what followed the action with what the critic expected from that situation. What
        followed is the hour's reward plus the rewards of subsequent hours, with each later
        hour weighted slightly less than the one preceding it. The difference is the action's
        \emph{advantage}: positive if the action performed better than expected, and negative
        if it performed worse. The library calculates it using \emph{generalized advantage
        estimation}, a standard method of blending the critic's estimates with the rewards
        actually received \cite{schulman2016gae}, controlled by \texttt{gae\_lambda=0.95}, the
        library's default. Looking ahead stops at the end of the rollout or at the end of
        the month, whichever comes first. If the month has not ended, the critic's estimate
        represents the remaining hours; if it has, nothing follows.
  \item \emph{Update the policy and the critic.} The library then processes the 2,048 time
        steps ten times (\texttt{n\_epochs=10}), each time in random minibatches of 256 time
        steps (\texttt{batch\_size=256}), yielding 80 gradient steps in total. Each gradient
        step makes actions with a positive advantage more likely in similar situations and actions
        with a negative advantage less likely, and moves the critic's estimates closer to the
        rewards that actually followed. PPO is distinguished by a limit on how strongly each
        rollout can change the policy: once an action has become approximately 20\% more or less
        likely than at the start of the rollout (\texttt{clip\_range=0.2}), further changes earn
        nothing \cite{schulman2017ppo}. This prevents one rollout of experience from changing the
        policy too far. It is not a hard cap, since the policy can still move further, but
        nothing in the rollout rewards it for doing so.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Update Equations}

The policy \(\pi_\theta(a\mid o)\) describes the distribution of sampled actions;
\(V_\phi(o)\) estimates discounted training returns. Here \(o_t\) is the
normalized observation and \(\widetilde r_t\) is the normalized and clipped
training reward. Generalized advantage estimation uses
\begin{align}
  \delta_t
  &=\widetilde r_t+\gamma V_\phi(o_{t+1})-V_\phi(o_t),
  \label{eq:td-error}\\
  \hat A_t
  &=\sum_{l=0}^{K_t-1}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l},
  \label{eq:gae}
\end{align}
where \(K_t\) is the number of collected steps from \(t\) to the earlier of
the rollout boundary or month-end. At month-end the next-state value is
zero. At an unfinished rollout boundary it supplies the bootstrap value.
Every run uses \(\gamma=0.99\) and \(\lambda=0.95\).

Advantages are standardized within each minibatch before the policy update.
For the sampled action \(a_t\), define
\(\rho_t(\theta)=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)/
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid o_t)\). PPO maximizes
\begin{equation}
  L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta)
  =\mathbb E_t\!\left[
    \min\!\left(
      \rho_t\hat A_t,\;
      \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t
    \right)
  \right],
  \label{eq:ppo-clip}
\end{equation}
with \(\epsilon=0.2\). The probability ratio uses the sampled Gaussian
action; the environment receives its clipped version. Clipping limits the
incentive for large policy changes; it does not enforce a bound on the
policy itself.

The critic fits estimated returns with squared error, weighted 0.5
in the combined loss. There is no entropy bonus, and gradients are clipped
to norm 0.5. These settings are listed in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}.
