\begin{abstract}
Large data centers consume enough electrical power that the timing and location of their
workloads affect the power grids that serve them. This study examines how much a fleet operator
can smooth each market's hourly net-load ramps and reduce its monthly net-load peak by deferring
batch work and routing work between sites, within service, deadline, and capacity limits. A
synthetic fleet of four data centers is constructed from Google's ClusterData2019 workload trace
and PowerData2019 power measurements. Its grid data are the hourly demand, wind, and solar
generation of four U.S. markets (CAISO, MISO, SPP, and ISO-NE) from the Energy Information
Administration. A joint objective combines the squared one-hour net-load ramps and
the monthly net-load peaks of the four markets, both measured from a no-flexibility baseline that
executes all work where and when it was recorded. A scheduler trained with proximal policy
optimization uses only the information available at each hour, including least-squares forecasts
of demand and net load. The scheduler is evaluated by four-fold cross-validation over the twelve
months of 2025 and on May 2026, a test month served by a second fleet built from different Google
cells. The trained policies outperform the no-flexibility baseline in every evaluated month. Total
squared one-hour ramps fall by 3.6\% in cross-validation and 3.3\% in the test month, and ISO-NE's
average hourly ramp by 27~MW and 17~MW. In the test
month, ISO-NE's monthly peak falls by 109 MW and CAISO's by 115 MW, which correspond to 64\% and
91\% of the most that any schedule could remove, given the sites' idle power. Because routing is
assumed to be free and instantaneous, these results represent an optimistic setting for geographic
flexibility.

\noindent Keywords: data centers, demand response, grid ramp, net-load peak,
reinforcement learning, workload scheduling
\end{abstract}

\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:intro-background}

A data center is a facility that houses information and communication technology (ICT)
equipment, such as servers, telecommunications, and storage systems, together with the power
delivery and cooling infrastructure that supports it \cite{vasques2019review}. Over the past
decade, cloud computing has moved most of the applications and data that users once kept on their
own machines into data centers \cite{katal2023energy}. The largest of these facilities are known as
hyperscale data centers, and those under construction in the United States for artificial
intelligence (AI) range from 600~MW to 5~GW in capacity \cite{iea2026energyai}.

According to the International Energy Agency (IEA) \cite{iea2026energyai}, global data center
electricity consumption grew by more than 15\% between 2024 and 2025, reaching nearly
500 terawatt-hours (TWh), or slightly more than 1.5\% of global annual electricity demand. The
IEA projects this demand to roughly double by 2030, with electricity use by AI-focused data
centers growing much faster than the overall sector. Approximately half of the world's installed
data center capacity is located in the United States \cite{iea2026energyai}.

Large hyperscale data centers consume enough electrical power that the timing and geographic
location of their computing workloads affect the power grids serving them. For a grid operator,
the sheer magnitude of this load is not the only concern. Power system operators must
continuously balance the \emph{net load}, the residual electricity demand remaining after
variable wind and solar generation is subtracted, as it ramps from hour to hour and rises toward
its peaks. These net-load ramps become substantially steeper as wind and solar penetration
increases \cite{lannoye2012assessment,eia2023duckcurve}. Consequently, a large flexible load that
shifts when and where it draws power directly reshapes both net-load ramps and peaks.

\section{Workload Flexibility}
\label{sec:intro-flexibility}

In data centers, computing workloads differ fundamentally in their scheduling flexibility:
latency-sensitive tasks must execute immediately, whereas lower-priority jobs can be deferred
within a completion window. Furthermore, an operator managing multiple geographically
distributed facilities can select where these workloads execute. In this study, workloads
requiring immediate execution are referred to as service workloads, and deferrable workloads are
referred to as batch workloads. Together, temporal deferral of batch workloads and spatial
routing of both types of workloads across sites constitute the two control levers available to
the scheduler. At each hour, the scheduler determines where incoming service workloads execute,
how much batch work to execute, and where to route it.

\section{Research Question and Objectives}
\label{sec:intro-questions}

This study addresses the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{How much can a causal scheduler jointly smooth one-hour net-load ramps and reduce monthly
net-load peaks across regional electricity markets through temporal deferral and geographic
routing, subject to immediate-service, batch-window, and site-capacity constraints?}
\end{quote}

A scheduler is \emph{causal} when each hourly decision relies only on information
available at that hour, never on future electricity demand, renewable generation, or workload.
Rather than scheduling individual jobs, the scheduler operates on aggregate workloads across
sites and hours.

To investigate this question, a synthetic fleet of four geographically distributed data centers
is constructed using Google workload traces and regional grid data from the U.S. Energy
Information Administration (EIA). Proximal policy optimization (PPO) is used to train a
scheduling policy that satisfies workload deadlines and site-capacity limits while minimizing an
equally weighted sum of two scaled terms: the monthly sum of squared one-hour net-load ramps and
the monthly net-load peak. Accordingly, the objectives of this study are to construct the
multi-site fleet from public datasets, define a joint score for ramps and peaks, train the causal
PPO scheduler, and compare its performance with a no-flexibility baseline that executes all
workloads at their originally recorded times and locations.
The scheduler is trained and validated across the twelve months of 2025 using four-fold
cross-validation, and subsequently evaluated on May 2026 using an independent test fleet built
from distinct Google cells.

\section{Scope and Assumptions}
\label{sec:intro-scope}

This study adopts the perspective of the operator of a multi-site data center fleet, who controls
only where service workloads execute and when and where batch workloads execute. Regional
electricity market demand, wind generation, and solar generation are treated as exogenous
historical time series that cannot be influenced by the scheduler. Electricity prices, carbon
emissions, on-site generation, and demand-response programs or incentives are outside the scope of
this study. Similarly, cooling systems are not actively controlled, although the empirical
facility power measurements used in this study capture data center floor cooling. Finally, the
scheduler is compared exclusively against the no-flexibility baseline.

The model rests on four simplifying assumptions. First, the largest site in the training fleet is
assigned a rated power capacity of 500~MW, representative of a modern hyperscale data center.
Second, the rated power capacity of every other site across both fleets scales proportionally with
its compute capacity. Third, each site is assigned to a distinct regional electricity market via a
hypothetical geographic mapping. Fourth, all batch workloads are subject to a strict 24-hour
completion window.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

This study makes three main contributions. First, it defines a joint grid-centric objective that
evaluates a schedule by its impact on each market's hourly net-load ramps and monthly net-load
peak relative to the no-flexibility baseline. In contrast to existing literature, which typically
focuses on a data center's own electricity cost, energy use, or carbon emissions, this
grid-centric perspective is particularly important because the clustering of data centers creates
significant challenges for utilities and grid operators \cite{iea2026energyai}. Second, it
constructs a reproducible four-site testbed from public datasets, integrating Google workload and
power traces with EIA electricity demand, wind, and solar generation alongside causal forecasts of
demand and net load. All code and processed data are available at
\mbox{\url{https://github.com/januszjgal/dc-energy-joint-optimization}}, and
Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction} lists the commands that reproduce the results. Third, it trains a
causal PPO scheduler and evaluates policy generalization across unseen months and an independent
test fleet. Because PPO is an established reinforcement learning algorithm, the contribution lies
in the scheduling model formulation and evaluation rather than in algorithmic novelty.

\chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}
\label{chap:literature}

This chapter reviews why net-load ramps and peaks matter to grid operators, how prior work has
used the workload flexibility of data centers, and where this study departs from that work.

\section{Net Load, Ramps, and Peaks in Power Systems}
\label{sec:lit-net-load}

Because wind and solar generation has priority in dispatch in many power systems, the remaining
generators must meet the net load, the system demand not met by this variable generation
\cite{lannoye2012assessment}. Changes in net load must therefore be met by conventional,
dispatchable resources, and the \emph{flexibility} of a power system is defined as its ability to
use its resources to meet these changes \cite{lannoye2012assessment}. The flexibility required
depends on the duration of the change: a system needs less flexibility to meet a change in net
load over 10 minutes than one lasting 12 hours. This study considers one-hour ramps, the interval
at which the grid data are reported and at which the scheduler makes its decisions.

Solar generation reshapes the diurnal profile of net load. On the grid of the California
Independent System Operator (CAISO), net load dips at midday, when solar generation is highest,
and rises steeply in the evening, when solar generation drops off. This profile, known as the
duck curve, deepens as solar capacity grows \cite{eia2023duckcurve}. The evening ramp requires
conventional plants to increase their output quickly, which makes it more difficult for operators
to match supply with demand \cite{eia2023duckcurve}. The midday dip, in turn, can force solar
generation to be curtailed \cite{denholm2015overgeneration}.

Peaks matter for a different reason. Electric systems maintain enough capacity to meet the
expected peak load plus a reserve margin \cite{eia2014peakratio}; therefore, the highest load of a
period, rather than its average, determines how much capacity must be available. In New England,
for example, the ratio of peak to average hourly demand rose from 1.52 in 1993 to 1.78 in 2012
\cite{eia2014peakratio}. Consequently, a load that can change when and where it draws power can
help the grid in two ways. By drawing more power while net load is low and less while it rises,
such a load smooths ramps; by drawing less power in the hours of highest net load, it reduces the
peak.

\section{Data Centers as Grid Loads}
\label{sec:lit-grid-loads}

The growth of data center load has made its connection to the grid a planning problem. The IEA
reports that data center connection queues have increased dramatically in many jurisdictions and
that the peak demand of a data center often remains well below its nameplate capacity
\cite{iea2026energyai}. The IEA suggests that demand-response programs, dynamic contracts, and
faster grid access in exchange for non-firm connection agreements could reduce the need for
additional reserves, network upgrades, and peaking capacity \cite{iea2026energyai}. Each of these
benefits depends on when, and how much, power a data center draws.

Vasques \emph{et al.} \cite{vasques2019review} distinguish two uses of data center flexibility:
load management that reduces the data center's own operating cost, and demand-response services
that support the efficient and reliable operation of the grid. Demand response may require a
temporary load reduction or a temporary load increase during a surplus of renewable generation.
Most studies divide the flexible resources into delay-tolerant information and communication
technology (ICT) workloads and non-ICT measures, such as cooling set points and uninterruptible
power supplies \cite{vasques2019review}. This study uses only the first resource, flexible ICT
workload.

Inside a facility, workload priority already serves as a lever for limiting power. Sakalkar
\emph{et al.} \cite{sakalkar2020oversubscription} describe Google's power-capping service, which
suspends only low-priority tasks while power exceeds a threshold. This capping allows Google to
oversubscribe its power infrastructure by 25\% or more, with the achievable oversubscription
limited by the power drawn by high-priority work. The same priority structure separates service
from batch work in this study. The capping, however, protects the electrical limits of the
facility and does not address the net load of the market that serves it.

\section{Geographic Routing Among Sites}
\label{sec:lit-routing}

Routing work between sites allows an operator to exploit differences among the locations of its
data centers. Qureshi \emph{et al.} \cite{qureshi2009cutting} combine 39 months of hourly
electricity prices at 29 U.S. locations with request traffic from the Akamai content distribution
network and simulate price-aware request routing. Existing systems could reduce energy costs by at
least 2\% without increasing bandwidth costs, and a fully energy-elastic system with relaxed
bandwidth constraints by over 30\%. The savings depend on energy elasticity, the degree to which
the energy consumed by a cluster depends on its load, and they drop sharply as idle power rises.
Rao \emph{et al.} \cite{rao2010minimizing} formulate a similar routing problem as a mixed-integer
program with service-delay guarantees and approximate it by a linear program. Compared with an
average workload assignment, the resulting assignment reduces the hourly electricity cost by
30.15\% and 17.53\% on two sets of real price data.

Liu \emph{et al.} \cite{liu2011glb} replace prices with renewable availability. In a trace-based
study, routing that follows renewable generation substantially reduces the renewable capacity
needed, and small-scale storage helps most in combination with this routing.
Zhao \emph{et al.} \cite{zhao2025cfws} apply deep reinforcement learning to the same
lever: an agent migrates virtual machines among geographically distributed data centers under
time-varying electricity prices and location-varying carbon rates. The agent reduces fossil-fuel energy
by 5.67\% to 13.22\% and virtual-machine migrations by up to 86.53\%.

In these studies, the grid enters only through electricity prices, carbon rates, or renewable
availability, and a schedule is evaluated by the operator's own cost, renewable energy use, or
carbon. None of them measures what the routed load does to the ramps or the peak of the market
that receives it. The routing model of this study, with conservation of work, a capacity limit at
each site, and no transfer cost, follows these formulations; its objective does not.

\section{Temporal Deferral of Batch Work}
\label{sec:lit-deferral}

Deferral exploits the difference between workloads that must execute immediately and workloads
that can wait. Liu \emph{et al.} \cite{liu2012renewable} divide the workload into interactive
services that operate continuously and delay-tolerant batch jobs whose deadlines range from
several hours to multiple days. Predictions of renewable supply and information technology (IT)
demand feed a plan that
shifts batch work toward hours of renewable availability and efficient cooling. Compared with
existing techniques, the plan reduces recurring power costs and non-renewable energy use by as
much as 60\%. Grange \emph{et al.} \cite{grange2018green} schedule batch jobs with due dates in a
data center supplied partly by on-site renewable sources. Their scheduling heuristics are
agnostic of the electrical infrastructure: a separate system that manages the renewable sources
supplies the objective function. Fossil-fuel energy falls by up to 49\%, and the freedom allowed by the
service-level agreement largely determines the achievable saving.

Xu \emph{et al.} \cite{xu2021selfadaptive} pair a brownout mechanism for interactive workloads
with a deferral algorithm for batch workloads. In a prototype system evaluated with web services
under real traces, fossil-fuel energy use falls by 21\% and renewable energy use rises by 10\%.
Haghshenas \emph{et al.} \cite{haghshenas2022infrastructure} schedule service and batch jobs with
a three-step heuristic that accounts for renewable supply, cooling efficiency, and a tariff that
combines time-of-use prices with peak demand charges. Batch jobs must be finished within 24 hours
of submission, and the total energy cost falls by up to 46\% compared with previous solutions.

Radovanovi\'{c} \emph{et al.} \cite{radovanovic2021carbonaware} describe the carbon-aware
computing system that operates across Google's fleet. Day-ahead forecasts of grid carbon intensity
and of cluster demand feed a risk-aware optimization that sets hourly limits on the resources
available to temporally flexible workloads. The limits preserve the total daily capacity of these
workloads, so that all such work completes within a day. The optimization minimizes a weighted sum of the expected
carbon footprint and the daily power peaks of all clusters. Peaks are included because data
centers are planned around peak power, and smaller peaks reduce the need for new capacity.

The 24-hour completion window of this study follows Radovanovi\'{c} \emph{et al.} and Haghshenas
\emph{et al.}, and the division of work into service and batch follows Liu \emph{et al.}
\cite{liu2012renewable} and Haghshenas \emph{et al.} Because Grange \emph{et al.} find that the
slack before the due date governs the saving, the results of this study depend on this assumed
window.

\section{Learned Schedulers}
\label{sec:lit-learned}

Reinforcement learning (RL) addresses sequential decision problems in which an agent learns by
interacting with an environment \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. At each time step, the agent
observes the state of the environment, selects an action, and receives a scalar reward, after
which the environment moves to a new state. The behavior of the agent is described by a policy,
which maps states to actions, and the objective of learning is a policy that maximizes the
expected return, defined as the cumulative, typically discounted, reward. Model-free RL methods
improve the policy from sampled interaction alone. Consequently, they require neither an
explicit model of the environment nor knowledge of its future inputs, which suits control
problems that must be solved one decision at a time under uncertainty.

RL methods are commonly divided into three families \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Value-based
methods, such as Q-learning, estimate the expected return of each action in each state and
select the action with the highest estimate; they are suited to discrete sets of actions.
Policy-gradient methods represent the policy directly as a parameterized function, typically a
neural network, and adjust its parameters in the direction that increases the expected return,
which extends naturally to continuous actions. Actor-critic methods combine the two approaches:
a learned value function (the critic) is used to reduce the variance of the updates applied to
the policy (the actor). Proximal policy optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017ppo} is an
actor-critic method that limits how far the policy can change in each update through a clipped
objective function, which stabilizes training while allowing several passes of minibatch
updates over each batch of collected experience. PPO is the method used in this study.

Kahil \emph{et al.} \cite{kahil2025rl} review 65 studies, published between 2019 and August 2024,
that apply reinforcement learning to data center cooling and to ICT processes such as task
scheduling, resource allocation, and virtual-machine consolidation. Most of these studies evaluate
their algorithms in simulated environments, and the review identifies the lack of real-time
validation and of standardized metrics as the main gaps. Ran \emph{et al.} \cite{ran2019deepee}
train a deep Q-network with a parameterized action space that jointly schedules jobs and adjusts
the cooling airflow under a two-time-scale control mechanism. In simulations driven by a real
workload trace, this joint control saves up to 15\% of energy compared with separate control of
the two systems.

Naug \emph{et al.} \cite{naug2024sustaindc} provide SustainDC, a set of environments for
benchmarking multi-agent reinforcement learning in data centers. A load-shifting agent, a cooling
agent, and a battery agent act on a simulated data center driven by public workload traces from
Alibaba and Google. The load-shifting agent observes current and forecast grid carbon intensity,
is rewarded for a lower carbon footprint, and is penalized if it fails to reschedule flexible
workloads within their time horizon. Its baseline performs no workload shifting, which the authors
describe as the current standard practice in most data centers. Outside data centers, Zhang and
Lam \cite{zhang2018practical} train a deep reinforcement learning agent on a calibrated model of a
radiant heating system and deploy it in an office building. Over three months, the agent reduces
heating demand by 16.6\% to 18.2\% compared with the previous rule-based control.

This study follows the pattern of SustainDC: a simulated environment driven by public traces, a
reinforcement learning agent, and a baseline without workload shifting. The forecasts of demand
and net load play the role that carbon-intensity forecasts play in SustainDC and in Google's
system, as the scheduler's only view of the hours ahead. The reward differs: the load-shifting
agent of SustainDC is rewarded for a lower carbon footprint, whereas the scheduler of this study
is rewarded for reducing each market's net-load ramps and monthly peak. As in most of the studies reviewed by Kahil \emph{et al.}, the results
of this study come from simulation.

\section{Workload Traces and Power Models}
\label{sec:lit-data}

The workload of this study comes from Google's 2019 cluster trace, documented by Wilkes
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019} and analyzed by Tirmazi \emph{et al.} \cite{tirmazi2020borg}.
The analysis groups jobs into priority tiers, of which the free and best-effort batch tiers carry
no service-level objective. Best-effort batch work accounts for approximately 20\% of cell
capacity.
The analysis summarizes each
cell by the resource usage of each tier as a fraction of cell capacity, the aggregate view adopted
in this study. Da Costa \emph{et al.} \cite{dacosta2016modeling} model the earlier 2011 Google
trace and provide a reference implementation that generates synthetic workloads for comparable
scheduling experiments, whereas this study replays measured usage. Lin \emph{et al.}
\cite{lin2024systematic} catalog the real-world datasets used in this literature, including
workload traces, renewable energy data, and electricity price traces.

Dayarathna \emph{et al.} \cite{dayarathna2016survey} organize more than 200 data center power
models and note the finding of Fan \emph{et al.} that a linear function of processor utilization
accurately tracks dynamic power at the level of a power distribution unit. A model of the same
form, idle power plus a term proportional to utilization, is fitted to Google's power
measurements in this study.
Cooling accounts for approximately 30\% of a data center's power consumption
\cite{zhang2021cooling}, and joint control of ICT and cooling requires models of their coupling
\cite{zhang2016joint}. Software techniques such as virtualization, consolidation, and
virtual-machine placement reduce the energy needed for each unit of work
\cite{katal2023energy,panwar2022systematic}. These techniques complement the question asked here,
which holds the work fixed and changes only when and where it executes.

The Google cluster trace datasets are large and detailed, containing far more
granularity than this study requires. For instance, the workload trace records

aggregate workloads rather than individual tasks. The workload trace is
therefore condensed into five-minute windows to capture each cell's overall
utilization, distinguishing between work that must run immediately (service)
and work that can wait (batch). Similarly, the power trace is processed into a
linear function per cell that maps CPU utilization directly to power
consumption. Finally, while the grid data retain their original hourly resolution, they
are extended with the following derived parameters: net load and multi-hour lookahead
forecasts.

The two Google traces cover May 2019, and the grid data span calendar year

scheduler are referred to as \emph{training months}; all of them lie in 2025. May 2026 is
reserved as the \emph{test month}, on which the finished scheduler is evaluated.

\section{Data Center Workload Data: Google ClusterData2019}
\label{sec:clusterdata2019}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-role}

A \emph{Borg cell} comprises a set of heterogeneous machines managed together as a
single autonomous administrative unit. The Google May 2019 trace contains data
for eight cells, labeled \(a\) through \(h\)
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019, tirmazi2020borg}. Borg supports two kinds of
resource requests: jobs and allocation sets. A \emph{job} is a
user-submitted computation composed of one or more \emph{tasks} (i.e.,
individual programs running on single machines). An \emph{allocation set} represents a
reservation of machine resources within which jobs can run. Google's
documentation categorizes jobs and allocation sets as \emph{collections}, and
tasks and reserved resource blocks as \emph{instances}
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}. For each machine and task, the trace records
machine capacity, actual CPU usage, requested resources, and job
priority, with all user and application identifiers anonymized.

This study uses three trace tables: Machine Events (to determine
each cell's total CPU capacity), Instance Usage (to track actual CPU utilization),
and Collection Events (to extract job priorities). Because the trace is approximately 2.4 TiB compressed, local downloads are impractical, and
the trace is queried online through BigQuery, Google's cloud database service.

Rather than tracking individual jobs and their execution, this study uses
aggregate CPU utilization within five-minute windows to represent computing
demand. This approach aligns with both the summarization methodology of Tirmazi \emph{et al.}
\cite{tirmazi2020borg}, which tracked CPU usage per priority tier over
hour-long intervals, and the utilization analysis provided in Google's GitHub
repository \cite{googleClusterAnalysisColab}.

\subsection{Fixed Reference Capacity}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-capacity}

Google measures CPU in Google Compute Units (GCUs). In the released trace, GCUs
are normalized so that the largest machine across all eight cells has a CPU
capacity of 1.0 \cite[p.~4]{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}. Thus, a machine with capacity
0.5 possesses half the CPU capacity of the largest machine. A cell's
\emph{reference capacity} \(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}\) is defined as the
sum of these normalized GCUs over all machines in that cell:
\begin{equation}
  C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}
  =
  \sum_{\text{machines in } cell}
  \bigl(\text{machine CPU capacity}\bigr),
  \label{eq:reference-capacity}
\end{equation}
where the summation extends over every machine ever observed in the cell.
Because a machine can appear in the Machine Events table multiple times throughout the
month, such as when it is added, removed, or updated, its maximum recorded
capacity is used \cite{tirmazi2020borg, googleClusterAnalysisColab}.

Table~\ref{tab:cell-capacity} lists the reference capacities of all eight cells. Because every cell shares a uniform scale,
reference capacities can be compared directly across cells. This table
indicates that raw machine count is a poor indicator of cell size. For example,
Cell \(d\) has the second-largest number of machines among \(a\)--\(d\) but has
the smallest capacity. Similarly, Cell \(e\) has the highest machine count
overall but a lower capacity than Cells \(f\), \(g\), and \(h\).

The Fleet column of Table~\ref{tab:cell-capacity} divides the cells into two groups of four.
Cells \(a\)--\(d\) form the \emph{training fleet}, which is simulated in every month of 2025,
whereas Cells \(e\)--\(h\) form the \emph{test fleet}, which is simulated only in the test
month.

\subsection{Five-Minute CPU Utilization}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-utilization}

For each five-minute window \(k\), the CPU usage by all tasks in a cell is
summed and divided by the cell's fixed reference capacity. The resulting
metric, \emph{cell CPU utilization} \(u_{\mathrm{cell},k}\), represents the
fraction of total cell capacity in use during that time window:
\begin{equation}
  u_{\mathrm{cell},k}
  =
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{r\ \text{in window}\ k} a_r\,\ell_r/300}
         {C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}} ,
  \label{eq:clusterdata-utilization}
\end{equation}
where \(k\) indicates the five-minute windows, and the summation runs over every
usage measurement \(r\) (representing an individual task outside an allocation set
or a reserved block) recorded within window \(k\).
The term \(a_r\) denotes the average CPU usage over the measurement, expressed on the
same normalization scale as machine capacity, and \(\ell_r\) denotes the measurement's
length in seconds. For example, a task that fully utilizes a machine of capacity 0.5
throughout the five-minute window yields \(a_r=0.5\) and \(\ell_r=300\), contributing
0.5 to the sum. The same task measured for only 60 seconds contributes 0.1.
The numerator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:clusterdata-utilization}, the total CPU usage of
the window, is therefore on the same scale in every cell, and the denominator turns
it into a fraction of the cell's capacity.

Two properties of the usage measurements govern the formulation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:clusterdata-utilization}. First, tasks in an
allocation set draw resources from a reserved machine block, and Google reports
the block's aggregate usage across all contained tasks
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}. To avoid double-counting CPU consumption,
individual measurements for tasks within a block are excluded, retaining only
the block's top-level measurement. Such tasks are identified by a field of the Instance Usage
table that records the allocation set in which a task runs; this field is empty for tasks
outside any allocation set and for the reserved blocks themselves.
Second, while usage measurement windows are typically
five minutes long, Google logs shorter windows when a
task starts, stops, or experiences a state change
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019, googleTraceFormatV3}. Because \(a_r\) represents an average
over the measurement's own length, it is weighted by \(\ell_r/300\), the share of the
five-minute window that it covers.

\subsection{Service and Batch Classification}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-tier-split}

Google records job priorities rather than explicit service and batch
labels. Consequently, this study leverages the priority bands defined in
Google's trace analysis, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:priority-bands}. Each
usage measurement inherits the priority of its parent job or, in the case of a
reserved block, the priority of its allocation set.

  \centering
  \footnotesize

Jobs and allocation sets with a priority level of 115 or below have weak or no
service-level objectives (SLOs) and are classified as deferrable batch workload.
Conversely, those with a priority level of greater than 115 are categorized as
non-deferrable service workload. If a job has no recorded priority, it is
conservatively treated as service. The aggregate CPU consumption across these
two classes defines the \emph{batch utilization}
\(u^{\mathrm{bat}}_{\mathrm{cell},k}\) and the \emph{service utilization}
\(u^{\mathrm{svc}}_{\mathrm{cell},k}\), calculated as:
\begin{align}
  u^{\mathrm{bat}}_{\mathrm{cell},k}
  &=
  \frac{1}{C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}}
  \sum_{\substack{r\ \text{in window}\ k\\ \text{priority}\,\leq\,115}} a_r\,\ell_r/300,
  \label{eq:batch-utilization}\\
  u^{\mathrm{svc}}_{\mathrm{cell},k}
  &=
  u_{\mathrm{cell},k}-u^{\mathrm{bat}}_{\mathrm{cell},k}.
  \label{eq:service-utilization}
\end{align}

\section{Data Center Power Data: Google PowerData2019}
\label{sec:powerdata2019}

\subsection{Dataset and Power Model}
\label{subsec:powerdata-purpose}

The PowerData2019 dataset \cite{googlePowerData2019} provides five-minute power
measurements for the same eight Borg cells discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019}. Electricity reaches machines through power
distribution units, each supplying several rows of server racks. Google defines
the set of machines behind a single power distribution unit as a
\emph{power domain}, and each cell has multiple domains. Every five minutes, the
trace records each domain's power draw as a fraction of its rated capacity
\cite{googlePowerTraceDocumentation}. For
example, a value of 0.6 indicates that the domain consumed 60\% of its rated
power. Absolute power in MW is not published in the trace.
Because each cell has multiple domains, the cell-level power fraction is the average over
its domains, weighted by their machines' CPU capacity, just as the cell's CPU utilization
weights them. Per the
\emph{Google Data Center Power Trace} documentation
\cite[p.~2]{googlePowerTraceDocumentation}, the power measurements incorporate both IT
load and data center cooling. To predict the power fraction from CPU
utilization, both series are averaged by cell and by hour before fitting an
ordinary least-squares linear regression. Only complete hours are used, because the
first and last hours of the trace hold fewer than twelve five-minute windows. The
resulting model estimates the
\emph{power fraction} \(\widehat p_{\mathrm{cell}}(u)\) of a cell as a function
of its hourly CPU utilization \(u\):
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{\mathrm{cell}}(u)
  =
  \alpha_{\mathrm{cell}}+\beta_{\mathrm{cell}} u.
  \label{eq:powerdata-affine-fit}
\end{equation}
The intercept \(\alpha_{\mathrm{cell}}\)
represents the modeled power draw at zero CPU utilization, while the slope
\(\beta_{\mathrm{cell}}\) quantifies the
increase in power consumption from zero to full utilization. Note that the intercept relies
on extrapolation, as hourly CPU utilization ranges only between 0.36 and 0.75 in the
training cells and between 0.33 and 0.78 in the test cells. Therefore, the regression model
must be projected beyond the empirical domain to estimate power consumption at zero load.

\section{Power Grid Data: EIA Demand, Wind, and Solar}
\label{sec:energy-data}

\subsection{Four Markets}
\label{subsec:energy-sources}

Each cell is mapped to an electricity market. Hourly electricity demand and fuel-specific
generation profiles are retrieved from the EIA Grid Monitor \cite{eiaGridMonitor}
for four balancing authorities: CAISO, MISO, SPP, and ISO-NE, each
referred to as a \emph{market} in this study.

For each market, hourly time series of gross electricity demand, wind power
generation, and solar power generation are used. Because the EIA indexes data by
interval-ending hours, timestamps are shifted back by one hour to align with the
beginning-of-hour convention used in Google traces. Monthly
EIA data records are compiled into a \emph{panel} table, which holds one record for each
market and each hour of the month. Each record contains the gross demand, wind power
generation, and solar power generation of that market and hour, together with the quantities
derived from them in this section (the net load and the forecasts issued at that hour).
Appendix~\ref{app:data-schemas} lists every column of the panel with an example record.

\subsection{Net Load}
\label{subsec:energy-net-load}

Net load is defined as the gross electricity demand minus combined wind and
solar power generation. Let \(\tau\) denote a UTC hour, \(D_m\) the
\emph{gross demand} for market \(m\), and \(G^{\mathrm{wnd}}_m\) and
\(G^{\mathrm{sol}}_m\) its observed \emph{wind power generation} and
\emph{solar power generation}, all in MW. The \emph{net load} \(N_m\) is given by:
\begin{equation}
  N_m(\tau)
  =
  D_m(\tau)
  -G^{\mathrm{wnd}}_m(\tau)
  -G^{\mathrm{sol}}_m(\tau).
  \label{eq:energy-net-load}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy-net-load}, wind and solar power generation are deducted
from the gross demand because they are generally regarded as non-dispatchable.
In contrast, hydroelectricity is treated as dispatchable generation. Net load
thus represents the power demand that must be satisfied by dispatchable
generation sources.

Figure~\ref{fig:net-load-week} illustrates the gross demand and net load for
each market during a week in July. The gap between the two curves
reflects wind and solar power generation: prominent in CAISO and SPP, and
negligible in ISO-NE. Notably, CAISO's net load drops during midday solar peak hours
and ramps up sharply in the evening.

\subsection{Demand and Net-Load Forecasts}
\label{subsec:energy-forecasts}

Each hourly record in the panel includes gross demand and net-load forecasts
issued at that hour, with lead times of one, three, six, and twelve hours.
The decision for hour \(t\) is made at the start of that hour, before its
actual demand and generation are observed. Because each record is indexed by its
beginning hour (Section~\ref{subsec:energy-sources}), the most recent complete
record available at the moment of decision is for hour \(t-1\). Consequently,
making the decision requires querying the panel at hour \(t-1\), accessing
forecasts targeting \(t\), \(t+2\), \(t+5\), and \(t+11\), corresponding to the
current decision hour along with look-ahead horizons of two, five, and eleven
hours. These forecasts are generated from the EIA dataset using ordinary
least-squares regression.

Each forecast is produced by the linear regression model in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:forecast-model}. Let \(Y\) denote either gross demand \(D_m\) or
net load \(N_m\) for a given market, \(\tau\) the \emph{issue hour}, and
\(h\in\{1,3,6,12\}\) the forecast \emph{lead time} in hours. The \emph{forecast}
of \(Y\) for the \emph{target hour} \(\tau+h\)
is expressed as a constant \(b_0\) plus each \emph{input feature}
(\(x_1,\dots,x_9\)) multiplied by its corresponding regression coefficient
(\(b_1,\dots,b_9\)):
\begin{equation}
  \widehat Y(\tau+h\mid\tau)
  =
  b_0+b_1x_1+b_2x_2+\dots+b_9x_9 .
  \label{eq:forecast-model}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:forecast-inputs} lists the nine input features: five historical
values (\(x_1\)--\(x_5\)) of \(Y\), two cyclic features (\(x_6\), \(x_7\))
representing the time of day, and two features (\(x_8\) and \(x_9\))
corresponding to wind and solar power generation at the issue hour. The same nine inputs
are used in all 32 models (4 markets \(\times\) 2 response variables \(\times\) 4 lead times).

The \emph{regression coefficients} \(b_0,\dots,b_9\) are estimated using ordinary
least squares. A separate regression equation is used for each combination of
market (4), response variable (2), and lead time (4), yielding 32 distinct
models. The coefficients vary with lead times because relative to a 1-hour
forecast, a 12-hour forecast relies less on the most recent observation and more
heavily on the value from one week prior. The simplest benchmark,
\emph{persistence}, assumes that the forecasted value \(h\) hours ahead equals
the latest observed value. Mathematically, this corresponds to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:forecast-model} with \(b_1=1\) and all other coefficients set
to zero. Because the look-back inputs reach up to one week before the issue hour and no data
before 2025 are retrieved, the first week of January 2025 has no look-back history, and its
forecasts use persistence. For the test month, the week of data retrieved before May 2026
supplies this history.

Once the regression models are fitted on the training months, they are held
constant thereafter. The models are not updated online as the
simulation advances. Instead, only the input features update at each hour as new
data become available. Therefore, a forecast issued in May 2026 relies strictly on
regression models fitted on the 2025 data and observations available up to its issue hour.

\subsection{Forecast Fitting}
\label{subsec:forecast-fitting}
Each issue hour in the hourly EIA data supplies one data point for the regression:
the nine input features at that hour and the value of \(Y\) realized at the target
hour. Each regression model must be fitted on the training months alone, so that its forecasts
for any other month are produced without data from that month. A single data point, however,
spans several hours, because its inputs reach back as far as one week before the target hour.
Therefore, a data point is used only if all of its hours, from the oldest input
(\(x_5\)) to the target, fall in training months. The rule excludes the data points of the first
week after a month that is not a training month, whose look-back inputs would be taken from that
month, and those of the last hours before it, whose targets would fall inside it. The rule
affects only the fitting; the fitted models still issue a forecast for every hour. With all of
2025 as training months, 8,592 of the 8,760 data points are used for each model.

\subsection{Forecast Accuracy}
Forecast accuracy is evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE). For \(n\)
forecasts \(\widehat Y_i\) of realized values \(Y_i\), MAE is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MAE}
  =
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl|\widehat Y_i-Y_i\bigr|.
  \label{eq:forecast-metrics}
\end{equation}
The MAE represents the average absolute magnitude of the forecast error,
expressed in MW.

2025, and compared against the persistence baseline model. Table~\ref{tab:forecast-accuracy}

\chapter{PROBLEM FORMULATION}
\label{chap:problem}

This chapter constructs two synthetic four-site fleets from the prepared data, one for the
months of 2025 and one for the test month, and formulates the optimization problem, defining
its inputs, control decisions, constraints, and objectives. Because the
formulation is decoupled from the underlying optimization method, any scheduling algorithm
can be evaluated against the identical problem setup.

\subsection{Monthly Simulation and Hourly Workloads}
\label{subsec:transform-pairing}

The Google workload data start at midnight Pacific Daylight Time on May 1, 2019.
Workloads from Cells \(a\)--\(d\) are applied to every month of 2025.
Because the trace dataset spans
only a single month, the 744-hour workload repeats at the beginning of each
month, utilizing the requisite hours for months with under 31 days. Workloads from Cells
\(e\)--\(h\) are applied directly to the test month, May 2026.

Each month is simulated independently as a continuous period. For a given month
\(e\), the set of \emph{decision hours} is defined as
\(\mathcal T_e=\{0,\ldots,T_e-1\}\), where \(T_e\) represents the total number of
hours in that month.

Each monthly simulation begins one hour early at a designated \emph{warm-up hour}
\(t=-1\) (e.g., 06:00 UTC on July 1). The warm-up hour is neither a decision hour nor part of the performance evaluation. Its sole purpose is to establish two
initial conditions for the first decision hour \(t=0\): the grid data and the
site power demand. The grid data are extracted directly from historical records, while
the site power demand is calculated by assuming all tasks recorded in that hour are
processed in place without queuing and reallocation to other sites. Consequently,
every month begins with an empty queue of tasks. 

Let \(s_{m,t}\) and \(q_{m,t}\) denote the hourly \emph{service workload} and
\emph{batch workload} at site \(m\), respectively. Recall that service workloads must run
immediately, whereas batch workloads may be deferred.

The normalized CPU utilization at five-minute intervals described in
Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019} is converted to hourly work units by averaging the
twelve intervals within each hour and multiplying by the site's computing capacity
\(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\)
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:synthetic-service-arrivals} and~\ref{eq:synthetic-batch-arrivals}).
One \emph{work unit} is equivalent to the computation performed by the trace's largest
machine running at full capacity for one hour.
Because multiplying by capacity inverts the normalization step from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:clusterdata-utilization}, the calculation recovers the hourly average of that
equation's numerator. These work units are measured against a common Google Compute Unit (GCU)
scale, enabling them to be aggregated and migrated across different cells.
Both service work \(s_{m,t}\) and batch work \(q_{m,t}\) represent the aggregate work to be
processed at site \(m\) and are assumed to be known at the beginning of each simulation
hour:
\begin{align}
  s_{m,t}
  &=
  C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\,
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{b=0}^{11} u^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,12t+b}}{12},
  \label{eq:synthetic-service-arrivals}\\[6pt]
  q_{m,t}
  &=
  C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\,
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{b=0}^{11} u^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,12t+b}}{12}.
  \label{eq:synthetic-batch-arrivals}
\end{align}

\subsection{Information Available at Each Decision}
\label{subsec:problem-causality}

The inputs of Table~\ref{tab:model-inputs} describe the entire month, whereas each hourly
decision may use only the information available at that hour.
The scheduler makes its decisions prior to the realization of the net load for hour \(t\).
At the conclusion of the hour, these decisions are evaluated against the actual observed net load.
Therefore, at decision hour \(t\), the scheduler operates under an information structure
restricted to the following parameters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item gross demand and net load for hour \(t-1\);
  \item the gross-demand and net-load forecasts issued at hour \(t-1\),
        which target \(t\), \(t+2\), \(t+5\), and \(t+11\) (the current
        decision hour and two, five, and eleven hours later);
  \item the hour-\(t\) service and batch workloads, and each site's queued
        batch work;
    \item the \emph{urgency} of the queued batch work: how much of it must execute
        within the next 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours;
  \item modeled site power from hour \(t-1\);
    \item each market's highest net load of the month so far, with and without the site's
        power, over completed decision hours (zero before the first decision); and
  \item UTC hour, UTC day of week, and the number of hours left in the
        month.
\end{itemize}

\section{Control Decisions}
\label{subsec:problem-decisions}

In each decision hour \(t\in\mathcal T_e\), the scheduler optimizes three primary
operational variables:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Executed service work} \(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\): the amount of
        service workload processed at destination site \(j\);
  \item \emph{Total batch release} \(z_t\): the total queued batch workload released
        for execution; and
  \item \emph{Executed batch work} \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\): the amount of batch
        workload processed at destination site \(j\).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Constraints}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Every schedule must satisfy the constraints in this section.

\subsection{Immediate Service}
\label{subsec:problem-service}

All service workloads must be executed immediately within their recorded hour. Their
spatial allocation across sites must satisfy the following:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\in\mathcal M}x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}
  =
  \sum_{m\in\mathcal M}s_{m,t},
  \qquad
  x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\geq0.
  \label{eq:service-conservation}
\end{equation}
Because both sides of the constraint are expressed in work units, the
sum holds valid across sites regardless of differences in their individual computing
capacities. For example, at 22:00 UTC on July 29, 2025, the CAISO, MISO, SPP, and
ISO-NE sites have service workloads of 4,035.4, 3,579.7, 3,303.5, and 2,634.8 work
units. Equation~\eqref{eq:service-conservation} then requires the total, 13,553.4 work
units, to run somewhere in the fleet that hour. With no flexibility, each site runs its
own workload; a schedule may instead split the total among the four sites in any way
that fits their capacity.

\subsection{Batch Queue and Deadlines}
\label{subsec:problem-batch}

The scheduler determines the amount of queued batch workloads to execute and
distributes them across sites with available compute capacity. Let \(z_t\) denote the
total batch release from the fleet, \(y_{i,t}\) the batch work released from site
\(i\)'s backlog, and \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\) the batch work executed at destination
site \(j\). The released and executed batch workloads satisfy the following:
\begin{equation}
  z_t
  =
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal M}y_{i,t}
  =
  \sum_{j\in\mathcal M}x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t},
  \qquad
  x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\geq0.
  \label{eq:batch-total}
\end{equation}
Let \(B_{i,t}\) represent site \(i\)'s \emph{batch work backlog} at the beginning of
hour \(t\). The batch workload \(q_{i,t}\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:synthetic-batch-arrivals} increases the backlog, while the batch
release \(y_{i,t}\) reduces it. Furthermore, released batch work cannot exceed the
available work. The following constraints apply:
\begin{align}
  B_{i,t+1}
  &=
  B_{i,t}+q_{i,t}-y_{i,t},
  \label{eq:queue-balance}\\
  0
  &\leq y_{i,t}\leq B_{i,t}+q_{i,t}.
  \label{eq:queue-availability}
\end{align}
The \emph{completion window} \(H\) is the number of hourly slots in which batch work
may execute, including its entry slot. For \(H=24\), new batch work may execute
immediately or during any of the following 23 hourly slots, provided these hours fall
within the current month. Setting \(H=24\) for flexible workloads aligns with the
convention of Google's carbon-aware system \cite{radovanovic2021carbonaware} as well
as the batch model proposed by Haghshenas \emph{et al.}
\cite{haghshenas2022infrastructure}. Note that \(H\) is a modeling assumption rather
than an empirical measurement, as the trace records when work actually executed, not
when it was due.

Batch work entering in the \emph{entry slot} \(\nu\) must therefore be executed by its
\emph{deadline slot}
\begin{equation}
  d_{\nu}
  =
  \min\{\nu+H-1,\;T_e-1\}.
  \label{eq:batch-deadline}
\end{equation}
Releases follow a global earliest-deadline-first queue. All queued batch work across
all sites is maintained in a unified list sorted by deadline slot. Each hour, the
batch release \(z_t\) is dispatched from the head of the queue, prioritizing work with
the earliest deadline regardless of its origin. Ties in deadline slots are broken
first by origin cell, in the order of Table~\ref{tab:active-market-map}, and then by entry order.

For each site, cumulative
execution of batch work through hour \(t\) must meet
or exceed all batch work due by that hour:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\rho=0}^{t}y_{i,\rho}
  \geq
  \sum_{\substack{\nu\in\mathcal T_e\\d_{\nu}\leq t}}q_{i,\nu},
  \qquad i\in\mathcal M,\ t\in\mathcal T_e.
  \label{eq:deadline-feasibility}
\end{equation}
In words, by the end of hour \(t\), site \(i\) must have released at least as much
batch work from its backlog as the total volume of work due by \(t\). The sum on the
right covers every entry slot \(\nu\) whose deadline slot \(d_\nu\) is at or before
hour \(t\). For example,
with \(H=24\), batch work entering in hour 7 may execute during any of hours 7 through
30. Thus, by the end of hour 30, all batch work from hour 7 must be completed.
Furthermore, because all deadlines are capped at the final hour of the month,
evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:deadline-feasibility} at \(t=T_e-1\) ensures that every site
backlog is completely empty by month-end. Without this cap, the batch work of the final
day could be deferred beyond the end of the month, resulting in an unpenalized reduction
in fleet power demand via unexecuted batch work.

\subsection{Compute Capacity and Site Power}
\label{subsec:problem-feasibility}

The total \emph{executed work} \(w_{m,t}\), combining service and batch execution, cannot
exceed the site's hourly compute capacity:
\begin{align}
  w_{m,t}
  &=
  x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}
  +x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t},
  \label{eq:executed-work}\\
  0
  &\leq w_{m,t}\leq C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m.
  \label{eq:site-capacity}
\end{align}
Executed work is converted to power using the model from
Section~\ref{sec:powerdata2019} together with the site's rated power
\(\overline P_m\) (Section~\ref{subsec:transform-mapping}). With
\(w_{m,t}/C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m\) representing the site's CPU utilization, the
\emph{site power} \(P_{m,t}\) is given by:
\begin{equation}
  P_{m,t}
  =
  \overline P_m
  \left(
    \alpha_m+\beta_m\frac{w_{m,t}}{C^{\mathrm{ref}}_m}
  \right)
  \quad\mathrm{MW}.
  \label{eq:runtime-power}
\end{equation}

Work may execute at any site with available capacity. This study does not account for
potential inter-site transfer constraints such as latency, bandwidth limit,
data-residency restrictions, energy overhead, or data movement costs. Consequently,
the formulation represents an optimistic setting for geographic flexibility.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:problem-objectives}

\subsection{Net-Load Ramps and Peaks with the Fleet}
\label{subsec:problem-objective}

The scheduler pursues two primary objectives: smoothing hourly net-load ramps within
each market and reducing each market's monthly net-load peak. Both objectives are
evaluated on the \emph{adjusted net load} \(A_{m,t}\), defined as the market's
net load augmented by the site power:
\begin{equation}
  A_{m,t}
  =
  N_{m,t}+P_{m,t}.
  \label{eq:adjusted-net-load}
\end{equation}
Because a \emph{scheduling policy} \(\mu\) dictates the control decisions for each hour, the
adjusted net load depends on \(\mu\). Both objectives are measured from the
\emph{no-flexibility baseline} \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\), the policy that executes all work
immediately at its original site. Because the fleet is present under both policies, the
comparison isolates the effect of flexibility from that of the fleet itself.

For the first objective, the change in adjusted net load between consecutive hours is
evaluated. This one-hour interval aligns with the temporal resolution of the EIA power
grid data and the scheduling decisions. Normalizing by \(S_m\) expresses each one-hour
change as a fraction of the market's nominal hourly demand scale, yielding the
\emph{fleet-adjusted grid power ramp} \(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}\):
\begin{equation}
  \Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu)
  =
  \frac{A_{m,t}(\mu)-A_{m,t-1}(\mu)}{S_m}.
  \label{eq:adjusted-ramp}
\end{equation}
The policy's impact on the net-load ramp, the \emph{ramp impact} \(I_{m,t}\), is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
  I_{m,t}(\mu)
  =
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu)\right)^2
  -
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})\right)^2.
  \label{eq:incremental-impact}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:incremental-impact}, the power ramps are squared to penalize large
fluctuations and treat upward and downward ramps equally. A negative value of
\(I_{m,t}\) indicates that the policy reduces the power ramp relative to the
no-flexibility baseline, whereas a positive value indicates an increase.

The second objective is to reduce each market's monthly net-load peak. As with ramps,
the policy's impact is evaluated relative to the no-flexibility baseline. The policy's
impact on peak power, the \emph{peak impact} \(\Phi_{m,e}(\mu)\), in month \(e\) is
defined as the difference between the monthly peak adjusted net load under \(\mu\) (the
\emph{adjusted peak}) and under \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\) (the \emph{no-flexibility peak}),
divided by \(S_m\):
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{m,e}(\mu)
  =
  \frac{\displaystyle\max_{t\in\mathcal T_e} A_{m,t}(\mu)
        -\max_{t\in\mathcal T_e} A_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})}{S_m}.
  \label{eq:peak-impact}
\end{equation}
A negative value of \(\Phi_{m,e}\) indicates that the policy lowers the monthly peak
relative to the no-flexibility baseline.

\subsection{Joint Objective}
\label{subsec:problem-normalization}

Because the impacts on power ramp and monthly peak have different numerical scales,
combining them into a single objective function requires normalizing each metric by a
fixed positive reference value. Based on the no-flexibility baseline, the reference
values for the net-load ramp impact \(C_R\) and monthly peak power impact \(C_Q\) are
defined as:
\begin{align}
  C_R
  &=
  \frac{1}{|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|}
  \sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}}
  \sum_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M}
  \left(\Delta^{\mathrm{adjusted}}_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})\right)^2,
  \label{eq:ramp-reference}\\
  C_Q
  &=
  \frac{1}{|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|}
  \sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M}
  \max_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\frac{A_{m,t}(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})}{S_m}.
  \label{eq:peak-reference}
\end{align}
where \(\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}\) denotes the set of training months and
\(|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|\) their number.

\(C_R\) is the total of a month's squared ramps over all hours and markets, and \(C_Q\) is the
sum of the four markets' normalized monthly peaks, each averaged over the training months.

Each simulated month \(e\) is a distinct optimization episode. The joint objective is
to identify a scheduling policy that minimizes the equally weighted sum of the scaled
monthly net-load ramp impact and the scaled monthly peak power impact. Defining
\(J_e(\mu)\) as the joint objective, or \emph{joint score}, and \(\mathfrak M\) as the
\emph{feasible policy set} satisfying
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:service-conservation}--\eqref{eq:site-capacity}, the optimization
problem is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    &\min_{\mu\in\mathfrak M} J_e(\mu),\\
    &J_e(\mu)
    =
    \frac{0.5}{C_R}
    \sum_{t\in\mathcal T_e}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M} I_{m,t}(\mu)\\
    &\hspace{2em}
    +\frac{0.5}{C_Q}
    \sum_{m\in\mathcal M} \Phi_{m,e}(\mu).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
subject to the constraints in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:service-conservation}--\eqref{eq:site-capacity}.
The 0.5 weights apply after dividing each term by its reference scale.
Because both impacts are measured
from \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\), \(J_e(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}})=0\): a negative joint score indicates
that the policy outperforms the no-flexibility baseline, and a positive score indicates
that it performs worse. However, a lower joint score may not improve both components.
Appendix~\ref{subsec:problem-worked-joint} presents a worked example
of the joint score for one hour of July 2025.

\chapter{SCHEDULING METHOD: PROXIMAL POLICY OPTIMIZATION}
\label{chap:method}

This chapter casts the data center workload scheduling problem presented in
Chapter~\ref{chap:problem} into a reinforcement learning (RL) framework. It then details the
training methodology using PPO and outlines the evaluation protocol for the trained
scheduling policies.

\section{Reinforcement Learning Formulation}
\label{sec:method-formulation}

If the net load and workload of a whole month were known in advance, the problem formulated in
Chapter~\ref{chap:problem} could be solved directly as an optimization. A causal
scheduler lacks that knowledge: each decision must be made from the information available at
that hour, including imperfect forecasts. Reinforcement learning is used because it
learns a decision rule from simulated months, without requiring an explicit model of the
forecast errors.

In reinforcement learning, a \emph{policy} maps observations to actions and is optimized
via trial and error to maximize the expected return \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.
In this study, the policy acts as the scheduler, parameterized by a compact neural network,
while the \emph{environment} is defined by the monthly simulation of the data center fleet
across the four electricity markets. At each hourly \emph{time step}, the policy receives an
\emph{observation} vector constructed from the available information at that hour
(Section~\ref{subsec:problem-causality}) and outputs an \emph{action} vector, which the
environment converts into a fully feasible schedule satisfying all constraints. After
calculating the resulting site power demands and hourly net loads of the electricity markets,
the environment returns a scalar \emph{reward}. Because
the objective is to minimize the joint score \(J_e\), the hourly reward is formulated such
that the undiscounted sum of a month's rewards equals \(-J_e\).

\subsection{Observation}
\label{sec:method-observation}

The observation vector formalizes the information described in
Section~\ref{subsec:problem-causality} into the 82 state features listed in
Table~\ref{tab:observation}.

Among the state features, only the \emph{maximum forecast ramp rates} are not explicitly
defined in Section~\ref{subsec:problem-causality}, although they are derived from parameters
presented there. Specifically, the realized value at hour \(t-1\) alongside the four forecasts
trace a trajectory spanning hour \(t-1\) to hour \(t+11\), with the maximum ramp rates defined
as the largest rate of change along that trajectory. This feature is introduced to facilitate
policy learning, because the ramp term of the objective function depends on how rapidly the
net load is about to escalate.

The hour of day and day of week are encoded as cyclical sine and cosine pairs to ensure
temporal continuity across periodicity boundaries. For example, the hours of 23:00 and 00:00
remain proximal in the state space since they are only one hour apart. The simulator tracks
every queued batch workload by origin site and deadline. The observation summarizes this queue
in nine of its 82 features: the queued work of each site and the urgency totals, that is, the
fleet-wide queued work due within 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours. This summary suffices because the
deadlines are enforced by the decoder, which operates on the complete queue.

\subsection{Actions and the Feasibility Decoder}
\label{sec:method-actions}

The policy does not determine workload allocations directly. Instead, it outputs a
9-dimensional \emph{preference} vector, where each element is bounded between \(-6\) and 6.
A fixed set of rules, referred to as the \emph{decoder} \cite{koziel1999homomorphous}, converts these continuous
preferences into a fully feasible schedule that satisfies all operational constraints. The
decoder is not learned and is held identical across all evaluated policies. The nine
preferences fulfill three distinct functions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Four \emph{service allocation preferences} \(a^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\), one per
        site, govern the spatial routing of the current hour's service workload across the
        fleet.
  \item One \emph{batch release preference} \(a^{\mathrm{rel}}_t\) regulates the aggregate
        volume of queued batch workload released for execution during the current hour.
  \item Four \emph{batch destination preferences} \(a^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\), one per site,
        govern the spatial routing of the released batch workload across the fleet.
\end{itemize}

Based on the service allocation preferences \(a^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\), the fleet's total
service workload is distributed among the sites through a \emph{softmax} transformation,
yielding the \emph{requested service work} \(\hat x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\) at each site
\(j\):
\begin{equation}
  \hat x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}
  =
  \frac{\exp\bigl(a^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\bigr)}
       {\sum_{j'\in\mathcal M}\exp\bigl(a^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j',t}\bigr)}
  \sum_{m\in\mathcal M}s_{m,t}.
  \label{eq:service-request}
\end{equation}
The softmax function offers two properties that facilitate reinforcement learning. First,
the resulting distribution depends solely on the relative differences among the four
preferences rather than their absolute magnitudes. For example, suppose the policy outputs
two different preference vectors: \([0,1,0,0]\) and \([5,6,5,5]\). Although the second vector
contains larger values than the first, the relative differences are identical: the preference
for the second site exceeds that of the other three sites by 1. When passed through the softmax
transformation, both vectors produce the exact same workload distribution: 47.5\% for the second
site and 17.5\% for each of the remaining sites.
Consequently, the neural network only needs to learn relative differences across sites rather than exact absolute target values. Second, a
preference trailing the highest value by 6 or more receives virtually zero allocation. Thus,
bounding the policy outputs to \([-6,6]\) is sufficient, as wider bounds would not unlock
meaningful new routing behavior.

If the requested service workload exceeds the compute capacity available at a site, the
decoder caps that site's assignment and redistributes the excess to sites with spare
capacity, which yields the final executed service workloads \(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\).
Specifically, the capped site is assigned exactly its compute capacity, and its excess is divided
equally among the sites that still have spare capacity. If this division fills another site,
that site is capped in the same way, and the step is repeated until every assignment fits.

Based on the batch release preference \(a^{\mathrm{rel}}_t\), the \emph{requested batch
release} \(\hat z_t\) is obtained by linearly scaling \(a^{\mathrm{rel}}_t\) onto \([0,1]\)
and multiplying by the total queued batch workload across all sites:
\begin{equation}
  \hat z_t
  =
  \frac{a^{\mathrm{rel}}_t+6}{12}
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal M}\bigl(B_{i,t}+q_{i,t}\bigr).
  \label{eq:release-request}
\end{equation}
A batch release preference of \(-6\) requests no release, 0 requests half of the available
batch workload, and \(+6\) requests the entire batch workload. From this request, the decoder
determines the total batch release \(z_t\) by keeping the request between two limits. The upper
limit is the smaller of the available batch workload and the compute capacity remaining in the
fleet after service execution. The lower limit is the batch workload that must be released in
the current hour for all deadlines to be met: the work due in that hour, plus any work with a
later deadline that the compute capacity guaranteed to remain free before that deadline could
not absorb. A request between the two limits is executed unchanged.
Consistent with Section~\ref{subsec:problem-batch}, released batch workloads
are always dispatched across the fleet in earliest-deadline-first order, regardless of their
originating sites.

Similar to service workload allocation, the batch destination preferences distribute
\(z_t\) across the sites via a softmax transformation. If the requested batch workload exceeds
the compute capacity available at a site, the decoder caps that site's assignment and
redistributes the excess to sites with spare capacity, which yields the final executed batch
workloads \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\). The redistribution follows the same rule as for service:
the excess is divided equally among the sites that still have spare capacity, with the compute
capacity available at each site taken as the capacity that remains after service execution.

Because every set of preferences maps to a feasible schedule, the policy can explore the
entire action space during training without triggering constraint violations, eliminating
the need for penalty terms in the reward function. Moreover, the decoder remains agnostic to
net load, forecasts, and the optimization objective. Consequently, the decoder strictly
enforces operational feasibility, while the selection among feasible schedules rests entirely
with the learned policy.

For example, suppose that at 22:00 UTC on July 29, 2025, the hour whose service workloads
are detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:problem-service}, the policy outputs service allocation
preferences of 0.5 for CAISO, 0 for MISO and SPP, and \(-6\) for ISO-NE, a batch release
preference of 3, and batch destination preferences of 0 across all sites:
\[
  (0.5,\;0,\;0,\;-6 \;\mid\; 3 \;\mid\; 0,\;0,\;0,\;0).
\]
Raising \(e\) to the four service preferences yields 1.65, 1, 1, and 0.0025, which sum to
3.65. The requested service workloads are therefore 45\%, 27\%, 27\%, and 0.07\% of the 13,553
work units, corresponding to 6,120, 3,712, 3,712, and 9 work units, respectively. Nearly all
of ISO-NE's service workload shifts to the other three sites. Meanwhile,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:release-request} requests
\((3+6)/12=0.75\) of the queued batch workload, and the equal batch destination preferences
divide the released batch workload evenly across the sites, subject to each site's residual
compute capacity after service execution.

Notably, the no-flexibility baseline can be represented within this action space. Its service
preferences are the natural logarithms of each site's share of the hour's service workload, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:service-request} returns those exact shares. Its batch release
preference is \(+6\), which executes all available batch workload immediately. Its batch
destination preferences equal the natural logarithms of each site's share of that batch
workload, routing all batch work to its originating site. The baseline is therefore evaluated
through the same decoder and simulation as the learned policies, and its joint score of zero
serves as a check of the implementation.

\subsection{Reward}
\label{sec:method-reward}

At each hourly step \(t\), the environment returns a scalar reward equal to the hour's
contribution to the negated objective (\(-J_e\)) defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective}. The
sign is negated because RL maximizes cumulative reward, whereas the objective in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} is minimized.
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    r_t
    ={}&-\frac{0.5}{C_R}\sum_m I_{m,t}\\
    &-\frac{0.5}{C_Q}\sum_m\left[
      \left(M_{m,t}-M_{m,t-1}\right)
      -\left(M^{\mathrm{NF}}_{m,t}-M^{\mathrm{NF}}_{m,t-1}\right)
    \right],
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where \(r_t\) denotes the reward at hour \(t\), and
\(I_{m,t}\) denotes the ramp impact defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:incremental-impact}. The
\emph{running adjusted peak} \(M_{m,t}\) and \emph{running no-flexibility peak}
\(M^{\mathrm{NF}}_{m,t}\) represent the highest adjusted net loads (normalized by \(S_m\)) of
the month observed up to hour \(t\) under the learned policy \(\mu\) and the no-flexibility
baseline \(\mu_{\mathrm{NF}}\), respectively, with both initialized to zero
before the first decision hour. The cumulative monthly reward equals \(-J_e\) exactly.

\section{Training with PPO}
\label{sec:method-ppo}

\subsection{Algorithm}
\label{subsec:method-algorithm}

The policy is trained using proximal policy optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017ppo}, a
standard reinforcement learning method for continuous action spaces such as the
nine-dimensional preference vector defined in this formulation. This study uses the
implementation in the Stable-Baselines3 library
\cite{raffin2021sb3}. PPO trains two neural networks concurrently: the policy network
(actor), which maps observed states to actions, and the value network (\emph{critic}), which
estimates the expected cumulative future reward from a given state. The critic is used
exclusively during training to evaluate whether selected actions perform better or worse
than expected.

At each hour, the policy network receives the observation and outputs a mean value for each
of the nine preferences. During training, PPO adds Gaussian \emph{exploration noise} to each
output mean so that the agent can explore different actions and identify high-performing
ones. During this exploration process, any action value outside \([-6,6]\) is clipped to the
nearest bound. The noise standard deviation, initialized to 1, is learned separately for each
preference. During evaluation, the noise is disabled, and the policy acts deterministically
using the output means. Because the outputs of an untrained network are close to zero,
an untrained policy distributes service evenly across the four sites and requests
approximately half of the queued batch workload each hour, regardless of grid conditions.

Training proceeds in iterative \emph{rollouts}. During each rollout, the agent interacts with
the environment for 2,048 time steps with exploration noise enabled. The critic's value
estimates are then used to assess whether each action performed better or worse than
expected. Based on this assessment, the policy is updated to increase the probability of
higher-performing actions in similar states and decrease the probability of lower-performing
ones. To maintain training stability, PPO constrains the magnitude of each policy update, and
Appendix~\ref{subsec:ppo-details} provides the update equations.

\subsection{Normalization and Discounting}
\label{subsec:method-normalization}

The 82 entries of the observation vector differ by several orders of magnitude: net load
reaches tens of thousands of MW, whereas the sine of the hour remains bounded by one. Because
neural networks update their parameters incrementally, unscaled large inputs would initially
dominate layer activations and gradient updates before smaller inputs could meaningfully
contribute. Consistent with the Stable-Baselines3 documentation \cite{sb3tips}, which
recommends normalizing observations, each observation entry is therefore standardized using
running statistics via the \emph{VecNormalize} wrapper and clipped as follows:
\begin{equation}
  o=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{v-\bar v}{\sigma_v},\,-10,\,10\right),
  \label{eq:observation-normalization}
\end{equation}
where \(v\) denotes an entry of the raw observation vector in its original unit, \(\bar v\)
and \(\sigma_v\) denote the running mean and standard deviation of that entry computed over
the time steps of training elapsed so far, and \(o\) denotes the \emph{normalized entry} passed to
the policy network.

PPO, like most reinforcement learning methods, also discounts future rewards. Training
maximizes the \emph{discounted return} \(G_t\) rather than the undiscounted monthly reward:
\begin{equation}
  G_t=\sum_{l=0}^{T_e-1-t}\gamma^{l}\,r_{t+l},
  \label{eq:discounted-return}
\end{equation}
where the \emph{discount factor} \(\gamma\) is set to 0.99. Consequently, a reward occurring
one day ahead is weighted at 0.79 times an immediate reward, one week ahead at 0.18, and the
end of a month at virtually zero. In contrast, the joint score weights every hour of the month
equally.

Rewards are similarly normalized so that their magnitudes remain within a range well suited
for stable training:
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde r_t=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{r_t}{\sigma_r},\,-10,\,10\right),
  \label{eq:reward-normalization}
\end{equation}
where \(\widetilde r_t\) denotes the \emph{normalized reward} and \(\sigma_r\) denotes the running
standard deviation of the discounted sum of rewards. Training on discounted, normalized
rewards is therefore an approximation of the monthly optimization problem. During evaluation,
all running normalization statistics are frozen at their end-of-training values, and every
reported result is computed using the original unscaled rewards and the undiscounted joint
score.

\subsection{Settings}
\label{sec:method-training}

All PPO hyperparameters use the default values of the Stable-Baselines3 library
\cite{sb3ppodocs}, with two exceptions. First, each rollout of 2,048 time steps is collected
across four parallel environments (512 time steps per environment) rather than from a single
environment. Second, the minibatch size is increased from 64 to 256 time steps.
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters} in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} provides the complete
parameter settings.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:method-evaluation}

\subsection{Training, Validation, and Test Months}
\label{sec:method-cv}

A policy must be evaluated on months not used for training, because strong performance on
the training months alone could simply indicate memorization rather than generalization. In
this study, the data are organized by month, and each month forms a continuous simulation
(Section~\ref{subsec:transform-pairing}), in which queued batch work carries over from
hour to hour and power ramping connects adjacent hours. Two distinct sets
of months are used:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Training and validation months}: The twelve months of 2025,
        simulated using the training fleet (Cells \(a\)--\(d\)).
  \item \emph{Test month}: May 2026, simulated using the test fleet (Cells
        \(e\)--\(h\), Section~\ref{subsec:transform-mapping}). This month is
        used because it matches the calendar month of the Google cluster trace while
        being temporally separated by one year from the 2025 dataset.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Cross-validation} is applied to the 2025 months for two purposes: it
estimates how well a trained policy generalizes to unseen months,
and it allows modeling alternatives, such as the forecast inputs of
Table~\ref{tab:forecast-inputs}, to be compared without touching the test month.

In \(k\)-fold cross-validation, the data are divided into \(k\) equal parts, or
folds. In each \emph{iteration}, a model is trained on \(k-1\) folds and evaluated on the
held-out fold.
This process is repeated until every fold has served as the validation set once,
and the \(k\) resulting scores are averaged. Because every month is evaluated in validation
exactly once, the performance estimate does not depend on any single validation split.

Here \(k=4\), so each fold comprises three months. Folds are
constructed by selecting one month from each four-month block of the year,
thereby ensuring that the nine training months preserve the
seasonal distribution of the complete dataset.
Table~\ref{tab:data-splits} lists the four iterations.

Each iteration uses its nine training months exclusively to compute the modeling
parameters, including the forecast coefficients (Section~\ref{subsec:energy-forecasts}), the
market demand scales \(S_m\) (Section~\ref{subsec:problem-given-inputs}), and the
objective reference values \(C_R\) and \(C_Q\)
(Section~\ref{subsec:problem-normalization}). The scheduler is then trained on these
nine months and evaluated on the three validation months. The overall cross-validation
performance is calculated as the mean joint score across all twelve validation months. This mean
is referred to as the cross-validation estimate.

Cross-validation therefore covers the forecast models as well as the scheduler. In each
iteration, the regression models are fitted with the rule of
Section~\ref{subsec:forecast-fitting} on the same nine training months on which the policy is
trained, so that the forecasts of each validation month, like those of the test month, come from
models fitted without that month. Over the twelve validation months, the MAE of these forecasts
is 41\% to 83\% below that of persistence, close to the 42\% to 87\% of the test month
(Table~\ref{tab:forecast-accuracy}); Table~\ref{tab:forecast-accuracy-cv} lists their errors.
The same iterations are used to compare forecast inputs. For example, the wind and solar inputs
(\(x_8\) and \(x_9\) in Table~\ref{tab:forecast-inputs}) lower the MAE of the gross-demand models
by 0.6\% on average and that of each of the 16 net-load models by up to 4.2\%. Across the four
iterations, \(C_R\) ranges from 5.853 to 6.613 and \(C_Q\) from 3.576 to 3.739. For the final
policies, both are computed from all twelve months of 2025 (\(C_R=6.102\) and \(C_Q=3.654\)) and
are applied unchanged to the test month.

Within each iteration, three independent training runs are executed using the same set of
three random seeds, yielding a total of twelve trained policies and three validation scores
for every month of 2025. The three runs within an iteration differ only in their
random \emph{seed}, which governs the initial network parameters, exploration noise,
minibatch sampling order, and the sequence of training months. Because the training data,
constraints, and RL hyperparameters remain identical across runs within an iteration, any
performance variation between runs reflects stochasticity in the training process rather
than modeling differences.

After cross-validation, ten \emph{final policies} are trained across all twelve months of
2025 using distinct random seeds. These policies are evaluated on the held-out May 2026 test
month. This evaluation provides an unbiased measure of policy performance on a month that
had no influence on model design, parameter estimation, or training.

The May 2026 test month differs from the 2025 months in three aspects: its power grid data
come from the following year, its computing workload is drawn from Cells \(e\)--\(h\) rather
than \(a\)--\(d\), and its fleet power capacity is larger (2,398 MW vs.\ 1,869 MW;
Section~\ref{subsec:transform-mapping}). Because every 2025 month
replays the same 744-hour workload trace from Cells \(a\)--\(d\), cross-validation evaluates
generalization to unseen grid conditions under a familiar workload and fleet, whereas the
test month additionally tests generalization to an unseen workload and an unseen fleet.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:method-protocol}

Each policy and the no-flexibility baseline are simulated on the same workloads, power models,
grid data, and forecasts of each evaluated month. For each evaluated month and training run, the
joint score defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} and its weighted ramp and peak components are
reported. The totals behind the two components, the squared normalized ramps and the markets'
normalized peaks, are also reported as percentage reductions from their no-flexibility values.

Because the joint score is a normalized quantity, the effect on each market is also reported in
MW relative to the no-flexibility baseline. A \emph{one-hour ramp} is the change of the adjusted
net load from one hour to the next, and two measures summarize the ramps of a month: the
\emph{average hourly ramp}, the mean magnitude of the one-hour ramps, and the \emph{steepest
hourly ramp}, the largest of them. The average hourly ramp reflects how much dispatchable
generation must change in a typical hour, whereas the steepest hourly ramp reflects the largest
single-hour change that the system must have the flexibility to meet
(Section~\ref{sec:lit-net-load}). The percentage change in the total squared one-hour ramp, the
quantity that the objective penalizes, is reported alongside them. For peaks, the change in the
monthly peak of the adjusted net load is reported, together with the \emph{fleet-added peak}: the
amount by which the fleet raises a market's monthly net-load peak under the no-flexibility
baseline. Most of a market's monthly peak is its own load, which no schedule can change, whereas
the fleet-added peak is the part that the fleet contributes; the share of it that a policy
removes shows how much of this contribution flexibility can avoid. Because a site always draws
at least its idle power, no schedule can remove all of it. For all changes, negative values
denote reductions.

The May 2026 test score represents the final unbiased benchmark of the scheduler performance.
Because the test covers only one month, the twelve validation months across 2025
are used to characterize inter-month performance variability.

\section{Training Behavior}
\label{sec:results-training}

Figure~\ref{fig:learning} shows the joint score of the training months as training progresses for
the three runs of each cross-validation iteration, and Figure~\ref{fig:learning-final} shows it for
the ten final policies. A time step is one simulated hour, and a rollout comprises the 2,048 time
steps collected between two policy updates; a run of 1,024,000 time steps therefore consists of
500 rollouts, or about 1,400 simulated months. The faint lines show the value of each rollout, the
mean joint score of the months completed in it, and the bold lines show their moving average over
the ten most recent rollouts, approximately 36 months. Both span the entire run; the moving
average only removes the scatter that arises because consecutive rollouts complete different
months.

All runs learn quickly and stably. Every run outperforms the no-flexibility baseline within
approximately 37,000 time steps, that is, within the first 4\% of training or about 50 simulated
months, and its training score never returns above zero afterward. Learning then slows, and the
curves flatten over the second half of training. At the end of training, a joint score of
approximately \(-0.019\) in the
validation months means that the policies reduce the total squared ramps by about 3.6\% and the
markets' summed peaks by about 0.3\%; in ISO-NE, they reduce the average hourly ramp by 27~MW and
the monthly peak by 113~MW (Section~\ref{sec:results-cv}).

The outcome is robust to the random seed. Within each iteration, the three runs are nearly
indistinguishable, and their means over the twelve validation months lie within 2\% of one
another, whereas the joint score varies far more between months than between runs
(Table~\ref{tab:months}). The ten final policies, trained on all twelve months, follow the same
course and end between \(-0.0199\) and \(-0.0182\), close to the cross-validation runs
(Figure~\ref{fig:learning-final}). As
training progresses, the standard deviation of the exploration noise decreases from 1 to
approximately 0.2 for the service allocation preferences and to approximately 0.3 to 0.7 for the
batch release and destination preferences. The policies therefore become nearly deterministic in
routing service, whereas the batch preferences retain more exploration, which suggests that the
joint score is less sensitive to them.

\subsection{Policy Behavior}
\label{subsec:results-cv-behavior}

The learned policies rely primarily on rerouting service workloads across sites to achieve spatial
load flexibility, reassigning 28\% to 32\% of monthly service work. In contrast, batch workloads
rely on short temporal deferral, waiting only 0.8 to 1.4 hours on average within their 24-hour
execution window. The decoder modifies the policy's requested schedule in 70\% to 85\% of hours,
almost always because of capacity rather than deadlines: the batch release is raised to meet a
deadline only in the final hour of each month. In 55\% of hours, the policy requests more service
work at a single site than that site can hold, on average two thirds of the fleet's service work.
The decoder then runs that site at full capacity and divides the excess equally among the other
three sites, moving 13\% of the hour's work, or about 73~MW of site power, on average. The
policies have thus learned to use the decoder: requesting more than a site can hold is the
simplest way to run it exactly at full capacity. Because the reward is computed from the executed schedule, the resulting
division of the excess still serves the joint objective.

Figure~\ref{fig:isone} illustrates ISO-NE net load and site power over a three-day window around the
May 2025 monthly peak hour under the trained PPO policy (cross-validation iteration 1, run 2). Under
the no-flexibility baseline, site power stays relatively flat between 267 and 306~MW. In contrast,
the learned policy performs active valley-filling and peak-shaving: it operates the site near full
capacity (approximately 395~MW) during low net-load periods and curtails power as grid net load
ramps toward its evening peak. Consequently, at the baseline's peak hour, site power drops to
157~MW, a reduction of 139~MW relative to the no-flexibility baseline.

reduce the average ramp by 21~MW (1.4\%) and the steepest by 97~MW. In SPP and MISO, the average
hourly ramp changes by less than 5~MW, and the steepest ramp rises by 13~MW and 17~MW,
respectively.

\subsection{Policy Behavior}
\label{subsec:results-test-behavior}

The final policies behave similarly to those observed during cross-validation, albeit with a
slightly broader policy-to-policy variation. In the test month, 25\% to 33\% of service work is
reassigned across sites, batch work waits an average of 0.6 to 1.2 hours within its execution
window, and the decoder modifies the requested schedule in 61\% to 85\% of hours, moving 13\% of
the hour's work, or about 98~MW of site power, on average.

\chapter{CONCLUSIONS}
\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter presents the conclusions of the study, states its limitations, and identifies future
work.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion-findings}

A four-site data center fleet built from public Google and EIA data was used to test whether a
learned scheduler can smooth grid ramps and reduce monthly net-load peaks within its service,
deadline, and capacity limits. The main takeaways are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The learned scheduler outperformed the no-flexibility baseline in every evaluated month:
        in all twelve validation months of 2025 and, for all ten final policies, in the May 2026
        test month.
  \item Ramps fall most in ISO-NE, where a site's power range is largest relative to the hourly
        net-load ramps, and least in MISO, where it is smallest. In ISO-NE, the average hourly ramp
        falls by 27~MW (5\%) in cross-validation and by 17~MW (4\%) in the test month, and the
        steepest hourly ramp of the month by 114~MW and 46~MW. In MISO, the ramps are essentially
        unchanged.
  \item Monthly net-load peaks fall by approximately 90 to 115~MW in ISO-NE and CAISO, in both
        cross-validation and the test month. In the test month, these reductions remove 26\% and
        39\% of what the fleet adds to the two markets' peaks, and 64\% and 91\% of what any
        schedule could remove, given the sites' idle power. SPP's peak falls by 91~MW in
        cross-validation but by only 6~MW in the test month, and MISO's peak changes by less than
        30~MW.
  \item The policies act mainly by moving service work between sites: 25\% to 33\% of each
        month's service work changes sites, whereas batch work waits only 0.6 to 1.4 hours on
        average within its 24-hour window. In about half of all hours, a policy requests more work
        at one site than the site can hold, so that the decoder runs that site at full capacity.
  \item Policies trained on 2025 carried over to a new month and a larger fleet, although their
        reductions were smaller overall than in cross-validation, which is consistent with the
        larger test sites' inputs lying outside the training range
        (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\end{itemize}
These results describe an optimistic setting: routing is free and instantaneous, the sites operate
from nearly idle to full capacity, and the workload comes from one month of one company. They
therefore indicate what such flexibility can achieve rather than what a real fleet would save.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Four dataset limitations should be noted when interpreting the results. First, the workload traces
come from one company over a single month in 2019. Because every month in 2025 replays the same
workload from Cells \(a\)--\(d\), cross-validation evaluates generalization across unseen grid
demand and renewable profiles but not unseen workload patterns. Only the May 2026 test month tests
genuinely unseen workloads from Cells \(e\)--\(h\). Second, the trace is replayed from its first
day in every month without aligning days of the week. For example, May 1, 2019 fell on a
Wednesday, whereas February 2025 began on a Saturday. Third, batch work enters the queue at the
hour it executed in the trace rather than its original submission time, meaning any scheduling
delays already imposed by Google's scheduler remain embedded in the trace workload. Fourth, one
anomalous value from the EIA dataset is retained as published: CAISO's reported demand at
20:00 UTC on July 31, 2025 drops to 11,819~MW, compared with 29,881~MW and 29,886~MW in the
adjacent hours. The resulting net load of \(-7{,}242\)~MW introduces two steep ramps of about
18,000~MW in July. An audit across all markets and both years confirmed that no other single-hour
anomaly of this kind occurs.

The simulation rests on several modeling simplifications. First, the fleet and workload
representation assumes a 500~MW rated power for the site built from Cell \(c\), linear scaling of
site power capacity with CPU capacity, the geographic mapping of cells to regional electricity
markets, and a fixed batch completion window. Second, the linear power model is fitted over an
empirical operating regime where hourly CPU utilization lies between 0.33 and 0.78
(Table~\ref{tab:powerdata-coefficients}), whereas the trained policies drive sites from nearly
idle to full utilization (Figure~\ref{fig:isone}). Consequently, site power estimates at both
extremes, including the idle power that limits the peak reductions, represent out-of-range
extrapolations. Third, the demand and net-load forecasts are simple ordinary least-squares
regressions fitted on public EIA data. Forecasts of differing accuracy could alter the scheduler's
realized gains. Finally, the model assumes instantaneous, zero-cost spatial routing and allows live
workload migration without penalty, which may overstate geographic flexibility.

The PPO implementation also has two limitations. First, the observation vector encodes each site's
workloads, queued work, and power in their physical units. A policy trained on one fleet encounters
inputs outside its training range when evaluated on larger sites, as in the test fleet: after
normalization, the test sites' workloads lie on average 2.7 standard deviations from their
training means, compared with 0.8 in a training month. The observation was not changed after this was
discovered, because a change prompted by the test result would leave the test month no longer
independent of the design. Second, the PPO hyperparameters were kept at the library's defaults
without tuning for this problem (Section~\ref{sec:method-training}), and better settings may exist.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion-future}

Several extensions would further evaluate the proposed method. First, training ramp-only and
peak-only policies would isolate how strongly each objective drives improvement in the other, and a
variant in which service workloads remain fixed at their origin sites and only batch jobs are
shifted in time would quantify the gains achievable through temporal deferral alone. Second,
additional benchmarking against simpler rule-based schedulers would isolate the value added by
reinforcement learning. Third, normalizing each site's workloads, queued work, and power by its
rated capacity would render the observation space scale-invariant, enabling policies trained on one
fleet to transfer to fleets of different sizes. Finally, PPO hyperparameter tuning or alternative
reinforcement learning algorithms could yield better results than those reported here.

Additional extensions would relax the structural assumptions of the simulation model. First,
incorporating routing costs, network latency constraints, and penalties or limits on live workload
migration would yield a more realistic estimate of achievable geographic flexibility. Second,
exploiting the submission and scheduling timestamps recorded in the trace job logs would allow the
delays already imposed by Google's production scheduler to be quantified and removed from the
workload. Third, evaluating additional test months, multi-year grid datasets, and workloads from
other cells or operators would verify whether the observed reductions generalize beyond a single
test month and a single company's workload profile. Finally, wholesale electricity prices or
marginal carbon intensity could be integrated into the objective, and data center cooling or
on-site generation would provide the scheduler with additional levers.

\section{Workload Extraction (Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019})}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \codepath{extract_cells.ipynb} runs the BigQuery queries in
        Google Colab or a local Jupyter kernel.
  \item The trace's tables are \codepath{machine_events} (the machine list),
        \codepath{instance_usage} (the task usage measurements), and
        \codepath{collection_events} (the job records). The fields used are
        \codepath{capacity.cpus}, a machine's CPU capacity on the scale of
        Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-capacity};
        \codepath{average_usage.cpus}, the average CPU used during one
        measurement, \(a_r\); \codepath{start_time} and
        \codepath{end_time}, the measurement window; and
        \codepath{alloc_collection_id}, the allocation set a task runs in,
        which is empty or 0 for tasks outside any set and for the reserved
        blocks themselves.
  \item The query's \codepath{cap} step, a common-table expression (a named
        sub-query), computes
        \(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}\) (Eq.~\eqref{eq:reference-capacity}) by
        taking \codepath{MAX(capacity.cpus)} for each \codepath{machine_id}
        and summing over machines. The capacity cell sums the same
        per-machine maxima, counting machines that report both a CPU and a
        memory capacity, and saves the result, with each cell's machine count, to
        \codepath{data/cell_capacity.json}, which sets the site sizes in
        Table~\ref{tab:active-market-map}.
  \item The usage query assigns each row to a 300-second bucket by
        \codepath{start_time}, sums \codepath{average_usage.cpus} times
        \codepath{end_time-start_time} as a share of 300 seconds, divides by
        \(C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{cell}}\), and clips the result to \([0,1]\), a bound no
        bucket reaches. Its \codepath{WHERE} clause keeps rows whose
        \codepath{alloc_collection_id} is empty or 0, which drops tasks
        running inside an allocation set and keeps the blocks.
  \item The \codepath{prio} step takes, for each job
        (\codepath{collection_id}), the highest priority among its
        submission events, which have \codepath{type=0}. A left join keeps
        usage rows with no matching priority, and those count as service.
  \item \codepath{scripts/bigquery/all_usage_windows.sql} runs that extraction for
        all eight cells in one query, saved as
        \codepath{scripts/bigquery/results/usage_all_windows.csv}. It also counts the
        rows that are short, exceed 300 seconds, or cross a bucket boundary; the last
        two counts are zero.
  \item The notebook writes \codepath{data/cells/cell_a_tiers.csv} through
        \codepath{data/cells/cell_h_tiers.csv}. \codepath{TRAIN_CELLS} lists
        \(a\)--\(d\) and \codepath{TEST_CELLS} lists \(e\)--\(h\); setting
        \codepath{INCLUDE_TEST_CELLS = False} extracts only the training
        cells.
\end{itemize}

\section{Constraints (Sections~\ref{subsec:problem-decisions}--\ref{subsec:problem-feasibility})}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \codepath{project_action} in \codepath{env/projection.py}
        turns each hourly action into \(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{j,t}\), \(z_t\), and
        \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{j,t}\) satisfying
        Eqs.~\eqref{eq:service-conservation}--\eqref{eq:site-capacity}.
        Section~\ref{sec:method-actions} describes the action encoding, and
        \codepath{action_schema} in \codepath{environment.py} names its nine
        preferences.
  \item The bound of \(\pm6\) on the preferences, set in the action space of
        \codepath{RampAwareEnv} and in \codepath{bounded_fraction}, is a fixed
        convention rather than a tuned value. It provides exact endpoints of 0 and 1
        for the release fraction and is wide enough for routing: a difference of 12
        between two preferences gives one site approximately
        \(e^{12}\approx160{,}000\) times the other's share, and wider bounds would
        therefore not materially change the routing. This range departs from the guide
        to the library, which recommends rescaling continuous actions to \([-1,1]\)
        \cite{sb3tips}; its cost is narrower early exploration.
  \item \codepath{project_capped_simplex} splits total service among
        destinations so that it is conserved and within capacity.
        \codepath{EDFQueue.drain} in \codepath{models.py} gives \(y_{i,t}\),
        and \codepath{exact_transport} pairs origins with destinations so both
        totals match.
  \item \codepath{EDFQueue} keeps the backlog ledger, and after every hour
        \codepath{step} checks that entered work equals completed plus queued

\chapter{PPO POLICY UPDATES}
\label{subsec:ppo-details}

\section{Policy Updates in Words}

After each rollout of 2,048 time steps, the library assesses each action as better or worse
than expected and then updates the policy and the critic. The two steps are as follows, each
with the library setting that controls it.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Assess each action.} For each of the 2,048 time steps, the library compares
        what followed the action with what the critic expected from that situation. What
        followed is the hour's reward plus the rewards of subsequent hours, with each later
        hour weighted slightly less than the one preceding it. The difference is the action's
        \emph{advantage}: positive if the action performed better than expected, and negative
        if it performed worse. The library calculates it using \emph{generalized advantage
        estimation}, a standard method of blending the critic's estimates with the rewards
        actually received \cite{schulman2016gae}, controlled by \texttt{gae\_lambda=0.95}, the
        library's default. Looking ahead stops at the end of the rollout or at the end of
        the month, whichever comes first. If the month has not ended, the critic's estimate
        represents the remaining hours; if it has, nothing follows.
  \item \emph{Update the policy and the critic.} The library then processes the 2,048 time
        steps ten times (\texttt{n\_epochs=10}), each time in random minibatches of 256 time
        steps (\texttt{batch\_size=256}), yielding 80 gradient steps in total. Each gradient
        step makes actions with a positive advantage more likely in similar situations and actions
        with a negative advantage less likely, and moves the critic's estimates closer to the
        rewards that actually followed. PPO is distinguished by a limit on how strongly each
        rollout can change the policy: once an action has become approximately 20\% more or less
        likely than at the start of the rollout (\texttt{clip\_range=0.2}), further changes earn
        nothing \cite{schulman2017ppo}. This prevents one rollout of experience from changing the
        policy too far. It is not a hard cap, since the policy can still move further, but
        nothing in the rollout rewards it for doing so.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Update Equations}

The policy \(\pi_\theta(a\mid o)\) describes the distribution of sampled actions;
\(V_\phi(o)\) estimates discounted training returns. Here \(o_t\) is the
normalized observation and \(\widetilde r_t\) is the normalized and clipped
training reward. Generalized advantage estimation uses
\begin{align}
  \delta_t
  &=\widetilde r_t+\gamma V_\phi(o_{t+1})-V_\phi(o_t),
  \label{eq:td-error}\\
  \hat A_t
  &=\sum_{l=0}^{K_t-1}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l},
  \label{eq:gae}
\end{align}
where \(K_t\) is the number of collected steps from \(t\) to the earlier of
the rollout boundary or month-end. At month-end the next-state value is
zero. At an unfinished rollout boundary it supplies the bootstrap value.
Every run uses \(\gamma=0.99\) and \(\lambda=0.95\).

Advantages are standardized within each minibatch before the policy update.
For the sampled action \(a_t\), define
\(\rho_t(\theta)=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)/
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid o_t)\). PPO maximizes
\begin{equation}
  L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta)
  =\mathbb E_t\!\left[
    \min\!\left(
      \rho_t\hat A_t,\;
      \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t
    \right)
  \right],
  \label{eq:ppo-clip}
\end{equation}
with \(\epsilon=0.2\). The probability ratio uses the sampled Gaussian
action; the environment receives its clipped version. Clipping limits the
incentive for large policy changes; it does not enforce a bound on the
policy itself.

The critic fits estimated returns with squared error, weighted 0.5
in the combined loss. There is no entropy bonus, and gradients are clipped
to norm 0.5. These settings are listed in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}.
