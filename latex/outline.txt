RESEARCH QUESTIONS
===========================================================================

Main question:
  How much can a causal scheduler jointly smooth normalized one-hour
  regional net-load ramps and reduce regional monthly net-load peaks,
  using temporal deferral and geographic routing while satisfying
  immediate-service, batch-window, and site-capacity constraints?

  RQ1  How large is the joint ramp-and-peak improvement over the
       no-flexibility baseline in the May validation month?
  RQ2  Which lever produces that improvement: routing service between
       sites, or delaying and moving batch work?
  RQ3  Which regions benefit, and how does that depend on the fleet's
       size relative to each region's hourly ramps?

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
===========================================================================
Why the timing and location of data-center load matter to the grid, what
question the thesis asks, and what it contributes. Written for a reader
who knows neither data centers nor grid operations.

1.1 Background: Data Centers on the Electric Grid              [revise]
    Data-center load is large and growing. Grid operators care about the
    shape of that load: how fast net load changes from hour to hour
    (ramps) and how high it gets each month (peaks). Wind and solar make
    net-load ramps steeper.
    Only the opening sentence is written; the rest is a "Planned:" note.

1.2 Workload Flexibility: Timing and Location                  [drafted]
    Some computing must run right away (service); some can wait (batch).
    An operator with several sites can also choose where work runs. These
    two levers, deferral and routing, are the only things the scheduler
    controls.

1.3 Research Questions and Objectives                          [drafted]
    The main question and RQ1-RQ3 above, and the four objectives that
    answer them: build a fleet from public data, define a joint
    ramp-and-peak score, train a scheduler, and compare it with the
    no-flexibility baseline.

1.4 Scope and Assumptions                                      [drafted]
    What is modeled and what is not. The thesis takes the fleet operator's
    view. It models no electricity prices, carbon, cooling control,
    on-site generation, or demand-response payments, and uses one
    baseline. The main assumptions (500 MW sites, the cell-to-region
    mapping, the 24-hour batch window) are named here and justified in
    later chapters.

1.5 Contributions                                              [drafted]
    (1) An objective that scores a schedule by what it does to regional
    net-load ramps and peaks, in MW. (2) A reproducible four-site fleet
    built from public Google and EIA data, with causal forecasts. (3) A
    learned scheduler evaluated against the no-flexibility baseline.

1.6 Thesis Organization                                        [drafted]
    One sentence per chapter.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW
===========================================================================
What prior work has done with each lever, and why its objectives differ
from this thesis. The sections start with the grid, move through the two
levers and the learning method, cover the data that such studies use,
and end with the gap this thesis fills.

2.1 Net Load, Ramps, and Peaks in Power Systems                [new]
    Grid-side background: what net load is, why solar and wind make
    evening ramps steep (the "duck curve"), and why monthly peaks drive
    capacity needs. None of the 26 current references covers this
    directly, so a few power-system sources are needed.

2.2 Data Centers as Grid Loads                                 [drafted]
    Data-center growth, data centers in demand response, and priority-
    based power capping inside a facility (IEA; Vasques et al.;
    Sakalkar et al.).

2.3 Geographic Routing Among Sites                             [drafted]
    Moving work between sites to follow electricity prices or renewable
    output (Qureshi et al.; Rao et al.; Liu et al., geographic load
    balancing; Zhao et al.).

2.4 Temporal Deferral of Batch Work                            [drafted]
    Delaying flexible work within a completion window to follow
    renewables or carbon (Liu et al. 2012; Grange et al.; Xu et al.;
    Haghshenas et al.; Radovanovic et al.).

2.5 Learned Schedulers                                         [drafted]
    Reinforcement-learning control of data centers and buildings, and
    what each one optimizes (Kahil et al.; DeepEE; SustainDC;
    Zhang et al.).

2.6 Workload Traces and Power Models                           [drafted]
    The public traces and power models that such studies rely on
    (ClusterData2019 and Borg; Da Costa et al.; PowerData2019;
    power-model and energy-efficiency surveys).

2.7 Research Gap and Position of This Thesis                   [revise]
    Prior schedulers see the grid through a price or carbon signal, and
    grid-side reports value flexibility without a scheduler. This thesis
    scores the schedule directly on regional ramps and peaks, from the
    fleet operator's side.
    Still to add: a summary table (study, lever, objective, data, method)
    that shows the gap at a glance.

CHAPTER 7: DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
===========================================================================
What the results mean, where they stop applying, and what should come
next.

7.1 Summary of Findings                                        [new]
    Answers to RQ1-RQ3 in plain numbers.

7.2 Interpretation for Fleet and Grid Operators                [new]
    What a ramp cut of about 3% and a peak cut of about 100 MW mean in
    each region, and why the gains are reported as changes in the modeled
    score rather than as cost or reliability savings.

7.3 Limitations                                                [revise]
    One month of workload from one company, reused every month; modeling
    assumptions (service/batch split, 24-hour window, 500 MW scale,
    cell-to-region mapping); free and instant routing; a deterministic
    power fit; forecasts fitted here rather than the operator's own; one
    validation month; whole-balancing-authority grid data.
    The problem-statement list is in place; PPO Chapter 6 still has to be
    merged into it.

7.4 Future Work                                                [new]
    Cooling control and on-site generation, price or carbon signals
    alongside ramps, routing costs and latency, more test months or
    years, and other learning algorithms.

    A short closing statement.

OPEN DECISIONS THAT AFFECT THE OUTLINE
===========================================================================

- Main research question (1.3): the current wording is kept. A shorter,
  plainer version is possible: "How much can a causal scheduler that
  routes service work and defers batch work across four data centers
  smooth one-hour regional net-load ramps and lower monthly net-load
  peaks, compared with running all work where and when it arrives?"
- Comparison runs (5.8 and 6.5): which of ramp-only, local-service, and a
  rule-based scheduler to include. The ten-seed campaign is needed
  either way.
- Forecasts (3.7): keep as a section of Chapter 3, or make them their own
  chapter if the committee wants more forecasting depth.
- Wording in 4.4: "optimistic upper bound on geographic flexibility"
  should become "optimistic scenario," since a trained policy does not
  establish a bound.
- Chapter 7: keep discussion and conclusions in one chapter (as here) or
  split them into two.
