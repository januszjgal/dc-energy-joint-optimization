RESEARCH QUESTIONS
===========================================================================

Main question:
  How much can a causal scheduler jointly smooth normalized one-hour
  regional net-load ramps and reduce regional monthly net-load peaks,
  using temporal deferral and geographic routing while satisfying
  immediate-service, batch-window, and site-capacity constraints?

  RQ1  How large is the joint ramp-and-peak improvement over the
       no-flexibility baseline in the May validation month?
  RQ2  Which lever produces that improvement: routing service between
       sites, or delaying and moving batch work?
  RQ3  Which regions benefit, and how does that depend on the fleet's
       size relative to each region's hourly ramps?

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
===========================================================================
Why the timing and location of data-center load matter to the grid, what
question the thesis asks, and what it contributes. Written for a reader
who knows neither data centers nor grid operations.

1.1 Background: Data Centers on the Electric Grid              [revise]
    Data-center load is large and growing. Grid operators care about the
    shape of that load: how fast net load changes from hour to hour
    (ramps) and how high it gets each month (peaks). Wind and solar make
    net-load ramps steeper.
    Only the opening sentence is written; the rest is a "Planned:" note.

1.2 Workload Flexibility: Timing and Location                  [drafted]
    Some computing must run right away (service); some can wait (batch).
    An operator with several sites can also choose where work runs. These
    two levers, deferral and routing, are the only things the scheduler
    controls.

1.3 Research Questions and Objectives                          [drafted]
    The main question and RQ1-RQ3 above, and the four objectives that
    answer them: build a fleet from public data, define a joint
    ramp-and-peak score, train a scheduler, and compare it with the
    no-flexibility baseline.

1.4 Scope and Assumptions                                      [drafted]
    What is modeled and what is not. The thesis takes the fleet operator's
    view. It models no electricity prices, carbon, cooling control,
    on-site generation, or demand-response payments, and uses one
    baseline. The main assumptions (500 MW sites, the cell-to-region
    mapping, the 24-hour batch window) are named here and justified in
    later chapters.

1.5 Contributions                                              [drafted]
    (1) An objective that scores a schedule by what it does to regional
    net-load ramps and peaks, in MW. (2) A reproducible four-site fleet
    built from public Google and EIA data, with causal forecasts. (3) A
    learned scheduler evaluated against the no-flexibility baseline.

1.6 Thesis Organization                                        [drafted]
    One sentence per chapter.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW
===========================================================================
What prior work has done with each lever, and why its objectives differ
from this thesis. The sections start with the grid, move through the two
levers and the learning method, cover the data that such studies use,
and end with the gap this thesis fills.

2.1 Net Load, Ramps, and Peaks in Power Systems                [new]
    Grid-side background: what net load is, why solar and wind make
    evening ramps steep (the "duck curve"), and why monthly peaks drive
    capacity needs. None of the 26 current references covers this
    directly, so a few power-system sources are needed.

2.2 Data Centers as Grid Loads                                 [drafted]
    Data-center growth, data centers in demand response, and priority-
    based power capping inside a facility (IEA; Vasques et al.;
    Sakalkar et al.).

2.3 Geographic Routing Among Sites                             [drafted]
    Moving work between sites to follow electricity prices or renewable
    output (Qureshi et al.; Rao et al.; Liu et al., geographic load
    balancing; Zhao et al.).

2.4 Temporal Deferral of Batch Work                            [drafted]
    Delaying flexible work within a completion window to follow
    renewables or carbon (Liu et al. 2012; Grange et al.; Xu et al.;
    Haghshenas et al.; Radovanovic et al.).

2.5 Learned Schedulers                                         [drafted]
    Reinforcement-learning control of data centers and buildings, and
    what each one optimizes (Kahil et al.; DeepEE; SustainDC;
    Zhang et al.).

2.6 Workload Traces and Power Models                           [drafted]
    The public traces and power models that such studies rely on
    (ClusterData2019 and Borg; Da Costa et al.; PowerData2019;
    power-model and energy-efficiency surveys).

2.7 Research Gap and Position of This Thesis                   [revise]
    Prior schedulers see the grid through a price or carbon signal, and
    grid-side reports value flexibility without a scheduler. This thesis
    scores the schedule directly on regional ramps and peaks, from the
    fleet operator's side.
    Still to add: a summary table (study, lever, objective, data, method)
    that shows the gap at a glance.

CHAPTER 3: DATA SOURCES AND PREPARATION
===========================================================================
Where every input comes from and how it becomes the hourly series the
model uses. The order follows the data: workload first, then power, then
grid data, then the forecasts built from the grid data, and finally the
checks that apply to all of it.

3.1 Overview of the Data Pipeline                              [new]
    One figure and one table: the three public sources (ClusterData2019,
    PowerData2019, EIA), what is taken from each, and the hourly outputs
    they become.

3.2 Workload Data: Google ClusterData2019                      [drafted]
    The Borg trace, the fixed reference capacity of each cell, and
    five-minute CPU utilization. Includes the 300-second usage filter and
    its measured effect (6.5% of CPU work removed). Subsections 3.2.1
    Dataset, 3.2.2 Fixed Reference Capacity, 3.2.3 Five-Minute Executed
    CPU.

3.3 Service and Batch Classification                           [drafted]
    Splitting work by priority: the no-SLO tiers (priority 115 or below)
    are batch and may wait; everything else is service and must run
    immediately.

3.4 Power Model: Google PowerData2019                          [drafted]
    A straight-line fit from CPU utilization to measured power for each
    cell, scaled to 500 MW per site, with each site's idle power and
    full-load swing. Floor cooling is already inside the measured power.

3.5 Grid Data: EIA Demand, Wind, and Solar                     [drafted]
    Hourly demand, wind, and solar for four balancing authorities in
    2025, and net load (demand minus wind and solar). The regions are
    named by their balancing authorities (CAISO, MISO, SPP, ISO-NE), since
    the data cover each whole balancing authority.

3.6 Training and Validation Calendar, and Regional Scale       [drafted]
    Eleven months of 2025 for training and May for validation. The fixed
    regional scale S_m (the 95th percentile of training-month demand)
    lets regions of very different size be compared.

3.7 Demand and Net-Load Forecasts                              [drafted]
    The 32 ridge-regression forecasts (4 regions x 2 quantities x 4 lead
    times): the model, its 28 inputs, fitting and penalty selection, the
    output columns, and accuracy against persistence (24-78% lower error
    in May). Subsections 3.7.1 Model, 3.7.2 Inputs, 3.7.3 Fitting,
    3.7.4 Output, 3.7.5 Accuracy.

3.8 Data Quality Checks                                        [new]
    Missing hours and how gaps are filled, the hour-ending to
    hour-beginning timestamp shift, and the causality checks on the
    forecast inputs.
    Open item: gap filling interpolates in both directions, so a filled
    hour can use later data. This section should report how many hours
    that affects.


===========================================================================
CHAPTER 4: SYNTHETIC FLEET AND PROBLEM FORMULATION
===========================================================================
How the prepared data become a four-site fleet, and the exact
optimization problem the scheduler must solve. Nothing in this chapter
depends on the learning method, so any scheduler could be scored against
it.

4.1 The Four-Site Fleet                                        [drafted]
    Each Google cell is placed in one region. All sites have the same
    compute capacity and a 500 MW reference power. One work unit is one
    site at full use for one hour.

4.2 Monthly Simulation and Hourly Arrivals                     [drafted]
    One calendar month is one continuous simulation. The May 2019
    workload repeats each month, and its five-minute use becomes hourly
    service and batch arrivals. One warm-up hour starts the month, and
    queues start and end empty.

4.3 Fleet Size Relative to Regional Ramps                      [drafted]
    How large the fleet's power swing is next to each region's typical
    hourly ramps: large in ISO-NE, small in MISO. This sets expectations
    for the regional results in 6.3.

4.4 Decisions and Constraints                                  [drafted]
    The three hourly decisions (where service runs, how much batch runs,
    and where it runs) and the rules they must follow: service runs in
    its arrival hour, batch finishes within 24 hours and by month-end,
    and no site exceeds its capacity. Routing has no latency or cost,
    which makes geographic flexibility optimistic. Subsections 4.4.1
    Sets, Inputs, and Units; 4.4.2 Scheduler Controls; 4.4.3 Immediate
    Service; 4.4.4 Batch Queue and Deadlines; 4.4.5 Capacity and Site
    Power.

4.5 Net-Load Ramps and Peaks with the Fleet                    [drafted]
    Adjusted net load (grid plus fleet), the one-hour ramp impact, and
    the monthly peak impact, each measured against the grid without the
    fleet.

4.6 Joint Objective                                            [drafted]
    The two fixed reference scales C_R and C_Q, the equally weighted
    score J, and what equal weights mean in practice (ramp gains
    dominate).

4.7 Information Available to the Scheduler                     [drafted]
    What the scheduler knows when it decides (last hour's grid data,
    forecasts, current arrivals, queues, running peaks) and what it
    cannot know (this hour's net load).

4.8 Baseline and Formal Problem Statement                      [drafted]
    The no-flexibility baseline, the improvement measure
    J(baseline) - J(policy), and the one-paragraph problem statement.

4.9 Worked Example: Joint Ramp and Peak Reduction              [drafted]
    One July hour in which moving ISO-NE service to CAISO lowers the
    ISO-NE ramp and monthly peak, carried through to the change in J.

CHAPTER 5: SCHEDULING METHOD: PROXIMAL POLICY OPTIMIZATION
===========================================================================
How the problem of Chapter 4 becomes a reinforcement-learning task, how
it is trained with PPO, and how the trained policies are evaluated.

5.1 Reinforcement Learning in Brief                            [new]
    Agent, observation, action, reward, episode, and policy in plain
    terms, using the monthly simulation as the example. Needed because
    the committee may not know reinforcement learning.

5.2 Observation                                                [in PPO]
    The 82 numbers the policy sees each hour, grouped by region, site,
    queue, and calendar.
    Now: PPO 2.1.

5.3 Actions and the Feasibility Decoder                        [in PPO]
    Nine preferences in [-6, 6] turned into a feasible schedule: softmax
    routing, the capacity projection, earliest-deadline-first batch
    release, and forced release when deadlines require it. Constraints
    are enforced here rather than penalized in the reward.
    Now: PPO 2.2.

5.4 Reward                                                     [in PPO]
    The hourly reward, whose monthly sum equals -J exactly, so that
    raising the return lowers the objective.
    Now: PPO 2.3.

5.5 The PPO Algorithm                                          [in PPO]
    Actor and critic networks, generalized advantage estimation, the
    clipped update, and observation and reward normalization, including
    where training (discounted) differs from evaluation (undiscounted).
    Full update equations go in Appendix C.
    Now: PPO 3.1, 3.2.

5.6 Training Setup                                             [in PPO]
    Network size, hyperparameters (not tuned), the eleven training months
    in shuffled order, 1,024,000 interactions per seed, and what a seed
    controls.
    Now: PPO 3.3.

5.7 Evaluation Protocol                                        [in PPO]
    The deterministic policy runs on May; the baseline runs on the same
    inputs. Reported measures: joint improvement, its ramp and peak
    parts, and regional MW. J is recomputed from the raw data and checked
    against the return. May is a validation month, not an untouched test.
    Now: PPO Chapter 4.

5.8 Comparison Runs                                            [planned]
    What else is run, and why: a ten-seed campaign (seeds 4101-4110);
    ramp-only and peak-only policies, to test whether the peak term is
    what causes the peak cuts; and, if chosen, a forecast-aware rule-based
    scheduler and a local-service variant (service stays at its own site
    and only batch moves).
    Now: PPO Chapter 6 (listed as next steps).

CHAPTER 7: DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
===========================================================================
What the results mean, where they stop applying, and what should come
next.

7.1 Summary of Findings                                        [new]
    Answers to RQ1-RQ3 in plain numbers.

7.2 Interpretation for Fleet and Grid Operators                [new]
    What a ramp cut of about 3% and a peak cut of about 100 MW mean in
    each region, and why the gains are reported as changes in the modeled
    score rather than as cost or reliability savings.

7.3 Limitations                                                [revise]
    One month of workload from one company, reused every month; modeling
    assumptions (service/batch split, 24-hour window, 500 MW scale,
    cell-to-region mapping); free and instant routing; a deterministic
    power fit; forecasts fitted here rather than the operator's own; one
    validation month; whole-balancing-authority grid data.
    The problem-statement list is in place; PPO Chapter 6 still has to be
    merged into it.

7.4 Future Work                                                [new]
    Cooling control and on-site generation, price or carbon signals
    alongside ramps, routing costs and latency, more test months or
    years, and other learning algorithms.

7.5 Conclusions                                                [new]
    A short closing statement.

  plainer version is possible: "How much can a causal scheduler that
  routes service work and defers batch work across four data centers
  smooth one-hour regional net-load ramps and lower monthly net-load
  peaks, compared with running all work where and when it arrives?"
- Comparison runs (5.8 and 6.5): which of ramp-only, local-service, and a
  rule-based scheduler to include. The ten-seed campaign is needed
  either way.
- Forecasts (3.7): keep as a section of Chapter 3, or make them their own
  chapter if the committee wants more forecasting depth.
- Wording in 4.4: "optimistic upper bound on geographic flexibility"
  should become "optimistic scenario," since a trained policy does not
  establish a bound.
- Chapter 7: keep discussion and conclusions in one chapter (as here) or
  split them into two.
