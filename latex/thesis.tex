\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Data centers are large electricity consumers, and part of what they do is
flexible. A web search or a database query has to run the moment it arrives ---
but it does not have to run in a particular building. Other work --- log
processing, indexing, batch analytics --- only has to finish by some deadline,
so it can also be run an hour later. This thesis asks whether a scheduler that
decides \emph{where} to run all of the work, and \emph{when} to run the part
that can wait, can make the surrounding electricity grid easier to operate.

``Easier'' here means smaller ramps. A grid operator's hard problem is usually
not high demand but \emph{fast changes} in demand, because a fast change has to
be met by generators that can start, stop, and swing output quickly. When the
sun sets on a solar-heavy grid, demand net of renewables can climb for several
hours in a row, and that climb is expensive and emissions-intensive to cover.

The simulation is assembled from public data. Google's ClusterData2019 trace
supplies measured compute demand for four production compute clusters. Google's
PowerData2019 trace supplies the relationship between compute use and power
draw. Data from the U.S.\ Energy Information Administration and four grid
operators supplies demand, wind, solar, and day-ahead prices for four American
electricity markets. One 500 MW data center is placed in each market, and a
reinforcement-learning agent trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is
given hourly control over where interactive work is served, and over how much
deferrable work runs now and where.

Across ten independently seeded training runs, evaluated on the same 28 unseen
days, every agent reduced grid ramp relative to a baseline that runs all work
as soon as it arrives (exact Wilcoxon \(p=0.001953\), mean paired improvement
\(-6.6161\times10^{-5}\) in normalized squared-ramp units). The improvement was
not uniform: three of the four markets improved in all ten runs, and MISO
worsened in all ten. Wholesale energy cost rose 0.12\%, and across all 280
evaluation days no work was dropped, missed a deadline, or was left unfinished.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Why grid ramps matter}
\label{subsec:intro-ramps}

Electricity has to be generated at the same instant it is consumed. A grid
operator therefore has to match supply to demand continuously, and the
difficulty of that job depends less on how much demand there is than on how
quickly it changes.

Wind and solar generation sharpen the problem, because operators cannot control
when they produce. What the rest of the fleet has to cover is not total demand
but \emph{net load}: demand minus wind and solar. On a sunny grid, net load
falls through the middle of the day as solar output rises, then climbs steeply
in the early evening as solar output disappears and people come home. This
shape is often called the duck curve, and its evening neck is the expensive
part: several gigawatts of generation have to come online within a few hours,
usually from gas plants that are held ready for exactly this purpose.

A schedule that flattens that climb is therefore worth something, even if it
does not change the total amount of energy consumed. That is the quantity this
thesis optimizes.

\subsection{What a data center can change}
\label{subsec:intro-flexibility}

Data-center load is unusual among large loads in that a meaningful share of it
is movable in time and in space.

The two levers are distinct, and they apply to different amounts of the load.

\emph{Movable in time} applies only to the part of compute that is not urgent.
Production traces distinguish latency-sensitive work, which must run on arrival,
from best-effort batch work, which is throughput-oriented and carries no
service-level objective. If batch work can wait an hour, the power it would have
drawn can be moved to an hour when the grid would rather have it. In the cells
used here, that is roughly 16--26\% of measured compute
(Table~\ref{tab:clusterdata-cell-summary}).

\emph{Movable in space} applies to everything. A large operator runs many sites,
and a request that must be served immediately still does not have to be served
in a particular building --- it only has to be served somewhere with capacity.
Two sites in two electricity markets are in different grid conditions at the same
moment: the sun sets on California three hours after it sets on New England.
Sending work to the site whose grid is currently falling rather than climbing
changes where the load lands, and that option is open for urgent and deferrable
work alike.

So the scheduler can relocate all of the load and delay a fifth of it. This
thesis models both levers together. What it does \emph{not} model is everything
else a real operator would weigh --- network bandwidth, data residency, the cost
of moving a dataset across a continent, cooling response. Those exclusions
matter most for the spatial lever, precisely because it applies so broadly, and
they are collected in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{What this thesis builds}
\label{subsec:intro-what}

The experiment has four pieces, and each of the next four sections builds one:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A workload.} Measured five-minute CPU demand from four Google
        Borg cells, split into work that must run immediately and work that can
        be deferred (Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019}).
  \item \textbf{A power model.} A fitted relationship, one per cell, converting
        compute utilization into a fraction of the site's rated power
        (Section~\ref{sec:powerdata2019}).
  \item \textbf{Four grids.} Hourly demand, wind, solar, and day-ahead price for
        CAISO, MISO, SPP, and ISO-NE, plus a forecast built only from
        information available at decision time
        (Section~\ref{sec:energy-data}).
  \item \textbf{A scheduler.} A PPO agent that sees 100 numbers each hour and
        outputs 9, which a decoder turns into a work assignment that is
        guaranteed to respect capacity and deadlines
        (Sections~\ref{sec:problem-statement} and
        \ref{sec:optimization-objective}).
\end{enumerate}

The agent is then compared, on days it never trained on, against a baseline
that runs everything as soon as it arrives --- which is what an ordinary
grid-unaware data center does.

\subsection{Contributions and scope}
\label{subsec:intro-scope}

The contribution is an end-to-end, reproducible pipeline from public workload
traces and public grid data to a trained scheduling agent, together with a
ten-seed evaluation that reports where the agent helps and where it does not.
Every number in this document is traceable to a committed file or a named
public source, and Section~\ref{sec:campaign} reports the market that got worse
alongside the three that got better.

Three limits are worth stating at the outset, because they bound what the
results mean. The workload is one month of one company's traffic from 2019,
repeated across a 2025--2026 electricity calendar; the deferral deadlines are
chosen by us rather than measured; and the fleet is assumed too small to move
prices, so the grid never reacts to what the data centers do.

\subsection{How to read this document}
\label{subsec:intro-roadmap}

Sections~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019}--\ref{sec:energy-data} describe measured
data and can be skimmed by a reader who trusts the sources. Section~\ref{sec:experimental-transformations}
is where measurement stops and our assumptions begin, and is the section to
read closely if you want to know what is real and what is stipulated.
Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement} states the scheduling problem without
reference to reinforcement learning. Section~\ref{sec:optimization-objective}
introduces PPO, the reward, and the training configuration; it assumes no prior
knowledge of reinforcement learning. Section~\ref{sec:campaign} reports the
results and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} what they do and do not establish.
Appendices hold material that would otherwise interrupt the argument.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Data-center energy management is a large and well-surveyed field. This section
places the present work inside it, and is organized by what is being optimized,
because that is where this thesis differs from its neighbors.

\subsection{The scale of the problem}
\label{subsec:related-scale}

Data centers were already about 1.5\% of global electricity consumption with an
annual growth rate around 4.3\% before the current wave of AI deployment
\cite{vasques2019review}, and the surveys of Dayarathna, Wen, and Fan
\cite{dayarathna2016modeling} and of Katal, Dahiya, and Choudhury
\cite{katal2022energy} document how much modeling effort that has attracted. The
International Energy Agency's \emph{Key Questions on Energy and AI}
\cite{iea2025energyai} is the most recent statement of why the question now
carries policy weight rather than only operational weight: the growth is
concentrated in a small number of very large, very concentrated loads, which is
precisely the shape of load a grid operator finds hardest to accommodate.

\subsection{Moving load in space}
\label{subsec:related-space}

The idea that a multi-site operator can exploit differences between electricity
markets goes back to Qureshi, Weber, Balakrishnan, Guttag, and Maggs, whose
\emph{Cutting the Electric Bill for Internet-Scale Systems}
\cite{qureshi2009cutting} showed using Akamai traces that routing requests
toward cheap locations could cut energy expenditure meaningfully. Rao, Liu, Xie,
and Liu formalized the same intuition as a constrained optimization over
distributed data centers in a multi-electricity-market environment
\cite{rao2010minimizing}, and Liu, Lin, Wierman, Low, and Andrew extended it
from price to renewable availability in \emph{Geographical Load Balancing with
Renewables} \cite{liu2011geographical}, which is the closest ancestor of this
thesis's spatial lever.

That literature almost uniformly optimizes cost or renewable utilization, and
almost uniformly assumes the workload is interactive and must be served
immediately somewhere. This thesis inherits the spatial lever but pairs it with
a temporal one and points both at a different target.

\subsection{Moving load in time}
\label{subsec:related-time}

Temporal shifting requires a workload that tolerates delay, which is why it
tends to appear in work grounded in real traces. Grange, Da Costa, and Stolf
schedule against renewable availability with explicit SLA-derived slack
\cite{grange2018green}, and Xu, Toosi, and Buyya combine a brownout mechanism
for interactive work with a deferral mechanism for batch work
\cite{xu2021selfadaptive} --- the same service/batch division this thesis takes
from the Borg priority bands.

The most directly comparable system is Google's own. Radovanovi\'{c} and
colleagues describe a production carbon-intelligent compute management system
that forecasts the next day's grid carbon intensity, predicts demand, and emits
hourly Virtual Capacity Curves limiting how much temporally flexible work may
run in each hour \cite{radovanovic2023carbon}. The structural resemblance is
close: day-ahead forecasting, hourly limits on flexible work, deadlines
preserved within a day. The difference is the objective. That system minimizes
carbon; this one minimizes ramp.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for data-center energy}
\label{subsec:related-rl}

Kahil, Sharma, Välisuo, and Elmusrati survey this area systematically
\cite{kahil2025rlreview} and find it dominated by cooling control, with
comparatively little work applying RL to workload scheduling against external
grid conditions. Representative examples include DeepEE, which jointly optimizes
job scheduling and airflow with a parameterized-action DQN
\cite{ran2019deepee}, and CFWS, which uses deep RL to trade energy cost against
carbon footprint across geo-distributed data centers
\cite{zhao2025cfws}. SustainDC \cite{naug2024sustaindc} provides a multi-agent
benchmark spanning workload scheduling, cooling, and battery management, and is
the closest thing the field has to a standard environment.

Two things distinguish the present work within this group. It excludes cooling
entirely --- a scope decision, since cooling control is a well-studied problem
with its own literature \cite{zhang2021cooling, zhang2016joint} and combining it
would confound the load-shifting question being asked. And its reward is a
grid-side quantity computed from measured market data, rather than a
facility-side quantity computed from a thermal or cost model.

\subsection{Workload traces}
\label{subsec:related-traces}

The Google cluster traces are the standard public workload for this kind of
study. Wilkes documents the 2019 v3 format \cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}, and
Tirmazi and colleagues describe what changed between the 2011 and 2019 traces
and why the newer one is more representative of modern production
\cite{tirmazi2020borg}. Da Costa, Grange, and De Courchelle provide a generative
model for researchers who need Google-like workload beyond the trace itself
\cite{dacosta2016modeling}. Sakalkar and colleagues describe how Google actually
manages power headroom at the facility level through priority-aware capping
\cite{sakalkar2020oversubscription}, which is the operational counterpart to the
priority tiers this thesis uses to decide what may be deferred.

\subsection{Where this thesis sits}
\label{subsec:related-gap}

The gap this work addresses is the objective. Prior systems overwhelmingly
optimize one of three things: electricity cost
\cite{qureshi2009cutting, rao2010minimizing, haghshenas2022infrastructure},
renewable utilization
\cite{liu2011geographical, grange2018green, xu2021selfadaptive}, or carbon
\cite{radovanovic2023carbon, zhao2025cfws}. Each is a quantity the operator
cares about.

Ramp is a quantity the \emph{grid} cares about. It is the rate at which net load
changes, and it determines how much fast-responding generation has to be held
ready --- a reliability and cost burden borne by the system rather than by the
data center. Optimizing it directly, against measured market data from four
real balancing authorities, with a workload split by measured production
priority and a power model fitted to measured production power, is the
contribution here. The result is also reported honestly enough to show the
limits of the framing: an equally weighted ramp objective improves three markets
and degrades one (Section~\ref{subsec:campaign-markets}), which is a finding
about the objective as much as about the agent.

\section{Google ClusterData2019 Workload Trace}
\label{sec:clusterdata2019}

\subsection{Dataset and tables used}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-role}

A Borg \emph{cell} is one management unit of Google's cluster scheduler: a few
thousand machines that jobs are submitted to and scheduled across, roughly
corresponding to one data center's compute fleet. ClusterData2019 records what
eight such cells, labeled \(a\)--\(h\), actually did during May 2019
\cite{wilkes2020clusterdata2019}. It contains machine capacity, per-instance
resource usage, job submission events, priorities, and resource requests.
Everything is anonymized: the trace names no user, no application, and --- a
point that matters later --- no geographic location.\footnote{The full dataset
is roughly 2.4 TiB compressed and is queried through BigQuery rather than
downloaded. For a cell \(c\), the dataset is
\codepath{google.com:google-cluster-data.clusterdata_2019_c}, with the final
character replaced by the cell letter.}

We use cells \(a\)--\(d\). Cells \(e\)--\(h\) are extracted the same way and
held back as a workload the trained agent has never seen; testing on them is
future work, not part of this experiment.

Three BigQuery tables produce the curves this experiment consumes ---
\codepath{machine_events} for capacity, \codepath{instance_usage} for measured
CPU, and \codepath{collection_events} for priority. Once the curves are
extracted, nothing downstream touches BigQuery again.
Table~\ref{tab:clusterdata-upstream-fields} in
Appendix~\ref{app:source-tables} gives the exact fields.

\subsection{Measured utilization extraction}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-utilization}

The goal is a single number per cell per five minutes: what fraction of that
cell's CPUs were busy. That is a ratio, so it needs a numerator (CPU actually
used) and a denominator (CPU available).

The denominator is total cell capacity. A machine can appear several times in
\codepath{machine_events}, through add, update, and snapshot records, so we take
each machine's largest reported capacity and sum across machines. This picks the
largest \emph{capacity} record, not the busiest moment.

The numerator sums \codepath{average_usage.cpus} across instances in each
five-minute bucket, after two filters:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Top-level instances only.} Google's trace lets a task run
        inside an \emph{allocation set}, a reserved block of resources that is
        itself a task. Counting both the allocation set and the tasks inside it
        would count the same CPU twice, so we keep only records whose
        \codepath{alloc_collection_id} is null or zero
        (\codepath{NO_ALLOC_COLLECTION}) \cite{googleTraceFormatV3}. Google's
        own analysis notebook calls these ``top-level instances.''
  \item \textbf{Complete windows only.} A usage record that covers less than the
        full five minutes is dropped. The trace emits a partial record when an
        instance starts or stops mid-window, and its reported average describes
        only the part it covered; summing it as though it covered all five
        minutes would overstate that instance. Requiring at least 300 seconds
        gives every retained row the same duration. The cost is that very
        short-lived work is underrepresented.
\end{itemize}

Dividing the filtered numerator by cell capacity gives normalized utilization.
Values above one can occur where the trace reports overcommitment, and are
capped at one.

The output is one file per cell, \codepath{data/cells/cell_X_tiers.csv}, with
columns \codepath{timestep}, \codepath{cpu_demand_norm}, \codepath{batch_share},
\codepath{service_demand_norm}, and \codepath{batch_demand_norm}.

\subsection{Service and deferrable-batch decomposition}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-tier-split}

The whole experiment depends on splitting measured CPU into work that must run
now and work that can wait. Google's trace supports that split through job
priority.

Every submitted job carries a priority, and Google groups priorities into named
bands. The two lowest bands --- ``free'' and ``best-effort batch,'' together
everything at priority 115 or below --- carry no service-level objective: Borg
may run this work whenever there is room, and may evict it when something more
important needs the machine \cite{googleClusterAnalysisColab}. That is the
operational definition of deferrable, and it is the definition this thesis uses.
Everything above 115 is treated as work that must be served on arrival.

Priority lives in \codepath{collection_events}, while usage lives in
\codepath{instance_usage}, so the extraction builds one priority record per
submitted job and joins it to usage by \codepath{collection_id}. Within each
five-minute bucket, batch CPU is the usage matched to a priority at or below
115, normalized by the same cell capacity as before. Service CPU is aggregate
minus batch, so the two components add back to the measured total at every
timestep.

That subtraction is why the second component is named \emph{service/residual}
rather than \emph{service}. Usage rows that find no matching priority record
are counted as zero batch, which leaves them in the service/residual side by
default, and the extraction does not audit how often that happens. The residual
is therefore an upper bound on non-deferrable work: some of it is genuinely
high-priority, and some of it is simply unmatched. Treating unmatched work as
non-deferrable is the conservative direction, since it understates rather than
overstates the flexibility available to the scheduler.

\subsection{Measured cell characteristics}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-cell-characteristics}

Table~\ref{tab:clusterdata-cell-summary} is computed directly from the tier
CSVs: mean and peak are over \codepath{cpu_demand_norm}, and batch share is the
mean of \codepath{batch_share}.\footnote{Machine counts and job-duration
statistics are not reported, because they are not retained in the tier CSVs and
nothing downstream uses them.}

The batch share needs care in interpretation, because it bounds one of the
scheduler's two levers and not the other. It is the fraction of each cell's work
that can be moved \emph{in time}, since only batch work may wait. It is not the
fraction that can be moved \emph{in space}: service work is pooled fleet-wide
each hour and may be routed to any site with capacity
(Section~\ref{subsec:problem-decisions}), so spatial flexibility applies to all
of the load. What service work cannot do is arrive in a later hour.

Batch share varies widely across cells --- from 8.26\% in cell \(f\) to 37.93\%
in cell \(g\). The four cells this experiment uses span 15.70\% to 26.11\%, so
every site has some deferrable work and none is dominated by it.

\subsection{Artifacts, reproduction, and limitations}
\label{subsec:clusterdata-artifacts}

Re-running the extraction needs a Google Cloud project with BigQuery billing
enabled: \codepath{extract_tier_curves.ipynb} for cells \(a\)--\(d\),
\codepath{extract_cells_eh.ipynb} for \(e\)--\(h\). What the repository keeps is
the small tier CSVs, which are what everything downstream reads.
Table~\ref{tab:clusterdata-artifact-lineage} in
Appendix~\ref{app:source-tables} records that lineage file by file.

Three limitations follow from the source. The trace is one company's workload
during one month of 2019, so these eight cells are a useful sample of
production behavior rather than a representative sample of the industry.

The complete-window filter underrepresents short-lived work, and we did not
audit by how much. The extraction aggregates before the discarded rows are
visible, so the quantity is not recoverable from the committed files; measuring
it means re-querying \codepath{instance_usage} and comparing CPU-seconds above
and below the 300-second threshold. Two things can be said without that query.
No five-minute bucket was emptied by the filter --- all 8,929 buckets are
present and non-zero in every cell --- so the loss is not concentrated enough to
create gaps. And short-lived instances are plausibly enriched in evicted
best-effort work, which would mean the filter understates batch share and
therefore understates the flexibility available to the scheduler. Both points
are reasoning, not measurement, and the audit remains open work.

Finally, unmatched priority rows fall into the service/residual side, so that
curve should be read as ``not identified as deferrable'' rather than ``measured
as high-priority.''

\section{Google PowerData2019 Power Model}
\label{sec:powerdata2019}

\subsection{Purpose and source}
\label{subsec:powerdata-purpose}

Section~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019} produced a curve of \emph{how busy} each cell
was. What the grid cares about is \emph{how many megawatts} the site drew.
Something has to connect the two, and a data center's power draw is not
proportional to its utilization: servers consume a large share of their peak
power while idle, so a cell at 0\% CPU still draws roughly half of what it
draws at 100\%.

PowerData2019 measures that relationship. It reports power utilization for 57
Google \emph{power domains} --- the electrical distribution units a set of
machines hangs off --- most of which belong to the eight ClusterData2019 cells
\cite{googlePowerData2019}. Because it reports power as a fraction of the
domain's capacity rather than in watts, it tells us the \emph{shape} of the
CPU-to-power curve but not the absolute size of any facility. The absolute size
is our own assumption, introduced in
Section~\ref{subsec:transform-scaling}.\footnote{The source is the BigQuery
wildcard \codepath{google.com:google-cluster-data.powerdata_2019.cell*}.}

The fit draws on all three sources: measured power from PowerData2019, and CPU
usage and machine capacity from ClusterData2019 to build the predictor.
Table~\ref{tab:powerdata-fields} in Appendix~\ref{app:source-tables} gives the
exact fields.

\subsection{Hourly alignment and fitting}
\label{subsec:powerdata-fitting}

Fitting a line needs paired observations, so power and CPU have to be put on
the same clock. For each cell and hour, power is the arithmetic mean of valid
\codepath{measured_power_util} rows, dropping any row flagged
\codepath{bad_measurement_data}.\footnote{Domains are averaged without weighting
by their capacity, so a small domain counts as much as a large one.} CPU
utilization for the same hour is the average of its twelve five-minute values,
using the filters from Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-utilization}. The two are
matched by cell and integer hour rather than by exact timestamp, because power
timestamps land on five-minute marks while cluster timestamps need not
\cite{googlePowerAnalysisColab}.

Each cell then gets its own straight line, fitted by ordinary least squares:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{p}_{c}(u)
  =
  \alpha_c + \beta_c u,
  \label{eq:powerdata-affine-fit}
\end{equation}
where \(u\) is normalized CPU utilization and \(\widehat{p}_c\) is the predicted
fraction of the cell's power capacity. Read plainly: \(\alpha_c\) is the idle
draw --- what the site pulls with no work at all --- and \(\beta_c\) is how much
more it pulls when driven from idle to fully busy. Their sum \(\alpha_c+\beta_c\)
is the fitted draw at full utilization, which is a prediction from the line
rather than a measured nameplate rating.

A line is a deliberate simplification, and the data would support more:
PowerData2019 ships a \codepath{machine_to_pdu_mapping} that would let CPU be
aggregated per power domain instead of per cell, fitted at five-minute cadence,
and weighted by domain capacity. Appendix~\ref{app:not-taken} records those
options. We keep the line because it stays interpretable after being multiplied
by a megawatt rating, and because its per-cell fits are already reasonable.

\label{subsec:powerdata-coefficients}

\subsection{Reproduction and limitations}
\label{subsec:powerdata-lineage}

Reproducing the fits needs BigQuery billing and access to both public datasets.
Run \codepath{extract_clusterdata2019_full.ipynb} for cells \(a\)--\(d\) and
\codepath{extract_cells_eh.ipynb} for cells \(e\)--\(h\), then merge the
\(e\)--\(h\) records into \codepath{data/power_model_params.json}, which is the
file everything downstream reads. Table~\ref{tab:powerdata-lineage} in
Appendix~\ref{app:source-tables} records that lineage.

What the line leaves out is most of a real facility. It has no cooling control,
no time-varying PUE, no UPS or distribution losses, no on-site storage or
generators, and no network equipment; it is a statistical association between a
cell's CPU utilization and its measured power fraction, not a physical model of
a server. Combined with one month of data from one operator and a
normalized rather than absolute power scale, the honest reading is that this
model captures how a data center's power \emph{responds} to load, and says
nothing about what any particular Google building consumes.

\section{Four-Market Electricity Data}
\label{sec:energy-data}

\subsection{Purpose and calendar}
\label{subsec:energy-purpose-calendar}

The electricity data does two separate jobs, and confusing them is easy.

The \emph{physical} data --- demand, wind, solar, and net load, all in MW --- is
what the objective is built on. It describes how fast each grid was already
changing before any data center was added, which is the thing the schedule is
supposed to improve.

The \emph{economic} data is one number per market-hour: the day-ahead locational
marginal price (LMP), in USD/MWh. This is the wholesale price set the day
before delivery in each market's day-ahead auction. It appears in the results as
a cost comparison and is shown to the agent as context, but it is never part of
what the agent is rewarded for. We use the day-ahead price rather than the
real-time price for a simple reason: the day-ahead price is published before the
hour it applies to, so a scheduler could actually know it when deciding. The
real-time price is not available in advance and is never used.

Four markets are used: CAISO NP15 (northern California), MISO Minnesota, SPP
North, and ISO-NE NEMA (greater Boston). All four physical series come from the
same EIA schema, so they are directly comparable; the prices come from each
operator's own reports.

The calendar spans September 2025 through February 2026, and its role is worth
being precise about. It supplies \emph{grid} diversity, not \emph{workload}
diversity. The Borg workload is the same measured May 2019 profile repeated
across every day; what changes day to day is net load, renewable output, price,
and forecast. The agent therefore sees the same demand shapes under many
different grid conditions, which is the intended design: we are asking it to
learn how to respond to the grid, not how to respond to varying workload.

September supplies the history needed to fit forecasts and compute
normalization constants. The 114 days from October 10 through January 31 are
training days, and February's 28 days are held back for validation --- 3,408
decision hours per market, 13,632 rows across the four. No test split has been
assigned, because a sealed test set is only meaningful once the design stops
changing.

different method again.

\subsection{Canonical fields and time semantics}
\label{subsec:energy-canonical}

Every native product is converted to explicit
\codepath{interval_start_utc} and \codepath{interval_end_utc}. Source-specific
interval-beginning or interval-ending semantics, time zone, and daylight-saving
rules are applied before products are joined. For example, MISO is interpreted
using its fixed-EST report convention, while ISO-NE repeated civil hours are
resolved using report order.

The physical table carries \codepath{gross_demand_mw}, \codepath{wind_mw},
\codepath{solar_mw}, and \codepath{net_load_mw}, together with the method used
to derive net load, the market scale defined below, and enough interval and
source metadata to check the joins. The price table keeps total day-ahead LMP,
plus real-time price and the energy, congestion, and loss components where an
operator publishes them; only the total day-ahead price is used. Negative
day-ahead prices are kept as they are --- they are real, they occur when
renewable output exceeds what the system can absorb, and clipping them would
misrepresent exactly the hours this thesis is about.

One rule governs all aggregation: a sub-hourly product may be averaged to an
hour only when every native interval in that hour is present. A duplicate
timestamp, a missing interval, or a short hour raises an error rather than
producing a filled-in value. Nothing is forward-filled or interpolated
anywhere in this pipeline. This is why a defect of 67 missing intervals was
enough to disqualify a source above.

\subsection{Net load, and what it stands in for}
\label{subsec:energy-net-load}

Gross demand is what customers consume. Net load is what is left for
controllable generators to cover after wind and solar have produced whatever
they happened to produce. That residual is the quantity an operator actually
dispatches against, and it is the quantity this thesis targets. Using gross
demand instead would hide most of the interesting movement, because the midday
solar trough and the evening climb are created by the renewables being
subtracted.

For every market, hourly net load is computed once:
\begin{equation}
  N_{m,t}
  =
  D_{m,t} - W_{m,t} - Q_{m,t},
  \label{eq:energy-net-load}
\end{equation}
where \(D\) is EIA balancing-authority demand, \(W\) is wind generation, and
\(Q\) is solar generation in MW.

Net load is also a rough indicator of emissions pressure. When renewable output
drops or demand climbs, the extra residual is usually picked up by dispatchable
plants, and in most U.S.\ systems a large share of that marginal response burns
fossil fuel. Hours of high or fast-rising net load are therefore hours in which
additional emissions are more likely.

That is an indicator, not a measurement, and the distinction matters for what
this thesis may claim. The residual can equally be met by nuclear, hydro,
imports, or storage, and our data contains no generator dispatch, fuel burn, or
emissions factors of any kind. What gets optimized is net-load ramp; no
conversion to tonnes of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is made or implied.

One data-cleaning note: EIA's bulk series occasionally reports a small negative
wind or solar value, an artifact of how generation is netted rather than a real
quantity. These are clipped to zero before
Equation~\eqref{eq:energy-net-load}, with the raw minimum and affected-hour
count retained. It affects 2,780 CAISO solar hours, with a raw minimum of
\(-66\) MW against a market of tens of gigawatts; no other series needed it.

\subsection{Causal forecast construction}
\label{subsec:energy-forecasts}

A scheduler that can only see the present is badly handicapped: deciding whether
to hold batch work for an hour is much easier if you have some idea what the
grid will look like in an hour. Real operators publish load forecasts, but none
came with the data we acquired, so we build our own.

The forecaster is a ridge regression --- ordinary linear regression with a
penalty on large coefficients --- predicting gross demand and net load from
features that are all observable at the time of prediction: the values 1, 3, 24,
and 168 hours ago, rolling means over the last 24 and 168 hours, and sine/cosine
encodings of hour-of-day and day-of-week.\footnote{The 168-hour features are
weekly: the same hour one week earlier is a good predictor of electricity
demand, since demand follows a strong weekly cycle.}

The word \emph{causal} in this section's title is the part that requires care.
It would be easy, and wrong, to fit one forecaster on the whole
October--February panel and use it everywhere. Such a model would have learned
from February while making predictions about October, so the October
``forecasts'' would quietly contain information from the future. Instead a new
model is fitted for each date, trained only on data preceding that date --- an
expanding window. At the February 1 boundary the model is frozen, so every
validation-day forecast comes from a model that saw no validation data.

The agent is shown forecast endpoints at \(t+1\), \(t+2\), and \(t+3\). These
come from forecasts issued at \(t-2\), \(t-1\), and \(t\) respectively, so no
forecast used at hour \(t\) was issued after hour \(t\). Realized future load
and real-time price are never features anywhere in this pipeline.

This forecasting step is entirely separate from the agent's training, and it
happens first: the forecaster estimates what the grid will do, and the agent
later learns what to do about it.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:energy-limitations}

Six months of data, September 2025 through February 2026, supports claims about
those six months and no others: nothing here speaks to a different year or to
long-run seasonal behavior.

The geography is coarse in a specific way. Physical demand is
balancing-authority-wide while price is quoted at a hub, and neither describes
the substation or feeder a real facility would actually connect to. Local
constraints --- the ones that most often determine whether a data center can be
built somewhere --- are invisible here.

The forecast is our reconstruction rather than an operator's published forecast,
so a real deployment would face different forecast errors than these. Day-ahead
LMP is a wholesale price and not the tariff a facility would be billed under.
And there is no carbon data in the panel, so carbon is described but never
optimized.

\section{Experimental Transformations and Modeling Assumptions}
\label{sec:experimental-transformations}

Everything up to here was measured by somebody else. This section is where our
own assumptions enter, and it is deliberately short and explicit so a reader can
see exactly where the line falls. Four things are stipulated: which workload
runs in which market, that the clock is hourly, how long deferrable work may
wait, and how big each facility is. Google, EIA, and the grid operators claim
none of these.

\subsection{Five-minute traces to hourly arrivals}
\label{subsec:transform-hourly-workload}

The experiment runs on an hourly clock, so the five-minute workload has to be
aggregated to match. It is worth separating which parts of that clock are forced
and which are ours, because the two halves have different futures.

Two inputs are hourly because their sources are. EIA's Form EIA-930 publishes at
hourly resolution and nothing finer, so the physical demand, wind, and solar
series cannot be had sub-hourly from that source. Day-ahead prices are hourly
because the day-ahead market product is hourly: all four operators clear one
price per hour, and no finer day-ahead price exists to acquire.

Two inputs are hourly only because we made them so. The workload is natively
five-minute, and PowerData2019 is measured at five-minute cadence and averaged
up by us (Section~\ref{subsec:powerdata-fitting}). Both are aggregated to meet
the grid data, not because anything about them requires it.

Each tier curve holds 8,929 samples. The last one marks the end boundary rather
than a full five-minute interval, so it is dropped, and the remaining 8,928 are
averaged in consecutive groups of twelve. That yields a 744-hour service profile
and a 744-hour batch profile per cell, which is exactly the 31 days of May 2019.
Since each five-minute value is a utilization rate, its twelve-sample mean is
the average demand across that hour, and multiplying by one hour gives the
compute-hours to be served.

The cost of this conversion is real and worth naming: five-minute spikes vanish
into hourly averages, and so does any opportunity to shift work \emph{within} an
hour. The results therefore support claims about hourly scheduling and nothing
finer.

That cost is not permanent, and a five-minute version of this experiment is
obtainable rather than impossible --- but not by simply re-running the pipeline
at a finer cadence. It would require replacing EIA with per-operator physical
feeds, since several operators do publish sub-hourly demand and generation:
CAISO's public history is five-minute, and SPP's GenMix snapshot is too. Those
feeds were rejected here for completeness and consistency
(Section~\ref{subsec:energy-sources}), not for their cadence, so the obstacle is
data quality rather than data existence. Day-ahead price would stay hourly and
be held constant within the hour, which is faithful to how the product actually
settles. Section~\ref{subsec:conclusion-future} returns to what such a version
would buy.

Those 744-hour profiles are then repeated end to end from a
2025-09-01 UTC epoch to cover the electricity calendar. This keeps the measured
shape of a real month --- its daily rhythm, its weekday/weekend structure ---
while making no claim that this Borg work happened during these grid
conditions. Repeating one month also means the workload has no seasonal trend,
no year-over-year growth, and no unusual regimes.

\subsection{How long batch work may wait}
\label{subsec:transform-deadlines}

This is the weakest assumption in the thesis, and it is load-bearing, so it gets
stated plainly.

The trace tells us which work is deferrable. It does not tell us \emph{how
long} any of it could actually have been deferred, because it records no
required completion time. We therefore choose the four maximum deferral windows
ourselves:
\([H_a,H_b,H_c,H_d]=[2,1,2,3]\) hours. These numbers come from us, not from
Google, not from any published SLO, and not from any statistic computed on the
trace. They exist so that the four sites differ in flexibility.

The semantics are inclusive counts of hourly opportunities. \(H_m=1\) means
batch arriving in hour \(t\) must run during hour \(t\). \(H_m=2\) allows hour
\(t\) or \(t+1\); \(H_m=3\) allows any of three consecutive hours. Because the
whole model is hourly, it cannot tell a task that could wait ten minutes from
one that could wait fifty, and does not try to.

A better version of this experiment would measure deferral tolerance rather than
assume it. The trace's \codepath{collection_events} records job submission and
scheduling as separate event types, so the wait a job actually tolerated in
production is in principle recoverable; deriving deadlines that way is the
single most valuable extension to this work
(Section~\ref{subsec:conclusion-future}).

One feasibility check on the raw arrivals: the largest simultaneous fleet-wide
service-plus-batch arrival is 2.4244 normalized work units against a fleet
capacity of 4, so the workload always fits even if nothing is deferred. Nothing
is capped, reclassified, or smoothed on the way in.

\subsection{Facility size: 500 MW per site}
\label{subsec:transform-scaling}

The workload is normalized --- a site's compute capacity is one unit of work per
hour --- and the power model is normalized too, reporting fractions rather than
watts. Somewhere a real number has to be chosen, and this is where.

Each site is rated \(R_c=500\) MW, giving a four-site fleet of 2,000 MW. That is
a plausible size for a large modern data center campus, and it is our choice
rather than a figure Google disclosed. It matters mainly through the ratios in
Table~\ref{tab:energy-market-scales}: it is what makes each site a small
fraction of its market, and therefore what makes the price-taking assumption
reasonable.

\subsection{Conversion from executed work to power}
\label{subsec:transform-runtime-power}

Let \(w_{c,t}\) be service plus batch work executed at site \(c\) during hour
\(t\), and let \(K_c=1\) be site compute capacity. Runtime utilization is
\(w_{c,t}/K_c\), bounded to the fitted range \([0,1]\). The corresponding proxy
power is
\begin{equation}
  P_{c,t}
  =
  500
  \left(
    \alpha_c + \beta_c \frac{w_{c,t}}{K_c}
  \right)
  \text{ MW},
  \label{eq:transform-runtime-power}
\end{equation}
using each cell's coefficients from Table~\ref{tab:powerdata-coefficients}.
This equation is the hinge of the whole simulation: it is the only place where a
scheduling decision becomes a quantity the grid can feel.

Two consequences are worth reading off it. First, because \(\alpha_c\) is around
0.4--0.55, an idle site still draws 190--275 MW; the scheduler can move a few
hundred megawatts around, not two gigawatts. Second, because
\(\alpha_c+\beta_c<1\) in every fitted cell, a fully loaded site draws roughly
434, 462, 483, and 476 MW for cells \(a\)--\(d\) rather than the full 500. We
leave that as fitted rather than rescaling the coefficients to hit 500 MW
exactly, since the fit is the measured quantity and 500 MW is the assumption.

Each episode starts with three hours of warm power computed from the repeated
arrival profile run as-is, which gives the first ramp windows a left endpoint.
After that, power is recomputed each hour from the work the scheduler actually
routed, so nothing is replayed.

\subsection{Where the transformation lives}
\label{subsec:transform-factory}

All of the above is implemented in
\codepath{energy_model_v3/four_market_v2.py} and configured by one file,
\codepath{env/protocols/four_market_v2.yaml}, which contains every number this
section stipulates.

Running it produces a \emph{factory}: one descriptor,
\codepath{output/four_market_v2/factory/factory.json}, and one
\codepath{fixture.json} per day under
\codepath{output/four_market_v2/factory/windows}. A fixture is a self-contained
day --- hourly service and batch arrivals, deadlines, warm power history, site
parameters --- plus a reference to the market panel rows it uses. Each day is
three warm hours, 24 decision hours, and a three-hour tail, for reasons given in
Section~\ref{subsec:problem-episode}.

Building and validating are separate steps on purpose: the builder writes
fixtures, and a \codepath{preflight} pass checks their structure, capacity,
calendar membership, and causal boundaries before any training run is allowed to
consume them.

\section{Problem Statement and System Boundary}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

This section states the scheduling problem: what is fixed, what may be changed,
and what counts as a legal schedule. It says nothing about reinforcement
learning. That separation is deliberate --- the problem below could be attacked
with a heuristic, a mixed-integer program, or an agent, and
Section~\ref{sec:optimization-objective} explains why we chose an agent.

\subsection{What is given}
\label{subsec:problem-given-inputs}

Write \(\mathcal{M}=\{1,\ldots,4\}\) for the four sites. Each site is paired
with exactly one market, so the index \(m\) names both a data center and the
grid it sits in. Write \(t\) for the hour being decided.

Everything the problem receives as fixed input has already been built in
Sections~\ref{sec:clusterdata2019}--\ref{sec:experimental-transformations}:
hourly service and batch arrivals \(s_{m,t}\) and \(q_{m,t}\), the deferral
windows \(H_m\), site capacity \(K_m\) and rated power, the power-model
coefficients, the four grids' net load \(N_{m,t}\) and day-ahead price
\(L^{\mathrm{DA}}_{m,t}\), the causal forecasts, the training-only scales, and
three hours of warm power history.
Table~\ref{tab:problem-given-inputs} in Appendix~\ref{app:given-inputs} indexes
all of them against the section and file that produced each.

These are assembled once into
\codepath{output/four_market_v2/factory/factory.json} and the daily fixtures it
points at, so a training run reads a fixture rather than re-deriving anything.

One simplification runs through all of it: work is treated as a divisible
quantity, like a fluid. There are no individual jobs, no applications, no users,
no machine types, and no placement decisions inside a site. ``Send 0.3 units of
batch work to Minnesota'' is a meaningful instruction in this model in a way it
would not be in a real cluster, where work arrives in indivisible pieces with
memory footprints and data dependencies. This is the standard simplification for
load-shaping studies, and it makes the scheduler's decisions continuous, which
matters for the method in Section~\ref{sec:optimization-objective}.

\subsection{What the scheduler controls}
\label{subsec:problem-decisions}

At each hour, a feasible scheduler may control three quantities:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Service destination.} It chooses how the fleet-wide total of
        immediate service is divided among the four sites.
  \item \textbf{Batch execution volume.} It chooses how much queued batch work
        to execute now rather than retain for a later hour.
  \item \textbf{Batch destination.} It chooses where the selected batch work is
        executed.
\end{enumerate}

Write \(x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}\) and \(x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t}\) for the service
and batch work executed at site \(m\), and \(z_t=\sum_m x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t}\)
for total batch execution that hour.

Origins are handled asymmetrically, for a reason. Service work is pooled the
moment it arrives, so once it is redistributed the model no longer tracks where
it came from --- it all has to run this hour anyway, so origin carries no
information. Batch work is different: its origin determines its deadline, so
the queue tracks it, and a transport matrix \(\Pi_{i,j,t}\) records how much
moved from origin \(i\) to execution site \(j\).

These are three operational levers. Turning them into something an agent can
emit takes a specific encoding, described in
Section~\ref{subsec:objective-decoder}.

\subsection{The rules work must obey}
\label{subsec:problem-workload-semantics}

Service work can be moved between sites but never between hours: whatever
arrives this hour runs this hour, somewhere, so
\(\sum_m x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}=\sum_m s_{m,t}\).

Batch work goes into a queue instead. The queue is
\emph{earliest-deadline-first} (EDF): when the scheduler decides to run some
amount of batch work, the work with the nearest deadline goes first. Each entry
keeps both its origin, which fixes its deadline, and the absolute hour by which
it must finish. Writing \(Q_t\) for the queued amount before execution, the
bookkeeping is what goes in minus what comes out,
\(Q_{t+1}=Q_t+\sum_m q_{m,t}-z_t\).

Three properties of this queue are non-negotiable, and are enforced rather than
encouraged: it conserves work exactly, nothing runs later than its deadline, and
it is empty when the day ends. The implementation is \codepath{EDFQueue} in
\codepath{env/ramp_v6/models.py}. Because these are hard constraints, a
scheduler cannot buy a better ramp by quietly dropping or delaying work --- an
important property when interpreting the results.

\subsection{Capacity and movement}
\label{subsec:problem-feasibility}

No site can run more work than it has capacity for, whatever the scheduler
prefers, so \(0\leq x^{\mathrm{svc}}_{m,t}+x^{\mathrm{bat}}_{m,t}\leq K_m\) for
every site and hour.

Movement between sites is tracked by the matrix \(\Pi_{i,j,t}\), whose row sums
equal the amount the EDF queue drew from each origin and whose column sums equal
the batch work executed at each destination. That is all it does: what leaves
the origins equals what arrives at the destinations. \(\Pi\) is an accounting
device, not a network model --- it has no notion of distance, and no route is
cheaper than any other.

Beyond capacity, routing is unrestricted --- but the scheduler is prevented from
routing itself into a corner. Before allowing a small amount of batch work to
run now, the decoder computes how much capacity future hours will need in order
to still meet every deadline in the queue, and forces enough work out now to
keep that promise satisfiable. This reservation is computed from arrivals and
deadlines alone. No grid condition, however favorable, can waive a deadline.

What the model omits here is substantial and all in the same direction. There is
no network latency, no bandwidth limit, no data-residency rule, no energy cost
for moving data, and no charge of any kind for relocating work between
continents. Every one of those would restrict spatial flexibility in reality, so
the geographic half of the scheduler's freedom should be read as an optimistic
upper bound.

\subsection{Episode and terminal boundary}
\label{subsec:problem-episode}

Each episode represents one UTC day with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item three preceding canonical-panel hours for ramp history;
  \item 24 active arrival and decision hours; and
  \item a three-hour terminal tail with no new arrivals.
\end{itemize}

The three warm hours exist so the first decision can be scored: a one-hour ramp
needs an hour to compare against, and a three-hour ramp needs three. The tail
exists for the mirror-image reason, and for one more --- it gives batch work
deferred late in the day somewhere to land, and it lets ramp windows that end
after the last arrival still be counted. The day never wraps around to its own
start. The queue begins empty and must end empty.

Each day is simulated independently. Nothing carries across the boundary: no
queue, no power history, no ramp state. Because consecutive days share hours ---
one day's tail covers the same clock hours as the next day's first decisions ---
this makes the calendar a set of repeated samples of market conditions rather
than one continuous operating record. That is a real modeling seam, and it means
no claim in this thesis is a claim about continuous multi-day operation.
Appendix~\ref{app:episode-seams} works through what the seam does and does not
affect, and what fixing it would cost. The rules are generated in
\codepath{energy_model_v3/four_market_v2.py} and enforced in
\codepath{env/ramp_v6/environment.py}.

\subsection{What the scheduler is allowed to know}
\label{subsec:problem-causality}

A scheduler that could see the future would be easy to build and worthless, so
the information available at hour \(t\) is restricted to what a real operator
would have at hour \(t\):
\begin{itemize}
  \item current and three trailing hours of observed gross and net load;
  \item native 1 h and 3 h ramps that have already closed;
  \item forecast endpoints whose issue time is no later than \(t\);
  \item current service and batch arrivals, queued work, and deadlines;
  \item previous modeled site power;
  \item current day-ahead LMP; and
  \item hour, day-of-week, and episode-progress features.
\end{itemize}

Realized future load, realized future real-time price, and any forecast issued
after \(t\) are off limits. A scheduling rule may therefore be written
\(\mu_t(\mathcal{I}_t)\), a function of that hour's information set and nothing
else.

\subsection{The grid does not react}
\label{subsec:problem-grid-boundary}

The four net-load trajectories and their prices are fixed inputs. Fleet power is
added on top of them to evaluate what the grid would have seen, but the
underlying grid never responds: no generator redispatches, no price moves, no
renewable output changes, and no forecast is revised because of anything the
data centers did.

This is the price-taking assumption from
Section~\ref{subsec:energy-scales}, and at 0.5--2.7\% of each market it is
reasonable. It is still an assumption, and it caps what these results can mean:
a real fleet large enough to matter would, by mattering, change the thing it was
optimizing against.

\subsection{The three systems being compared}
\label{subsec:problem-baselines}

Every result in this thesis is a comparison among three systems, and keeping
them distinct is essential to reading Section~\ref{sec:campaign}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Native grid.} \(N_{m,t}\) without any modeled data-center power.
        This is a physical counterfactual, not an operating policy.
  \item \textbf{Status quo.} The native grid plus a fleet that runs everything
        where it arrived, as soon as it arrived. It obeys the same capacity,
        conservation, and deadline rules, and keeps its own separate queue, but
        it never looks at the grid. This is what an ordinary data center does,
        and it is the comparison that matters.
  \item \textbf{Candidate policy.} The native grid plus a fleet scheduled by the
        trained agent, under those same feasibility rules.
\end{enumerate}

Since every result is a difference against status quo, its definition should be
precise rather than descriptive. It runs each hour's service work at the site
that received it, and drains the batch queue as fast as capacity allows:
\begin{align}
  x^{\mathrm{svc},\mathrm{sq}}_{m,t} &= s_{m,t}, \\
  z^{\mathrm{sq}}_t &= \min\!\left(Q_t + \textstyle\sum_m q_{m,t},\;
                       \textstyle\sum_m \left(K_m - s_{m,t}\right)\right),
  \label{eq:status-quo}
\end{align}
with batch work executed at its origin in the same proportions, subject to
capacity. In words: nothing waits and nothing travels. The queue exists only
because a site can be too full to drain it in one hour.

Status quo and candidate are run on identical days, with identical workload,
forecasts, capacity, and deadlines, and the baseline keeps its own separate
queue so the two never interact. The only difference between them is the
scheduling decision. The implementation is \codepath{_status_quo_action} in
\codepath{env/ramp_v6/environment.py}, which reaches the behavior above by
feeding the same decoder a fixed preference vector; the comparison is
aggregated in \codepath{ramp_rl/evaluation.py}.

The two comparisons answer different questions. Against the native grid: does
adding this fleet make the grid's ramps better or worse? Against status quo:
given that the fleet exists and has to run this work regardless, does scheduling
it intelligently beat not bothering? The second is the operationally meaningful
question, and it is the one the headline result reports.

\subsection{Statement of the problem}
\label{subsec:problem-formal-statement}

One property of the experimental design is worth restating before the formal
statement, because it bounds the claim. The many calendar days supply many
\emph{grid} conditions, so no result depends on one unusually convenient day.
They do not supply many workloads: one May 2019 profile repeats throughout. The
114 October--January days are for learning and the 28 February days are for
assessment on grid conditions never trained on. Whether any of this transfers to
a different workload is what cells \(e\)--\(h\) are reserved for, and is not
tested here.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Problem statement.}
Given four hourly raw service and batch arrival streams, provisional batch
deadlines, four capacity and power models, causal physical-grid and forecast
information, and day-ahead prices, construct a causal hourly schedule for
service destination, batch execution volume, and batch destination such that:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item all immediate service is completed in its arrival hour;
  \item all batch work is conserved and completed by its deadline;
  \item no site exceeds compute capacity and the terminal queue is empty; and
  \item the fleet's added power reduces, or at minimum does not amplify, the
        native grid's rapid net-load changes across the four market cases.
\end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

Note what this statement leaves open. It says nothing about reinforcement
learning, neural networks, the choice of ramp horizons, or how the objective is
turned into a single number. All of that is one possible answer, and it is what
the next section describes. Note also what is absent from the four conditions:
cost. Energy cost is measured and reported, but it is not something a schedule
must satisfy to be legal.

\section{Method: Objective, Agent, and Training}
\label{sec:optimization-objective}

This section describes how we attack the problem above. It introduces
reinforcement learning from scratch, and assumes no prior familiarity.

\subsection{The decision loop}
\label{subsec:objective-hourly-decision}

A simulated day proceeds one hour at a time, and each hour has the same four
steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The agent \textbf{observes} 100 numbers describing the four grids, the
        four sites, the queue, and the time
        (Section~\ref{subsec:objective-observations}).
  \item The agent \textbf{emits} 9 numbers --- four expressing where service
        work should go, one expressing how much queued batch work to run now,
        and four expressing where that batch work should go.
  \item A \textbf{decoder} turns those 9 numbers into an actual work
        assignment that is guaranteed legal
        (Section~\ref{subsec:objective-decoder}). The agent never sets megawatts
        directly, and never has the opportunity to propose something infeasible.
  \item The executed work is converted to power by
        Equation~\eqref{eq:transform-runtime-power}, the ramp windows ending at
        this hour are scored, and the agent receives a single number as
        \textbf{reward}.
\end{enumerate}

Scoring happens after the hour is executed, using only power that has already
happened. This is what keeps the reward causal: it compares the hour just
completed against hours already past, and never consults a future value.

\subsection{Meaning of the 1 h and 3 h horizons}
\label{subsec:objective-horizons}

The horizon \(h\) is the distance between the two endpoints of a ramp:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(h=1\) compares hour \(t\) with hour \(t-1\), capturing a fast
        hour-to-hour change; and
  \item \(h=3\) compares hour \(t\) with hour \(t-3\), capturing a broader
        multi-hour rise or fall.
\end{itemize}

Dividing by \(h\) expresses both as an average change per hour, so the two
horizons are comparable. The objective gives 40\% weight to the 1 h horizon
and 60\% to the 3 h horizon. This favors sustained multi-hour smoothing while
still penalizing sharp hourly changes.

These horizons are experimental choices rather than universal grid constants.
One hour matches the scheduling and source-data resolution. Three hours matches
the longest batch deadline, the causal forecast trajectory, and the window over
which an evening renewable decline can become a sustained ramp. Using both
prevents a schedule from smoothing a single hour while merely shifting the
change into the next few hours. The 40/60 weights place more emphasis on that
sustained effect and should ultimately be tested by sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Native and data-center-adjusted ramps}
\label{subsec:objective-native-adjusted}

A ramp is a change in megawatts, and megawatts are not comparable across grids
five times apart in size (Section~\ref{subsec:energy-scales}). Before ramps from
four markets can be averaged, each needs to be expressed relative to its own
market. We therefore define one reference demand per market, the 95th percentile
of its gross demand over the training period:
\begin{equation}
  S_m
  =
  Q_{0.95}
  \left(
    D_{m,t}: t \in \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{train}}
  \right).
  \label{eq:energy-market-scale}
\end{equation}
The 95th percentile is a busy hour rather than the single busiest, so the
constant does not depend on one outlier. Its values are in
Table~\ref{tab:energy-market-scales}.

Two things \(S_m\) is not. It is not published by anyone --- EIA reports
megawatts, and this percentile is computed from them and then frozen. And it
does not rescale the fleet: site power stays in absolute megawatts throughout
(Equation~\eqref{eq:transform-runtime-power}). Nothing is shrunk to fit a
market; only the scoring changes units.

Now let \(N_{m,t}\) be the physical net load in market \(m\) before adding the
modeled fleet and \(P_{m,t}\) the fleet power assigned to that market. The two
normalized ramps are
\begin{align}
  b_{m,t,h}
    &= \frac{N_{m,t}-N_{m,t-h}}{S_m h}, \\
  a_{m,t,h}
    &= \frac{(N_{m,t}+P_{m,t})-(N_{m,t-h}+P_{m,t-h})}{S_m h}.
  \label{eq:energy-ramps}
\end{align}

In words: \(b\) is how fast the grid was already changing without our fleet, and
\(a\) is how fast it changes once the fleet's power is added on top. Both are
divided by the same \(S_m\), so within a single market the normalization is a
change of units and nothing more. Dividing by \(h\) puts the two horizons in the
same units of change per hour.

Across markets it is not merely a change of units, because the objective goes on
to square these quantities. Section~\ref{subsec:objective-market-weighting}
works through what that does.

The most important thing about these definitions is what is \emph{inside} the
parentheses. The objective is built on the ramp of \(N+P\), the combined
trajectory the grid actually experiences --- not on the ramp of \(P\) alone.
Nothing in this thesis penalizes a data center for changing its own power draw.

That distinction has a consequence that is easy to get backwards. Swinging the
fleet's power \emph{hard} can be the correct move: cutting data-center power
sharply while the grid is climbing, or raising it sharply while the grid is
falling, makes the combined curve flatter even though it makes the data
center's own curve steeper. Conversely, a perfectly flat data center is merely
neutral --- it neither helps nor hurts. The agent is rewarded for opposing the
grid's motion, not for being steady.

\subsection{Why ramps are squared}
\label{subsec:objective-squared-ramp}

Ramps enter the objective squared, for two reasons.

Squaring removes the sign. A grid climbing at 2 GW/h and a grid falling at
2 GW/h both require generation to move quickly, and both are burdens; without
squaring they would cancel, and a schedule could look perfect by causing equal
amounts of up-ramp and down-ramp.

Squaring also makes big ramps count for much more than small ones. One 4 GW/h
ramp scores sixteen times a 1 GW/h ramp, not four times. That matches how the
burden actually behaves: a grid absorbs many small changes comfortably and
struggles with a single large one, so an objective that treated them
proportionally would let the agent trade away the hours that matter most.

Note that \(b^2\) alone is never minimized. The fleet did not cause the grid's
native ramps and cannot be credited or blamed for them; \(b^2\) enters only as
the baseline that gets subtracted next.

What squared ramp is \emph{not} is money. It is a dimensionless severity index.
The real cost of a ramp depends on which generators respond, what their startup
and fuel costs are, and what reserves and congestion look like at that moment ---
none of which is in this model. Cost is measured separately, in dollars, in
Section~\ref{subsec:objective-energy-cost}, and the two are never combined into
one number.

\subsection{Per-window incremental impact and PPO reward}
\label{subsec:objective-incremental-impact}

For one market, one endpoint, and one horizon, the incremental impact is
\begin{equation}
  I_{m,t,h}=a_{m,t,h}^2-b_{m,t,h}^2.
  \label{eq:energy-incremental-impact}
\end{equation}
A negative value means fleet power reduced the squared native ramp, zero is
ramp-neutral, and a positive value worsened it. The active impact at hour
\(t\) is the equal-market mean of the two weighted horizons:
\begin{equation}
  \overline{I}_t=
  \frac{1}{4}\sum_{m=1}^{4}
  \left(0.40I_{m,t,1}+0.60I_{m,t,3}\right).
  \label{eq:objective-primary-impact}
\end{equation}
The reward the agent receives is simply the negative of that number:
\begin{equation}
  r_t=-\overline{I}_t.
  \label{eq:objective-ramp-reward}
\end{equation}

The sign flip is a convention: reinforcement learning maximizes reward, and we
want impact minimized, so making the impact more negative makes the reward more
positive.

This one line is the entire reward function, and its simplicity is a design
choice worth being explicit about. There is no penalty term for violating a
constraint, no cost term, no weighted combination of objectives, and no special
reward at the end of the day. Feasibility is not something the agent is
encouraged toward and occasionally fails at --- it is enforced by the decoder
before the action is ever executed, so every action the agent can take is legal
by construction. That leaves the reward free to express one thing only. The
three tail hours, which have no arrivals, are scored with this same reward.

\subsection{What the market scaling does to the objective}
\label{subsec:objective-market-weighting}

Equation~\eqref{eq:objective-primary-impact} averages the four markets with
equal weight, and we have been calling it an equal-market mean. That
describes the arithmetic but not the incentive it creates, and the difference
matters enough to derive.

Write \(\Delta N_{m,t,h}=N_{m,t}-N_{m,t-h}\) for the native change in megawatts
over a window and \(\Delta P_{m,t,h}=P_{m,t}-P_{m,t-h}\) for the fleet's change
over the same window. Substituting the ramp definitions of
Equation~\eqref{eq:energy-ramps} into
Equation~\eqref{eq:energy-incremental-impact}, the squared native term cancels
and leaves
\begin{equation}
  I_{m,t,h}
  =
  \frac{2\,\Delta N_{m,t,h}\,\Delta P_{m,t,h}+\Delta P_{m,t,h}^{2}}
       {\left(S_m h\right)^{2}}.
  \label{eq:objective-scale-sensitivity}
\end{equation}

The numerator is entirely in megawatts and knows nothing about market size. The
denominator carries \(S_m^{2}\). So for a fixed physical action under fixed grid
conditions, a market's contribution to the objective scales as \(1/S_m^{2}\):
normalization divides by \(S_m\) once, and squaring does it again.

The four scales differ by a factor of 5.2, so the implied weights differ by a
factor of 27. One megawatt shifted in ISO-NE moves the reward 27 times as much
as the same megawatt shifted in MISO, with CAISO at 7.8 and SPP at 4.9 times
MISO. These weights were never chosen. They are a by-product of normalizing and
then squaring, and they are the sense in which the equal-market mean is not
equal.

Whether they are wrong depends on a question this thesis does not settle. If a
ramp's harm is proportional to system size --- a gigawatt swing being a larger
event in a 17.6 GW system than in a 91.5 GW one --- then weighting small markets
more heavily is defensible, and \(1/S_m^{2}\) is at least the right direction. If
instead the harm is absolute, since a megawatt of ramp requires a megawatt of
fast generation wherever it occurs, then the markets should be weighted by
\(S_m^{2}\) to cancel the effect entirely. Both positions are coherent; this
design implicitly takes the first, more strongly than anyone intended.

The consequence is visible in the results, and
Section~\ref{subsec:campaign-markets} returns to it. Testing the alternative
weightings is left to future work
(Section~\ref{subsec:conclusion-future}); nothing here has been reweighted after
the fact.

\subsection{Energy cost, measured but not optimized}
\label{subsec:objective-energy-cost}

Cost is deliberately kept out of the reward and reported afterwards, so that the
ramp result is not quietly a cost result. It is computed for the policy and for
status quo over identical hours:
\begin{align}
  C_{m,t}&=L^{\mathrm{DA}}_{m,t}P_{m,t}(1\ \mathrm{hour}), \\
  C_t&=\sum_{m=1}^{4}C_{m,t}, \qquad
  \rho_C=\frac{\sum_t C_t^{\mathrm{policy}}}
                 {\sum_t C_t^{\mathrm{status\ quo}}}.
  \label{eq:energy-cost}
\end{align}
\(L^{\mathrm{DA}}\) is in USD/MWh and \(P\) in MW, so \(C_{m,t}\) is dollars.
The ratio \(\rho_C\) is the interesting quantity: above 1.0 the policy spent
more than status quo, below 1.0 it spent less. Because the agent is never told
to care about this, \(\rho_C\) is a genuine side effect rather than a target,
which makes it informative --- it reveals what smoothing ramps happens to cost.

This is a wholesale energy price only. A real facility's bill would also include
demand charges, ratchets, capacity payments, taxes, and whatever its contract
says, several of which would react quite differently to a load-shifting
schedule.

\subsection{What the agent sees}
\label{subsec:objective-observations}

Each hour the agent receives a vector of 100 numbers, broken down in
Table~\ref{tab:objective-observations}. Everything in it passes the causality
test of Section~\ref{subsec:problem-causality}: it is either already observed,
or a forecast issued no later than now.

Observations do not dictate actions. Nothing in this vector tells the agent what
to do; the mapping from these 100 numbers to the 9 outputs is entirely learned.

The forecast entries deserve one clarification, because they are the only
forward-looking numbers in the vector. Forecasts influence what the agent
\emph{does}; they never influence what it is \emph{paid}. Reward is computed
from realized net load and executed power once a ramp window has closed. This is
what lets the agent act in anticipation --- holding batch work because it
expects a climb --- without ever being scored against a future it cannot know.

Some things are deliberately absent. Site constants such as capacity and rated
power never change, so including them would add inputs carrying no information.
Forecast age, vintage, and error metadata are omitted. Forecast bins beyond
three hours are omitted because three hours is the longest deadline in the
protocol, so a six-hour forecast could not be acted on anyway.

\subsection{From 9 numbers to a legal schedule}
\label{subsec:objective-decoder}

Write the agent's output as \(a\in[-6,6]^9\). These are preferences, not
megawatts: a decoder maps them to the executed allocation, and it is this map
that makes the feasibility rules of Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement} true by
construction rather than by training.

Two building blocks appear repeatedly. The softmax turns any four reals into
four positive shares summing to one,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(v)_m=\frac{e^{v_m}}{\sum_{m'}e^{v_{m'}}},
  \label{eq:decoder-softmax}
\end{equation}
and the capped-simplex projection returns the feasible allocation closest to a
desired one,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{proj}_{[0,u]}^{T}(d)=\arg\min_{x}\;\lVert x-d\rVert_2^2
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \textstyle\sum_m x_m=T,\;\; 0\le x\le u.
  \label{eq:decoder-projection}
\end{equation}
Projection matters here rather than simple rescaling: it keeps the executed
split as close as possible to what the agent asked for, in the least-squares
sense, while satisfying the constraint exactly.

\textbf{Service placement} uses the first four outputs. With
\(S_t=\sum_m s_{m,t}\) the hour's total service work and \(K\) the capacity
vector,
\begin{equation}
  x^{\mathrm{svc}}_t=\mathrm{proj}_{[0,K]}^{S_t}\!\left(S_t\,\sigma(a_{1:4})\right),
  \qquad
  \rho_t = K - x^{\mathrm{svc}}_t,
  \label{eq:decoder-service}
\end{equation}
where \(\rho_t\) is the residual capacity left for batch work.

\textbf{Batch volume} uses the fifth output, mapped linearly onto \([0,1]\) and
scaled by the queue, then clipped between bounds the agent does not control:
\begin{equation}
  z_t=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{a_5+6}{12}\,Q_t,\;\underline{z}_t,\;\overline{z}_t\right),
  \qquad
  \overline{z}_t=\min\!\left(Q_t,\;\textstyle\sum_m \rho_{m,t}\right).
  \label{eq:decoder-batch-volume}
\end{equation}
The ceiling \(\overline{z}_t\) is the obvious one. The floor is the interesting
one: it is the least amount that must run now for every deadline still in the
queue to remain satisfiable later,
\begin{equation}
  \underline{z}_t=\max_{d}\;\left(D_t(d)-F_t(d)\right)^{+},
  \label{eq:decoder-floor}
\end{equation}
where \(D_t(d)\) is queued work due by deadline \(d\) and \(F_t(d)\) is the
capacity guaranteed available before \(d\). At the final step \(\underline{z}_t\)
becomes the whole queue, which is what forces the terminal queue empty. An agent
requesting zero still runs \(\underline{z}_t\); one requesting everything still
respects \(\overline{z}_t\).

\textbf{Batch placement} uses the last four outputs against the residual
capacity,
\begin{equation}
  x^{\mathrm{bat}}_t=\mathrm{proj}_{[0,\rho_t]}^{z_t}\!\left(z_t\,\sigma(a_{6:9})\right),
  \label{eq:decoder-batch-placement}
\end{equation}
after which a deterministic northwest-corner transport step matches the origins
the EDF queue drew from against these destinations, yielding \(\Pi_{i,j,t}\)
with the required row and column sums.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: \emph{no action in \([-6,6]^9\) drops
work, misses a deadline, or overloads a site}. This is why the reward can be a
single clean term with no penalty machinery --- there is nothing to penalize ---
and why a poorly trained agent degrades toward mediocre-but-legal behavior
instead of toward violations.

One diagnostic falls out of this design and is worth noting for later work. The
environment records the Euclidean distance between what the agent requested and
what the projection executed (\codepath{semantic_adjustment_l2} in
\codepath{env/ramp_v6/projection.py}), which measures how often the agent asks
for something infeasible. It is written per step but not aggregated across the
campaign, so no value is reported here; aggregating it would show whether the
agent learned the constraints or is merely being clamped by them.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning, and why we use it}
\label{subsec:objective-rl}

Reinforcement learning is the branch of machine learning concerned with making
sequences of decisions. An \emph{agent} observes a state, takes an action,
receives a numerical reward, and observes the next state; over many repetitions
it adjusts a \emph{policy} --- the rule mapping observations to actions --- to
collect more reward. Nobody supplies correct answers. The agent is never told
what the best schedule for a given hour would have been; it only ever learns
that some behaviors scored better than others.

That is a good fit here for one specific reason: the decision at each hour has
consequences the current hour does not reveal. Holding batch work back is a bet
that the next few hours will be a better time to run it, and it consumes
capacity that later hours will need. Getting this right requires trading present
reward against future reward under uncertainty, which is exactly the problem
class reinforcement learning addresses.

An alternative would be to solve each day as an optimization problem directly.
With perfect knowledge of the whole day, a mixed-integer program would find the
genuinely optimal schedule --- but it would be cheating, because it would know
the evening's net load while deciding the morning's. Such a solution is a useful
upper bound and we recommend computing it (Section~\ref{subsec:conclusion-future}),
but it is not something an operator could run. The agent, by contrast, sees only
what Section~\ref{subsec:problem-causality} allows.

\subsection{PPO}
\label{subsec:objective-ppo}

We train with Proximal Policy Optimization \cite{schulman2017ppo}, using the
implementation in Stable-Baselines3 \cite{raffin2021sb3}. PPO is the default
choice for continuous-action control problems of this size, and the reasons are
worth understanding rather than taking on faith.

PPO is a \emph{policy gradient} method: it represents the policy as a neural
network and nudges the network's weights in whichever direction made
better-than-expected actions more likely. The naive version of this idea is
unstable. A single large update can push the policy somewhere much worse, and
because the agent generates its own training data by acting, a bad policy then
collects bad data and the run never recovers. PPO's contribution is a
\emph{clipped objective}: it measures how much more likely the new policy makes
an action compared to the old one, and refuses to reward changes beyond a fixed
ratio. The policy improves in small, trust-worthy steps.

PPO is \emph{actor-critic}: alongside the policy network (the actor) it trains a
second network (the critic) that predicts how much reward to expect from a given
state. The difference between what actually happened and what the critic
predicted is the \emph{advantage} --- was this action better or worse than
business as usual? --- and it is the advantage, not the raw reward, that drives
learning. This matters here, because our rewards are dominated by the grid's own
behavior: a calm hour scores well no matter what the agent does. Subtracting the
critic's prediction strips that out and leaves the part the agent was
responsible for.

Two entries are unusual enough to explain.

The discount factor is \(\gamma=1.0\), meaning future reward is not discounted
at all. Discounting is normally used to keep returns finite in problems that run
forever; our episodes are 27 steps long, so returns are finite regardless. More
importantly, an hour of grid stress at 8 p.m.\ is worth neither more nor less
than one at 9 a.m., and any \(\gamma<1\) would assert otherwise.

Reward normalization matters more than it might appear. The raw rewards from
Equation~\eqref{eq:objective-ramp-reward} are on the order of \(10^{-5}\), which
is far too small for stable gradient steps at a \(3\times10^{-4}\) learning rate.
\codepath{VecNormalize} rescales rewards by a running estimate of their standard
deviation, which fixes the scale problem without changing which behaviors are
preferred. The learning curves in Section~\ref{subsec:campaign-learning} report
raw, un-normalized impact, so what is plotted is the physical quantity rather
than the rescaled training signal.

Each training run therefore proceeds as follows. Four copies of the environment
run in parallel, each stepping through randomly drawn training days. Every 2,048
collected steps, PPO performs an update: ten passes over that data in minibatches
of 256. Training continues to roughly two million interactions. At evaluation the
policy acts deterministically, taking the mean of its action distribution rather
than sampling, so an evaluated policy gives the same schedule every time.

\section{Ten-Seed Validation Campaign and Results}
\label{sec:campaign}

A single training run proves very little. Reinforcement learning is stochastic:
the network's initial weights, the order days are drawn in, and the sampling of
actions during training all depend on a random seed, and two runs of identical
code can land in noticeably different places. A result from one seed may be a
property of the method or may be luck.

We therefore train ten policies, seeds 4101--4110, changing nothing but the
seed. Same protocol, same workload, same factory, same observations, same
reward, same baseline. Each requests 2,000,000 interactions and completes
without interruption; the actual figure is 2,045,952, because training is
rounded up to a boundary where both the 2,048-step update cycle and the
27-step episode end together.\footnote{The rounding quantum is the least common
multiple of \(4\times27\) and \(4\times512\), namely 55,296; 37 such blocks give
2,045,952.} All ten train on the same 114 October--January days and are
evaluated on the same 28 February days. Cells \(e\)--\(h\) are never touched, and
no test calendar exists to touch.

\subsection{How the comparison is made}
\label{subsec:campaign-statistics}

Within each seed, the trained policy and the status-quo baseline are run over
the same 28 validation days, and the difference between their mean ramp impacts
is recorded. That gives ten paired differences, one per seed.

The seed is the unit of analysis, not the hour and not the day. This is the
conservative choice and it costs statistical power: with \(n=10\) the strongest
result obtainable is a \(p\)-value of about 0.002. Treating each of the 3,000-odd
validation hours as independent would produce far smaller \(p\)-values and would
be wrong, because hours within a day are correlated, days are shared across
seeds, and the thing whose reliability is in question is the training procedure
itself, of which we have exactly ten samples.

The test is an exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test rather than a
\(t\)-test, because ten values give no basis for assuming normality, and the
Wilcoxon test asks only whether the differences are symmetrically distributed
about zero. Alongside it we report the Hodges--Lehmann shift (a median-based
estimate of the typical paired difference), matched-pairs rank-biserial
correlation oriented so positive favors the policy, and 95\% intervals from
10,000 bootstrap resamples over seeds.

\subsection{Overall paired result}
\label{subsec:campaign-overall}

All ten paired differences are negative. Every trained policy produced less ramp
impact than status quo on the same 28 days.

The magnitudes are worth reading carefully, because the two systems are not
close. Status quo achieves \(-6.4573\times10^{-6}\), meaning that simply adding
this fleet and running it naively already smooths the grid very slightly. The
trained policy achieves \(-7.2618\times10^{-5}\), about eleven times more
improvement, and the gap between them is \(-6.6161\times10^{-5}\). Nearly all
of the benefit therefore comes from scheduling, not from the fleet merely
existing --- which is the comparison that matters, since the fleet exists in
both systems.

Why status quo is slightly negative rather than zero is not established here. A
plausible explanation is that compute demand and electricity demand share a
daily human rhythm, so a naively run data center adds load in a shape that
partly opposes the evening net-load climb. That is a conjecture; testing it
would mean correlating the workload profile against the four net-load profiles,
which we have not done.

that such a policy would work on a different workload, a different year, or a
sealed test set, none of which were touched.

it was cheapest to spend, and the equal-market average concealed the price.

Two further properties of the MISO site point the same way, though they are
secondary. It runs cell \(b\), which has the lowest batch share of the four
cells at 15.70\%, so the least work that can be \emph{delayed}; and cell \(b\)
carries the one-hour deferral window, the shortest in the protocol, so what
little batch work it has can barely be delayed at all. Note that neither
weakness touches the spatial lever --- service work can still be routed away
from the MISO site or toward it --- which is consistent with the agent using
MISO as a destination for load that suits the other three grids.

All of this remains a conjecture, on four points, with market scale confounded
against batch share and deadline length. Separating them requires an ablation
--- varying MISO's deadline, its batch share, or its market scale independently
and observing whether the sign flips --- which was not run. What can be said
without an ablation is that the \(1/S_m^{2}\) weighting is a property of the
objective rather than of the agent, and would have to be addressed by changing
the objective.

The honest summary is that this thesis demonstrates an aggregate improvement
purchased partly at one market's expense. Whether that is acceptable depends
on who is asking: it is fine for an operator optimizing a portfolio and not fine
for a grid operator in Minnesota. Fixing it means changing the objective, not
the training --- either a floor requiring no market to worsen, or per-market
weights --- and that is the most direct extension of this work.

\subsection{What it cost, and what it broke}
\label{subsec:campaign-cost-safety}

Smoothing ramps is not free, and the point of measuring cost separately is to
find out what the bill was.

It was small. Mean day-ahead energy cost under the policy is 1.001233 times
status quo --- a 0.123\% increase --- with seed ratios spanning 0.995687 to
1.007319, and four of the ten seeds were actually cheaper than status quo. This
is the answer to
an obvious objection: the agent could in principle have bought its ramp
improvement by running work during expensive hours, and at a tenth of a percent
it plainly did not. It is also worth noting the sign. Moving load toward hours
when the grid is falling tends to mean moving it toward hours that are cheap,
so ramp-smoothing and cost-saving are not natural enemies here, and a version of
this work that optimized both jointly is a reasonable next step.

The safety accounting needs a word about what it does and does not measure,
because the obvious reading of it is wrong.

Feasibility violations in this environment are not recorded and counted --- they
are raised. Every step asserts that no deadline work remains after mandatory
execution, that no site exceeds capacity, and that the queue conserves work;
the terminal step additionally asserts the queue is empty
(\codepath{env/ramp_v6/environment.py}, lines 482, 485, 506, and 510). Any
violation raises and the episode dies. Correspondingly, the fields
\codepath{batch_expired}, \codepath{certificate_violations}, and
\codepath{emergency_feasibility} are written as constants, because within a
surviving episode they cannot be anything else.

So the meaningful statement is not ``six counters read zero.'' It is that all
280 policy-validation episodes, and all 280 status-quo episodes, ran to
completion without a single assertion firing. The two quantities that are
genuinely computed rather than asserted --- \codepath{service_unserved} and
\codepath{batch_unfinished} --- are also zero throughout.

None of this is surprising given the decoder of
Section~\ref{subsec:objective-decoder}, and it is not offered as evidence that
the agent learned to be safe. It is evidence that the implementation enforces
what the design claims: the ramp improvement in
Section~\ref{subsec:campaign-overall} was not obtained by dropping work,
missing deadlines, or overloading a site, because those outcomes are
unreachable rather than merely unobserved.

The summary numbers are in
\codepath{output/four_market_v2/campaign/aggregation.json}, and the learning
curves in \codepath{learning_curve_aggregate.csv} and
\codepath{learning_curve_aggregate.png} beside it.
Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction} gives the commands that regenerate all of it.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{What was shown}
\label{subsec:conclusion-shown}

A scheduler that decides when and where to run deferrable data-center work can
measurably reduce the ramp burden its fleet places on the electricity grid. Ten
independently trained agents all beat a grid-unaware baseline on the same 28
unseen days, improving the equal-market ramp impact by about an order of
magnitude over the baseline's own small benefit, at a wholesale energy cost
increase of 0.123\% and with no work dropped or delayed past deadline.

The mechanism is worth stating separately from the result, because it is the
part that generalizes. The agent has no control over how much work exists; total
compute is identical under both systems. Its leverage comes from two levers of
unequal reach. \emph{Placement} applies to all of the load: every hour's work,
urgent or not, can be sent to whichever of the four sites suits its grid, and
four grids in different time zones are never in the same state at once.
\emph{Timing} applies only to the 16--26\% that carries no deadline pressure,
and only within a one- to three-hour window. Neither lever requires new
hardware, storage, or on-site generation.

Because the broader lever is the one this model treats most generously ---
movement is free here and would not be in reality --- the placement half of the
result is also the half most in need of a more realistic follow-up.

\subsection{What was not shown}
\label{subsec:conclusion-notshown}

Four limits bound the claim, in rough order of how much they should worry a
reader.

\textbf{The deferral deadlines are invented.} The \([2,1,2,3]\)-hour windows of
Section~\ref{subsec:transform-deadlines} come from us, and the whole result
scales with how much work can wait and for how long. Every number in
Section~\ref{sec:campaign} is conditional on that assumption.

\textbf{One market got worse, and the objective made that likely.} The
aggregate improvement includes MISO worsening in all ten seeds
(Section~\ref{subsec:campaign-markets}). This is not only a missing per-market
floor. Normalizing ramps by market size and then squaring them weights each
market as \(1/S_m^{2}\), so the objective values a megawatt shifted in ISO-NE 27
times as much as the same megawatt in MISO
(Section~\ref{subsec:objective-market-weighting}). That weighting was implicit
rather than chosen, it predicts the observed ranking of all four markets, and it
means the headline aggregate has not been tested against any other reasonable
weighting.

\textbf{Spatial flexibility is free here and would not be in reality.} There is
no bandwidth limit, no latency constraint, no data-residency rule, and no cost
of any kind for moving work between continents
(Section~\ref{subsec:problem-feasibility}). The placement half of the result is
an upper bound.

\textbf{The evaluation is validation, not test.} February was used repeatedly
while the design was being refined, so it is not a sealed hold-out. The
workload is one repeated month, and cells \(e\)--\(h\) remain untouched, so
nothing here demonstrates transfer to a different workload or a different year.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{subsec:conclusion-future}

Six extensions, ordered by what would most change the strength of the claim.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Derive deadlines from the trace.} ClusterData2019 records job
        submission and scheduling as distinct event types in
        \codepath{collection_events}, so the delay batch work actually tolerated
        in production is in principle recoverable. Replacing the invented
        windows with measured ones would remove this thesis's weakest
        assumption.
  \item \textbf{Fix the market weighting, then constrain it.} The objective
        currently weights markets as \(1/S_m^{2}\) as a side effect of
        normalizing and squaring
        (Section~\ref{subsec:objective-market-weighting}), giving ISO-NE 27
        times MISO's weight per megawatt. Two things follow. The reported
        aggregate should be recomputed under alternative weightings ---
        including \(S_m^{2}\), which cancels the effect and measures absolute
        rather than proportional harm --- to see whether the ten-seed result
        survives. And a floor requiring no market to worsen would test whether
        the aggregate gain is achievable without spending one market at all.
  \item \textbf{Establish an upper bound.} Solving each day as a mixed-integer
        program with perfect foresight would show how much of the achievable
        benefit a causal agent actually captures. Without it we know the agent
        beats status quo but not how close to optimal it is.
  \item \textbf{Seal a test calendar and evaluate on cells \(e\)--\(h\).} These
        are the two hold-outs the design has deliberately preserved, and
        together they would convert a validation result into a test result on
        both unseen grid conditions and unseen workload.
  \item \textbf{Price movement.} Attaching even a crude bandwidth or
        transfer-energy cost to inter-site movement would show how much of the
        benefit survives contact with a real network.
  \item \textbf{Run the experiment at five-minute resolution.} The hourly clock
        costs two things: within-hour shifting is invisible, and so are
        sub-hourly ramps --- which is where much of the real dispatch stress
        lives, since operators run five-minute markets precisely to manage it.
        The workload and the power trace are both natively five-minute
        (Section~\ref{subsec:transform-hourly-workload}), so the aggregation is
        ours on that side. The obstacle is the grid data: EIA-930 is hourly, so
        a finer version would have to acquire physical series directly from each
        operator, which is the acquisition path rejected here on completeness
        grounds (Section~\ref{subsec:energy-sources}). Day-ahead price would
        remain hourly and be held constant within the hour, since that is how
        the product settles. The payoff would be a model that could speak to
        five-minute ramping, and a test of whether hourly averaging has been
        hiding either opportunity or harm.
\end{enumerate}

\label{app:reproduction}

The pipeline has four stages. Stages one and two need a Google Cloud project
with BigQuery billing enabled and access to both public Google datasets;
stages three and four run from the committed files alone.

\textbf{1. Extract the workload.} Run \codepath{extract_tier_curves.ipynb} for
cells \(a\)--\(d\) and \codepath{extract_cells_eh.ipynb} for cells
\(e\)--\(h\). These write \codepath{data/cells/cell_X_tiers.csv}, the
five-minute service and batch curves of
Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-tier-split}.

\textbf{2. Fit the power model.} Run
\codepath{extract_clusterdata2019_full.ipynb} for cells \(a\)--\(d\) and
\codepath{extract_cells_eh.ipynb} for cells \(e\)--\(h\), then merge the
\(e\)--\(h\) records into \codepath{data/power_model_params.json}
(Section~\ref{subsec:powerdata-coefficients}).

\textbf{3. Build and validate the factory.} From the repository root:

\begin{verbatim}
python scripts\build_four_market_v2_factory.py
python scripts\run_four_market_v2_campaign.py preflight
python scripts\build_energy_thesis_figures.py
\end{verbatim}

The builder writes \codepath{output/four_market_v2/factory/factory.json} and one
\codepath{fixture.json} per day; \codepath{preflight} validates their structure,
capacity, calendar membership, and causal boundaries; the figure builder renders
Figures~\ref{fig:energy-normalized-net-load} and
\ref{fig:energy-single-day-duck-curve}.

\textbf{4. Train and aggregate.} Run
\codepath{scripts/run_four_market_v2_campaign.py} for the ten seeds and
\codepath{scripts/aggregate_four_market_v2_campaign.py} to produce
\codepath{output/four_market_v2/campaign/aggregation.json} together with the
aligned learning curves. The training configuration is
Table~\ref{tab:ppo-hyperparameters}; every value it does not set comes from
Stable-Baselines3 2.9.0, whose version is asserted at runtime.

Two files fix the experiment's scope and should be checked first by anyone
trying to reproduce a specific number:
\codepath{env/protocols/four_market_v2.yaml} contains every stipulated
parameter, and \codepath{data/four_market_v2/calendar.json} names the exact 114
training and 28 validation days.

\section{Episode boundaries and the overlapping-window seam}
\label{app:episode-seams}

Section~\ref{subsec:problem-episode} noted that days are simulated
independently while their clock hours overlap. This appendix records exactly
what that costs.

Training and validation days are drawn as independent windows, and consecutive
windows share hours: one window's three tail hours cover the same clock hours as
the next window's first three decision hours, and its three warm hours are the
previous window's last three decision hours. The overlapping rows carry
identical observed load, renewable output, price, and forecast vintage. Only
the workload assumption differs, since the same hour carries no arrivals when it
is a tail and full arrivals when it is a decision hour. No queue, power history,
or ramp state crosses a boundary.

Two consequences follow.

First, batch work that a policy defers into a tail is never added to the load
the next window independently schedules for those same clock hours. A
continuously operated fleet could therefore experience a boundary rise that no
single episode records.

Second, the warm power history seeding each window is built from raw arrivals
executed on arrival. The ramp at the first decision hour is therefore scored
against a status-quo left endpoint rather than against whatever the preceding
window's policy actually did.

Neither consequence affects the paired comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:campaign}. Policy and status quo share the same warm seed, the
same tail, and the same market rows, and no reported quantity accumulates across
days, so both systems are affected identically. Both consequences would matter
for any claim about continuous multi-day operation, which is why no such claim
is made.

Two remedies exist for later work, and both have costs. \emph{Chaining}
consecutive windows would carry the queue and realized power history across the
boundary, making the trajectory physically continuous; the cost is that
independent daily episodes become a few long trajectories, which destroys the
per-day resampling unit behind the reported intervals and requires a different
variance estimator. \emph{Non-overlapping windows} --- retaining every second
day, so one window closes before the next opens --- removes both seams while
leaving the episode structure and resampling unit intact; the cost is roughly
half the available windows and a full retraining campaign.

\section{Extraction refinements not taken}
\label{app:not-taken}

Two places in the data pipeline were deliberately left simpler than the sources
would support. Both are recorded here so a later reader can see they were
considered rather than missed.

\textbf{Partial usage records.} The extraction in
Section~\ref{subsec:clusterdata-utilization} drops usage records covering less
than a full five-minute window, which underrepresents short-lived work. A more
complete version would retain them, weighting each record by its actual overlap
with the bucket rather than discarding it, since
\codepath{average_usage.cpus} is a rate over the record's own span.

The size of the discarded fraction is unmeasured, and measuring it is the
cheaper of the two changes: a single pass over \codepath{instance_usage},
partitioned on whether \codepath{end_time}\,\(-\)\,\codepath{start_time} reaches
300 seconds, comparing summed CPU-seconds rather than row counts. Splitting that
comparison by priority band would additionally show whether the discarded work
is disproportionately deferrable, which is the part that would change the
interpretation of Table~\ref{tab:clusterdata-cell-summary}.

\textbf{Power-domain-level fitting.} The power model in
Section~\ref{subsec:powerdata-fitting} regresses cell-wide CPU utilization
against an unweighted mean across the cell's power domains. PowerData2019 also
publishes \codepath{machine_to_pdu_mapping}, which would allow CPU to be
aggregated only from machines attached to each measured domain, fitted at the
native five-minute cadence, weighted by mapped capacity before combining, and
extended with memory utilization, nonlinear terms, or per-domain random effects.

Any replacement power model should be selected on held-out time periods rather
than in-sample \(R^2\), and should remain interpretable once multiplied by a
megawatt rating --- the affine form's main virtue.
